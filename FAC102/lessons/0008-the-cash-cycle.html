The cash cycle

Cash leaves to buy stock, sits as stock, becomes receivables when sold, and returns when customers pay. The operating cycle is that loop. The cash conversion cycle (CCC) is the part suppliers do not finance for you.

Your win todayCompute the operating cycle and CCC from six numbers, and say how to shorten it.

1 · Warm-up from lesson 7

Under which bad-debt method does the existing provision balance not matter? Why?

% of credit sales. It estimates the year's expense directly (income-statement approach); balance-sheet methods estimate the closing balance and top up.

2 · The formulas (use a 360-day year)

MeasureFormula
Receivables turnoverSales ÷ Receivables
DSO (days sales outstanding)360 ÷ Receivables turnover
Inventory turnoverCOGS ÷ Inventory
DIO (days inventory held)360 ÷ Inventory turnover
Payables turnoverPurchases (or COGS) ÷ Payables
DPO (days payables outstanding)360 ÷ Payables turnover

Operating cycle = DIO + DSO. Cash conversion cycle = DIO + DSO − DPO.

Example: sales 720,000, receivables 60,000 → turnover 12× → DSO 30. COGS 480,000, inventory 40,000 → 12× → DIO 30. Purchases 500,000, payables 25,000 → 20× → DPO 18. Operating cycle 60 days; CCC 42 days.

DSO 45, DIO 35, DPO 25. What is the cash conversion cycle in days?

35 + 45 − 25 = 55 days. The operating cycle is 80 days.

COGS 400,000; inventory 50,000. How many days is inventory held (DIO)?

Turnover = 400,000 ÷ 50,000 = 8×. DIO = 360 ÷ 8 = 45 days. Use COGS, not sales: inventory is at cost.

3 · Trade-offs

  • Carrying vs shortage costs: too much stock costs storage and idle money; too little loses sales.
  • Purchase vs sales discounts: skipping 2/10, n/30 costs about 2/98 × 360/20 ≈ 36.7% a year, so firms usually pay early.
  • Credit period: longer credit wins sales but raises DSO and bad-debt risk.
