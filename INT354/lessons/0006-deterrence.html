Nuclear deterrence

To deter is to stop someone acting by frightening them with what you would do in return. Week 9 asks what nuclear weapons are actually for, how a nuclear threat works, and why a small group of civilian strategists became so trusted.

Your win todayState McNamara's thesis, Powell's two kinds of threat, and Fraise's "script of deterrence".

1 · Warm-up from lesson 5

What did Masco say civil defense turned "nuclear terror" into? What did it create?

A usable "nuclear fear"; a "bunker society".

2 · Basic words

  • First use: being the first side to use nuclear weapons, for example against a conventional (non-nuclear) attack. No first use: a pledge never to do so.
  • Tactical weapons are for the battlefield; strategic weapons hit the enemy's homeland.
  • NATO's Cold War plan relied on possible early first use to deter a Soviet conventional attack in Europe.

3 · McNamara (1983): the military role

US Defense Secretary 1961–68. He quotes generals (Carver, Steinhoff) and Kissinger doubting any useful battlefield use, against Reagan officials (Weinberger, Haig) who defended first use. His view: nuclear weapons "serve no military purpose whatsoever. They are totally useless, except only to deter one's opponent from using them." He told Kennedy and Johnson never to initiate their use. He asks whether stronger conventional forces could replace NATO's reliance on first use.

Trap. McNamara does not argue for battlefield use. He argues against NATO first use.

4 · Powell (1985): two kinds of threat

Threat that leaves something to chance (Schelling)Limited retaliation
How escalation happensBoth sides lose collective control; war comes by accidentEach side stays in control and chooses to escalate
Resolve meansWillingness to run grave risks of uncontrolled warWillingness to inflict and endure more destruction

His conclusion: the claim that mutually invulnerable forces make major war less likely does not follow from deterrence theory alone. It needs an extra assumption from a wider theory of international politics.

5 · Fraise (2026) and Connelly (2012)

  • Fraise: RAND strategists (Brodie, Wohlstetter, Kahn, Schelling) gave US elites a script of deterrence: a vocabulary linking nuclear actions to supposedly predictable effects, though an avoided war can never be checked. Their ritualised expertise worked like "magic", turning radical uncertainty into a sense of control (ontological security). Not fraud; authority.
  • Connelly et al.: governments invested far more in forecasting the future (intelligence estimates, scenarios, simulations) than in history. Self-negating, imaginative, abstract, conditional and deceptive forecasts regulated the superpowers: "anticipating the future … is what kept the Cold War cold".

Whose phrase is "the threat that leaves something to chance"?

Thomas Schelling, used by Powell for his first type of threat.
