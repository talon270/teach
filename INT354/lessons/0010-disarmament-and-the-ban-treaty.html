Disarmament and the Ban Treaty

The 1968 Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT) promised disarmament, but progress was slow. In 2017, 122 states voted for a treaty banning nuclear weapons outright. How did that happen, and why do the nuclear powers and the ban's supporters talk past each other?

Your win todayTrace the humanitarian turn in four steps, and define Ritchie's two "nuclear ontologies".

1 · Warm-up from lesson 9

Name the three kinds of normative effect in Tannenwald (1999).

Regulative, constitutive, permissive.

2 · Gibbons (2018): the humanitarian turn

  1. Frustration with slow disarmament through the NPT. IPPNW (Nobel Peace Prize 1985) looked outside that framework, inspired by the bans on landmines (Ottawa) and cluster munitions (Oslo).
  2. ICAN (International Campaign to Abolish Nuclear Weapons) was founded in Australia from an IPPNW affiliate, with Poola Foundation funding.
  3. Three humanitarian conferences: Oslo (March 2013), Nayarit, Mexico (February 2014, 148 states), Vienna (December 2014, 158 states). They focused on the devastating effects of nuclear use. Of the nuclear-armed states, only India and Pakistan came to Oslo and Nayarit; the five NPT nuclear-weapon states jointly declined Oslo. At Vienna the United States and the United Kingdom attended, with India and Pakistan, and China sent an unofficial representative (Gibbons, pp. 21–22).
  4. The Humanitarian Pledge (127 states) aimed at "stigmatizing, prohibiting and eliminating" nuclear weapons. On 7 July 2017, 122 states adopted the TPNW, with one against (the Netherlands) and one abstaining (Singapore) (Gibbons, pp. 11, 27).

The strategy: stress humanitarian consequences to delegitimise possession.

How many states voted to adopt the TPNW on 7 July 2017?

122 states, at the UN General Assembly conference.

3 · Ritchie (2022): contested nuclear ontologies

  • Most accounts explain the TPNW fight by the realist "security environment". Ritchie uses Robert Cox's theory of hegemony instead.
  • Disarmament politics has hardened into a contest between two broadly incommensurable nuclear worldviews ("ontologies"): hegemonic nuclearism and subaltern antinuclearism.
  • The antinuclear ontology foregrounds violence against bodies and societies; post-colonialism (nuclear imperialism, racialised discourse, North–South inequality); the environment; and gender.
  • The contest is agonistic. "Bridge-building" approaches that seek a middle ground deny this and close down debate, which is why they often fail.
Links. Ritchie's "ontologies" echoes Hecht's "Nuclear Ontologies" (lesson 4). His subaltern antinuclearism links to the dissenting scientists (lesson 2) and to Choi & Eschle (lesson 11).
