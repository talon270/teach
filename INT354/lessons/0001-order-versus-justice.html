Order versus justice

The whole course keeps asking one question: whose order, whose justice? It comes from two readings. Hedley Bull shows that the tools which keep the world stable, the nuclear balance of terror among them, often offend justice. The 1967 panel chaired by Kenneth Waltz says that order still rests on "force checking force".

Your win todayDefine Bull's order, name his three kinds of justice and three doctrines, and quote the panel's line on force.

1 · Basic words

  • Nuclear order: in mainstream IR, the rules and institutions that manage nuclear weapons among states (deterrence, non-proliferation). Week 2 widens this.
  • Balance of terror: two sides each able to destroy the other, so neither attacks.
  • Second strike: the ability to retaliate after being hit first. It makes the balance stable.

2 · Bull (1971): order vs justice

  • Order is a pattern that sustains the elementary goals of coexistence: limits on violence, promises kept, possession secured. Among states it rests on rules and institutions: international law, diplomacy, the balance of power, the great powers' managerial role. It is "minimum order", far short of peace.
  • Justice comes in three kinds: interstate (between states), human (for individuals), cosmopolitan (for the world as a whole).
  • Order is "quite inhospitable" to cosmopolitan justice, gives human justice only a "selective" welcome, and is not unfriendly to interstate justice. But its own mechanisms "systematically affront" justice.
  • The balance of terror "offends against ordinary and widely agreed notions of international justice and also human justice".
  • Three doctrines: conservative (order first), revolutionary (justice first), liberal (they can be reconciled). Order is "prior" to justice as a precondition, but does not win in every case.
  • His closing paradox: "the nuclear peace … has made the world safe for just wars of national liberation".

Which kind of justice is order "quite inhospitable" to?

Cosmopolitan justice: justice for the world as a whole.

3 · The 1967 ISA panel

Chaired by Waltz; Osgood and Tucker presented; Ramsey and Snyder discussed; Asa P. Smith wrote the report. The report never says who said what, so cite "the report".

  • It rejects "utopian" claims that force is obsolete. States' ends (Hobbes's gain, glory, security) have not changed.
  • Nuclear weapons add new uses: punishing civilians without first defeating armies; a "shadow game" of politics without war; a balance of terror resting on second-strike capability, more stable when bipolar.
  • "The only reliable basis of order is force checking force." "Absolute power may equal absolute impotence."
  • A morality of prudence (Weber, Aron) may be needed, "but is prudence attainable and would it be sufficient" for both order and justice?
