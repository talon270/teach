The atomic everyday

The atom was sold as something that would heal and feed the world. It also entered classrooms, deserts and living rooms as fear. Weeks 5 and 6 show the atom in everyday life, and who it served first.

Your win todayGive each of five readings in one line, and explain Masco's move from "terror" to "fear".

1 · Warm-up from lesson 4

Define Hecht's nuclearity. What did developing states call safeguards?

A technopolitical spectrum that shifts in time and space. "Atomic colonialism".

2 · Atoms for food and health (week 5)

ReadingOne line
Creager (2006)From 1946 the US Atomic Energy Commission turned the Oak Ridge reactor into a national radioisotope supplier, to prove it was "truly a civilian agency". Isotopes changed biology (tracers) and medicine (diagnostics), but never delivered the promised cancer cure.
Hamblin (2009)In the 1960s the IAEA outmanoeuvred the FAO, silenced FAO geneticist Ronald Silow, and sold irradiated food, sterile insects and mutation breeding to poor countries: a manufactured consensus that "modern" meant "atomic".
Jacobs (2010)The 1951 school films Duck and Cover and Atomic Alert meant to teach survival and trust in authority. Instead they taught children that adults could not protect them; the "no future" generation became the New Left.
Masco (2004)The bomb turned the biosphere into an experiment: pigs blasted in Nevada, radioactive wildlife preserves, tritium-sniffing honeybees, the Area G waste dump. The Manhattan Project produced "mutant ecologies" that will outlast the state that made them.

3 · Life atomic (week 6, Masco)

  • After the Soviet bomb (1949) and NSC 68 (1950), planners set out to turn paralysing "nuclear terror" into a usable "nuclear fear": civil defense as emotional management, not protection.
  • Every city became a target. National rehearsals of the country's own destruction (Operation Alert, Operation Cue's mannequin suburb). The family fallout shelter sold as a frontier dream space for a white, suburban family.
  • The result: a "bunker society" that militarised daily life and moved responsibility for survival from the state to citizens.
  • The images return in The Day After (1983), the 1998 asteroid films, and the Bush administration's "mushroom cloud" case for invading Iraq. Americans live "between terror and fear", in "bunkers of the imagination".

What does Masco call the society civil defense created?

A "bunker society", fixated on attack and consoled by fantasies of fortresses.
