One world or none

In 1945 many believed the bomb left two choices: international control, or ruin. This lesson covers the first try at control, the scientists who broke ranks, and a wider definition of "nuclear order" that the rest of the course uses.

Your win todayContrast the Baruch and Soviet plans, give the four dynamics of nuclear order, and explain how fallout led to Pugwash.

1 · Warm-up from lesson 1

Name Bull's three kinds of justice and his three doctrines.

Interstate, human, cosmopolitan. Conservative (order first), revolutionary (justice first), liberal (reconcilable).

2 · A wider "nuclear order" (Bensaude-Vincent, Boudia & Sato, 2022)

Fukushima became "ordinary", as every disaster since Hiroshima has been trivialised. How did such a technology become mundane? Because nuclear order is not only deterrence among states. It is "the dimensions of the nuclear domain that constitute and mediate our experience of the world". Four entangled dynamics built it:

  1. Managing violence: splitting bombs from energy.
  2. Pacifying atoms: promotion, expertise and control.
  3. Normalising risk: secrecy, denial, exceptionalism.
  4. Timescaping: reordering past and future. "No end of the nuclear age in sight."

3 · The UN Atomic Energy Commission (Roehrlich, ch. 1)

  • From H. G. Wells's The World Set Free (1914) to the scientists' slogan "One World or None" (1945): control looked like the only way to survive.
  • The UNAEC (1946–52) deadlocked. The US Baruch Plan: a supranational authority, no veto, sanctions. The Soviet plan: ban the bomb first, inspect later. The Commission died after the Soviet test of 1949.
  • Not just Cold War theatre: the talks produced the one unquestioned idea, that every state should have access to peaceful nuclear energy, plus thinking on safeguards and a dual mandate. "The IAEA would be born from such thinking."

Which US plan wanted a supranational authority with no veto?

The Baruch Plan (1946). The Soviets wanted a ban first, inspection later.

4 · Dissenting scientists (Kraft, 2018)

  • Castle Bravo (1954, a US H-bomb test) made fallout a public danger.
  • Britain, racing for its own H-bomb, managed fallout in secret: a quiet deal with the BBC, the buried Strath Report, a 1956 MRC report calling fallout a "negligible hazard".
  • Scientists outside the "nuclear nexus", above all Joseph Rotblat and Bertrand Russell, broke the silence: Rotblat's 1955 Bravo analysis, the Russell–Einstein Manifesto, the 1957 strontium-90 statement. They were vilified.
  • Blocked at home, they went transnational. Fallout was "a necessary, and perhaps a sufficient, condition" for Pugwash, first held in Nova Scotia, July 1957.
