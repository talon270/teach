Atoms for Peace and nuclearity

In 1953 Eisenhower offered the world the peaceful atom. The agency it produced, the IAEA, was shaped by Cold War rivalry, by developing countries, and by apartheid South Africa. And Gabrielle Hecht asks a deeper question: what makes something "nuclear" at all?

Your win todayDefine nuclearity, and explain why Krige calls Atoms for Peace "polyvalent".

1 · Warm-up from lesson 3

One line each: Higuchi's and Todeschini's arguments.

Higuchi: the powers treated fallout as an "emergency" only while secretly tracking it, deepening low-dose ignorance. Todeschini: biomedicine defined hibakusha; women survivors became "illegitimate sufferers".

2 · The speech and the agency

  • Eisenhower, 8 December 1953 (UN General Assembly, a primary source): the US monopoly is over, no arsenal prevents surprise attack, so the powers should give fissile material to a UN-linked agency serving "the needs rather than the fears of mankind". Its "great virtue": no world-wide inspection needed.
  • Roehrlich, ch. 2: from Operation Candor to the IAEA Statute (1953–57). The material "pool" faded; safeguards "moved from the margins to the center". The hardest fight: India against South Africa over the Board.
  • Roehrlich (2016): the IAEA's founding is a North–South story, the first major body shaped by developing countries. Safeguards were attacked as "atomic colonialism". On proliferation, Strauss: "They will in any event."
  • Krige (2006): Atoms for Peace was "polyvalent": propaganda, diversion, industrial policy, a conference. At Geneva 1955, scientific internationalism and intelligence were "two sides of the same coin".

What did developing countries call IAEA safeguards?

"Atomic colonialism" (Roehrlich 2016).

3 · Hecht: nuclearity

  • Nuclearity "is a technopolitical spectrum that shifts in time and space". Being "nuclear" is a political and technical category that is argued over, not a physical given.
  • "Negotiating Global Nuclearities" (2006): South Africa's diplomat Donald Sole kept apartheid on the IAEA Board by calling the agency purely technical. The split between technology and politics was "discursive and never enacted in practice".
  • "Nuclear Ontologies" (2006): "uranium" is as underspecified as "Africa". By 1972 ore was no longer counted as nuclear, and miners' exposure dropped out of view.
  • "The Power of Nuclear Things" (2010): stripping ore of nuclearity created "the uranium market". Niger's Diori priced exceptionalism; France answered with "the banality of the market".
Course lens. Hecht's nuclearity is the course's third question ("what makes something nuclear?"). Use it in every essay.
