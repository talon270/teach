Why states build the bomb

The standard answer: a state builds the bomb when it faces a threat it cannot meet any other way. Scott Sagan says that is only one of three answers. Etel Solingen adds a fourth angle: what the ruling coalition wants from the world economy.

Your win todayName Sagan's three models with one case each, and state Solingen's argument.

1 · Warm-up from lesson 6

McNamara's thesis in one line. Powell's two types of threat.

Nuclear weapons serve no military purpose except deterring the opponent's use. Threats that leave something to chance (loss of control) vs limited retaliation (controlled choice).

2 · Sagan (1996/97): three models in search of a bomb

ModelStates build (or give up) the bomb…Case in the reading
Securityto increase security against foreign, especially nuclear, threatsSouth Africa, in the usual reading Sagan reports: built it against a perceived Soviet-backed threat in the 1970s, dismantled it when the threat ended (de Klerk, 1993) (p. 60). Sagan then contests this reading: he revisits South Africa under domestic politics (p. 69) and Ukraine under norms (p. 80).
Domestic politicsas tools of parochial domestic and bureaucratic interests (labs, military, politicians)India's 1974 "peaceful nuclear explosion": pro-bomb scientists in the Atomic Energy Commission lobbied Indira Gandhi
Normsbecause acquisition, or restraint, is a symbol of modernity and identityDe Gaulle's France (the bomb as "a dramatic symbol of French independence"); NPT norms as a symbol of restraint

His conclusion: multicausality. Different cases are best explained by different models, so no single policy can stop all proliferation.

Which of Sagan's models treats the bomb as a symbol of modernity and identity?

The norms model.

3 · Solingen (1994): the political economy of restraint

  • Security matters, but it does not explain why similar threats produce different nuclear postures.
  • Look at ruling coalitions. Liberalizing coalitions (export firms, internationalised business, "symbolic analysts") want an open world economy, so they embrace regional nuclear regimes and denuclearization.
  • Nationalist and radical-confessional coalitions, inward-looking, resist them.
  • Example: Brazil and Argentina, like Middle East states, long kept ambiguous nuclear postures; liberalization moved the Southern Cone toward safeguarded denuclearization.
  • International institutions bankroll liberalizing coalitions but can also become their "Achilles heel" as a symbol of lost sovereignty.
