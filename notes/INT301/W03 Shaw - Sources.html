Shaw on Sources

Malcolm N. Shaw, “Sources”, chapter 3 of International Law, 8th edn (Cambridge University Press, 2017), pp. 51–95. The complete argument, every case the chapter leans on, and a revision kit.

The chapter in one breath

International law has no legislature, no executive and no court with compulsory jurisdiction, so lawyers identify its rules through “sources”. Shaw takes Article 38(1) of the ICJ Statute as the authoritative list and works through each item: custom (state practice plus opinio juris, heavily shaped by power), treaties, general principles, equity, judicial decisions and writers. He then turns to newer law-making processes (General Assembly resolutions, soft law, the International Law Commission, unilateral acts) and ends with hierarchy, where jus cogens and erga omnes obligations sit above ordinary rules. His running point is that the categories leak: every source both makes law and evidences it.

Why international law needs a theory of sources

pp. 51–53 · Introduction · Article 38(1) · classifications

Domestic law versus international law pp. 51–52

Shaw opens with a contrast. In a domestic system such as England's, finding the law is routine. You check whether an Act of Parliament (or a statutory instrument) covers the point, read the law reports to see how the courts have interpreted it, and fall back on case law where no statute applies. That method tells you what the law says and also how it was made: by parliamentary legislation or judicial case-law. The result is certainty. You can tell when a proposition has become law, the mechanism for resolving disputes is obvious, and the hierarchical character of the national order, with its gradations of authority, gives the law stability and predictability.

International law has none of this. It lacks a legislature, an executive and a structure of courts. No single body can create laws binding on everyone, and there is no system of courts with comprehensive and compulsory jurisdiction to interpret and extend the law. So the basic problem is discovering where the law is found and how to tell whether a particular proposition amounts to a legal rule. The “anarchic nature of world affairs and the clash of competing sovereignties” make this harder. Even so, Shaw insists, international law exists and can be ascertained, because there are “sources” from which rules can be extracted and analysed.

By sources Shaw means provisions operating within the legal system on a technical level. He excludes “ultimate” sources such as reason or morality, and “functional” sources such as libraries and journals. The chapter is a survey of the process by which rules of international law emerge. fn McDougal and Reisman's “The Prescribing Function” gives the New Haven account of how law is made; d'Aspremont argues sources are better understood as communitarian constraints that cannot be reduced to rules.

Article 38(1) as the authoritative list p. 52

Article 38(1) of the ICJ Statute (text in the box at the top) “is widely recognised as the most authoritative and complete statement as to the sources of international law”. Strictly it only lists what the ICJ must apply. Shaw gives two reasons it is treated as a general statement anyway:

  • the Court's function is to decide disputes “in accordance with international law”; and
  • all UN members are automatically (ipso facto) parties to the Statute under article 93 of the UN Charter. Non-members can also become parties: Switzerland was the most obvious example until it joined the UN in 2002.

So nobody seriously disputes that Article 38(1) expresses the universally accepted list of sources.

Law-creating versus law-determining p. 52

Some writers (Shaw cites Schwarzenberger) split Article 38(1) in two. International conventions, custom and general principles are the three exclusive law-creating processes. Judicial decisions and academic writings are law-determining agencies, which verify whether alleged rules exist.

Shaw's view

In reality no hard-and-fast division is possible, because the functions overlap. Treaties often just reiterate accepted customary rules, and ICJ judgments may actually create law, in the same way that municipal judges formulate new law while interpreting existing law.

Formal versus material sources p. 53

A second classification separates formal sources, said to give rules their obligatory character (the “constitutional mechanism” for identifying law), from material sources, which comprise the actual content or subject-matter of the rules. Shaw reports that the division has been criticised on two grounds: the peculiar constitutional set-up of international law, and the fact that it distracts attention from more important problems by trying to separate substantive from procedural elements, which is difficult to maintain in international law. fn Daillier, Forteau and Pellet: formal sources are the procedures for making law; material sources are the sociological, political, moral or economic foundations of norms.

Custom

pp. 53–69 · the longest section · practice + opinio juris

Where custom comes from, and what it is worth pp. 53–55

In any primitive society, rules of behaviour emerge almost subconsciously. They are maintained by social pressure and by more tangible implements, are not written down at first, and survive through what Shaw calls an “aura of historical legitimacy”. As the community develops it creates legal machinery such as courts and legislatures, but custom remains and can keep evolving. It is regarded as an authentic expression of the community's needs and values at any given time. fn Unger defines customary law as any recurring mode of interaction that groups acknowledge produces reciprocal expectations of conduct; Hart, Maine, Dias and Lloyd are also cited.

Within developed domestic legal systems custom is now relatively cumbersome, unimportant and often of only nostalgic value. In international law it is a dynamic source, because the international system lacks centralised government organs.

The problems begin because custom has to be deduced from the practice and behaviour of states. How can one tell whether a line of conduct reflects a legal rule or mere courtesy? How can one discover what a state is doing, or why, when there is no living “state”, only thousands of officials in scores of departments? There are further questions about how quickly new rules form and what effect protests have.

Writers disagree about custom's value:

  • Some deny it can still be significant, because it is too clumsy and slow-moving to keep up with the evolution of international law (Friedmann; De Lupis).
  • Others call it a dynamic process of law creation, more important than treaties because it applies universally (D'Amato).
  • A third view (De Visscher) values custom because it springs from spontaneous behaviour and so mirrors society's current concerns, but sees its role as much diminished now that international law faces a massive increase in the pace and variety of state activity and must accommodate many different cultural and political traditions.
Shaw's view

Each view has elements of truth. Amid conflicting behaviour it is hard to isolate a new customary rule, and collating the information is an immense task. Custom is not always the best instrument for complex modern problems, but in particular situations it meets the contingencies of modern life. It can produce valid “instant” rules without a long gestation, and it often dovetails neatly with the mechanisms now used to identify and progressively develop international law.

Custom also mirrors the characteristics of the decentralised international system:

  • It is democratic: all states may share in forming new rules, though “the precept that some are more equal than others in this process is not without its grain of truth” (p. 55).
  • If the international community dislikes a rule, it can be changed relatively quickly, without convening and successfully completing a world conference.
  • It reflects consensus decision-making, with the ability of the majority to create new law binding on all, while states' participation encourages their compliance.
  • Its imprecision means flexibility as well as ambiguity. The exclusive economic zone in the law of the sea was created through this kind of process (discussed in Shaw's chapter 10).

The two elements pp. 55–56

Article 38 describes custom as “evidence of a general practice accepted as law”. Shaw reads this as two elements: the material facts, meaning the actual behaviour of states, and the psychological or subjective belief that such behaviour is “law”. In the Libya/Malta case ICJ 1985 the Court said the substance of customary law must be “looked for primarily in the actual practice and opinio juris of states” (p. 55). fn Also the Nuclear Weapons advisory opinion (1996) and ILC Draft Conclusion 2 (2015).

Practice is the obvious requirement, since customary law rests on what states actually do. The psychological element needs explaining. If custom were practice alone, law could not be separated from morality or social usage, because states do not restrict their behaviour to what is legally required. They may act purely from goodwill or in the hope of reciprocal benefits. They do not have to allow tourists in or launch satellites, and no law obliges them to distribute economic aid to developing nations; the bare fact that such things are done does not mean they have to be done. The issue is how to distinguish behaviour undertaken because of a law from behaviour undertaken for other reasons, “ranging from goodwill to pique, and from ideological support to political bribery” (p. 55). Looking only at overt acts cannot solve that.

Hence the second element: opinio juris sive necessitatis, the belief by a state that it behaved as it did because it was under a legal obligation to do so. It was first formulated by the French writer François Gény to distinguish legal custom from mere social usage. fn Gény, Méthode d'Interprétation et Sources en Droit Privé Positif (1899).

Writers dispute the relative weight of the two elements:

  • Positivists, who emphasise state sovereignty, make the psychological element paramount. States are bound only by what they have consented to, so if states believe a course of action is legal and perform it, even once, their tacit consent to the rule can be inferred. Positivist thinkers therefore minimise the need for repetition and duration. fn Anzilotti, Strupp, Tunkin; Cheng on “instant” custom.
  • Others take the opposite line: opinio juris is impossible to prove and so of no great consequence. Kelsen wrote that it is the courts that have discretion to decide whether a set of usages creates a custom, and that the subjective perception of the states concerned does not give the final verdict.

The material fact pp. 56–60

The practice of states is the first factor. Shaw examines its duration, consistency, repetition and generality.

Duration

Domestic systems usually fix a timescale for accepting a practice as custom: “time immemorial” in the English common law, dating back to 1189, or thirty to forty years in Continental systems. International law has no rigid time element; it depends on the circumstances and the nature of the usage. Air and space law developed quickly, other areas much more slowly. Duration is therefore not the most important component of state practice. fn Judge Negulesco's “unfortunate phrase” that custom required immemorial usage (European Commission of the Danube, PCIJ 1927); ILC Draft Conclusion 8: if the practice is general, no particular duration is required.

Consistency and uniformity

  • Asylum ICJ 1950 laid down the basic rule: a customary rule must be “in accordance with a constant and uniform usage practised by the States in question” (p. 56). Haya de la Torre, a Peruvian sought by his government after an unsuccessful revolt, was granted asylum in Colombia's embassy in Lima, but Peru refused a safe conduct to let him leave. Colombia asked the Court to recognise that it was competent to define his offence: criminal, as Peru said, or political, in which case asylum and safe conduct could be allowed. The Court held that a customary rule had to express a right belonging to one state (Colombia) and a duty incumbent on another (Peru), and that state practice had been so uncertain and contradictory that there was no “constant and uniform usage” on the unilateral qualification of the offence. Because this was a regional custom confined to Latin America, it can be argued that a general custom might be proved to a lower standard.
  • Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries ICJ 1951: some uniformity is essential. The UK argued against Norway's method of measuring its territorial sea by invoking an alleged customary rule that a straight line may be drawn across bays of less than ten miles, from one projection of the coast to the other, and treated as the baseline for measuring the territorial sea. The Court found that actual state practice did not justify any such custom: there was insufficient uniformity of behaviour.
  • North Sea Continental Shelf ICJ 1969 (West Germany against the Netherlands and Denmark, over delimiting the continental shelf): state practice, “including that of states whose interests are specially affected”, had to be “both extensive and virtually uniform in the sense of the provision invoked”, which was indispensable to forming a new customary rule. fn The Court was considering custom arising from what had been purely a treaty rule; the Paquete Habana (1900) and Lotus are also cited.
  • Nicaragua v United States ICJ 1986: the practice need not be “in absolutely rigorous conformity” with the rule.

In order to deduce the existence of customary rules, the Court deems it sufficient that the conduct of states should, in general, be consistent with such rules, and that instances of state conduct inconsistent with a given rule should generally have been treated as breaches of that rule, not as indications of the recognition of a new rule.

Nicaragua case, quoted at p. 57

How high is the threshold?

It depends on the nature of the alleged rule and the opposition it arouses. Ambiguity matters: in Asylum the evidence was conflicting and contradictory. An unsubstantiated claim by one state cannot be accepted, because that would amount to unilateral law-making and compromise a reasonably impartial system. If a proposition meets a great deal of opposition, it would be “an undesirable fiction” to ignore this and speak of an established rule. The strength of the prior rule that is supposedly being overthrown is also relevant. fn D'Amato and Akehurst; also Judge Alvarez in Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries and Judge Loder in Lotus.

Speed, and “instant” custom

The customary law on a state's sovereignty over its airspace developed very quickly in the years just before and during the First World War. The principle that the space route followed by artificial satellites is not subject to national sovereignty emerged soon after the first sputniks. Bin Cheng argued that in such circumstances repetition is not necessary at all, provided opinio juris is clearly established, so “instant” customary law is possible. Critics reply that customary rules can hardly be proved except through a series of usages.

Shaw's view

The international context plays the vital part. A society constantly facing new situations needs a reasonably speedy way of forming rules. In new areas, custom can form quickly because the situations are new, there are no contrary rules to overcome, and there is an overwhelming need to preserve a sense of regulation in international relations.

Power: the road across vacant land

De Visscher likened the growth of custom to the gradual formation of a road across vacant land. At first the direction is uncertain; then most users follow the same line, which becomes a single path, and before long the path becomes a road accepted as the only regular way, although no one can say at what precise moment the change happened. De Visscher adds that some people leave heavier footprints than others because of their greater weight. In the same way, the more influential states “mark the way with more vigour and tend to become the guarantors and defenders of the way forward” (p. 58).

Why a state acts as it does depends on how it perceives its interests, which depends on its power, role and international standing. Custom should to some extent mirror the perceptions of the majority of states, but some states are more influential and powerful, and their activities count for more. So custom may be created by a few states, provided they are intimately connected with the issue, whether through wealth and power or through a special relationship with the subject matter (for example, maritime nations and the law of the sea). “Law cannot be divorced from politics or power” (p. 59).

  • The United Kingdom's influence on the law of the sea and prize law in the nineteenth century, at the height of its power, was predominant; navigation procedures are one of many examples.
  • The Soviet Union (now Russia) and the United States have had a paramount impact on space law.
Shaw's view

For a custom to be accepted and recognised it must have the concurrence of the major powers in that particular field. A rule on the breadth of the territorial sea is unlikely to be treated as law if the great maritime nations do not agree to or acquiesce in it, “no matter how many landlocked states demand it”. Other countries may propose ideas and apply pressure, but without the concurrence of those most interested there is no customary rule. This follows from a system where all may participate but the views of those with greater power carry greater weight. So the duration and generality of a practice may take second place to the relative importance of the states involved: “Universality is not required, but some correlation with power is.” Some continuity is still needed, depending on the context and the nature of the usage (p. 59).

fn ILC Draft Conclusion 8: the practice must be “sufficiently widespread and representative, as well as consistent”.

These external features establish that a practice exists and is exhibited, not yet that it is law (that is the job of opinio juris). They do mean that any state that takes the trouble can discover the practice. This “conspicuousness” underlines the importance of the context in which the usage operates and of the overt acts that affirm a custom.

Failures to act

How significant is it when a state, especially a major state, does not take part in a practice? Is that acquiescence, or indifference that stops the practice becoming custom until a decision is made one way or the other? Failures to act are as much evidence of a state's attitude as actions are. Shaw's example: Britain consistently fails to attack France, and Chad consistently fails to send a man to the moon. Britain does recognise a rule against attacking its neighbour; Chad does not accept a custom against launching moon rockets. So a failure to act can come either from a legal obligation not to act or from incapacity or unwillingness in the circumstances. It has been maintained (Tunkin) that a continued habit of not acting may form a legal rule, but calling long inaction a negative custom produces absurdities, such as a supposed rule, before the late 1950s, against sending satellites or rockets into space. Where a rule of behaviour is already established, however, states' abstention from protest may amount to agreement with it.

  • Lotus PCIJ 1927: the Permanent Court set a high standard. Abstention can give rise to a custom only if it is based on a conscious duty to abstain: states must be aware that they refrain because they are under a definite obligation not to act.
Shaw's view

The Lotus standard has been criticised. It covers non-acts based on legal obligation, but not situations where states, simply by not acting against an existing rule, tacitly accept its legality and relevance.

Acquiescence must be based on full knowledge of the rule invoked. A failure to act that is connected with, influenced by or accompanied by ignorance of the relevant circumstances cannot be interpreted as acquiescence.

What is state practice? pp. 60–62

Does practice cover every kind of state behaviour, or only actual, positive actions? Does it include speeches, informal documents and governmental statements, or only what states actually do?

Customary law is based on how states behave in practice, but evidence of that comes from many sources: administrative acts, legislation, decisions of domestic courts, and activity on the international stage such as treaty-making. A state is not a living entity but a collection of departments and thousands of officials: legal officers, legislatures, courts, diplomats and political leaders all act in the international field, so all this material has to be examined.

fn Jurisdictional Immunities (2012): “State practice of particular significance is to be found in the judgments of national courts”. ILC Draft Conclusion 6: practice takes a wide range of forms, physical and verbal acts and sometimes inaction, including diplomatic acts and correspondence, conduct concerning resolutions of international organisations or intergovernmental conferences, conduct concerning treaties, executive and “operational conduct on the ground”, legislative and administrative acts and national court decisions, with “no predetermined hierarchy” among them. A 2016 proposal would strike out the reference to conduct concerning resolutions.

The obvious ways to find out how states behave: read the newspapers, consult historical records, listen to what governmental authorities say, and study official publications, memoirs of past leaders, official manuals on legal questions, diplomatic exchanges and the opinions of national legal advisers. One may add General Assembly resolutions, governments' comments on International Law Commission drafts, decisions of international courts and of national courts, treaties, and the general practice of international organisations.

fn The US publishes digests of its practice (Wharton 1887, Moore 1906, Whiteman 1963–70, an annual Digest 1973–80, composite volumes for 1981–8, resumed from 2000); British sources include Smith, McNair's International Law Opinions, Parry's British Digest and E. Lauterpacht's British Practice; yearbooks such as the British Yearbook carry national practice. Brierly warned that not every contention put forward for a state represents its settled or impartial opinion.

  • International organisations may help create customary law. The ICJ's Reparation advisory opinion ICJ 1949, holding that the UN had international personality, was partly based on the UN's actual behaviour. The ILC has said that “records of the cumulative practice of international organisations may be regarded as evidence of customary international law with reference to states' relations to the organisations”, and the Court has found evidence of rules in resolutions of the General Assembly and the Security Council. fn Reservations to the Genocide Convention (1951); Construction of a Wall (2004); ILC Draft Conclusion 4.
  • National laws may found a custom. In the Scotia US Sup. Ct. 1871, a British ship sank an American vessel on the high seas. The Court held that British navigation rules set by Act of Parliament formed the basis of the international custom, because other states had legislated in virtually identical terms, so the American vessel, which was not showing the correct lights, was at fault.

Are claims practice?

One view (D'Amato) holds that mere claims, as opposed to physical acts, cannot be state practice, on the basis that “until it [a state] takes enforcement action, the claim has little value as a prediction of what the state will actually do” (pp. 61–62). Shaw calls this “decidedly a minority view”. fn Judge Read, dissenting in Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries, is cited alongside D'Amato.

Shaw's view

Claims and conventions of states have been accepted as evidence of practice, and “this approach is clearly the correct one”: claims and counter-claims are a recognised way for states to tell each other how they see the status of rules, so they operate in the same way as physical acts: whether made in abstracto or about a particular situation, they are the raw material out of which rules of international law may be fashioned. The formulation that “state practice covers any act or statements by a state from which views about customary law may be inferred” (Akehurst) is “substantially correct”. But not all practice weighs the same: its value depends on its nature and provenance (p. 62).

fn Akehurst's formula also covers omissions and silence. Claims are treated as practice in Asylum, Rights of US Nationals in Morocco (1952), North Sea and Fisheries Jurisdiction (1974).

Opinio juris pp. 62–66

Once a usage exists, the question is how the state regards its own behaviour: as a moral, political or legal act? Opinio juris, the belief that the activity is legally obligatory, is what turns a usage into a custom and makes it part of international law. In the Special Rapporteur's Draft Conclusion 9: “the practice in question must be undertaken with a sense of legal right or obligation” (p. 62). fn A 2016 amendment proposed “accompanied by” instead of “undertaken with”.

The Lotus case

Lotus PCIJ 1927. A collision on the high seas between the French ship Lotus and the Turkish ship Boz-Kourt drowned several people aboard the Turkish vessel. Turkey alleged negligence by the French officer of the watch, and when the Lotus reached Istanbul he was arrested on a manslaughter charge. The question was whether Turkey had jurisdiction to try him. France argued that customary law gave the flag state of the accused (France) exclusive jurisdiction in such cases, so the victim's state (Turkey) was barred. To prove it, France pointed to the absence of previous criminal prosecutions by such states in similar situations and deduced tacit consent, which had therefore become a legal custom.

The Court rejected this. Even if such abstention could be proved, it would not amount to a custom:

only if such abstention were based on their [the states] being conscious of a duty to abstain would it be possible to speak of an international custom.

Lotus case, quoted at p. 63

The essential ingredient of obligation was lacking, and “the practice remained a practice, nothing more”.

The North Sea Continental Shelf cases

North Sea Continental Shelf ICJ 1969. The North Sea continental shelf was being divided into national zones for oil and gas exploration. West Germany could not agree boundaries with either the Netherlands or Denmark. Article 6 of the 1958 Geneva Convention on the Continental Shelf provided that, failing agreement and unless special circumstances justified another approach, the boundary would follow the principle of equidistance from the nearest points of the baselines. Because Germany's coastline is concave, equidistance lines drawn from where it met the Netherlands and Denmark would converge and enclose only a relatively small triangle of the North Sea. The Federal Republic had signed but not ratified the Convention, so it was not bound by the treaty. The question was whether the “equidistance-special circumstances principle” had become customary law and so bound Germany anyway.

The Court said no, on two counts:

  • No existing custom was codified. When the ILC considered the point in the draft that became the Geneva text, equidistance had been proposed “with considerable hesitation”, somewhat experimentally, and not at all as an emerging customary rule.
  • Later practice had not created one. Time was not itself decisive (only three years had passed before the proceedings began), but an indispensable requirement was that, within that period, however short, state practice, including that of specially affected states, “should have been both extensive and virtually uniform in the sense of the provision invoked, and should moreover have occurred in such a way as to show a general recognition that a rule of law or legal obligation is involved” (p. 63).

Nicaragua and Jurisdictional Immunities

The Court kept this approach in Nicaragua ICJ 1986, expressly citing North Sea:

for a new customary rule to be formed, not only must the acts concerned ‘amount to a settled practice’, but they must be accompanied by the opinio juris sive necessitatis. Either the States taking such action or other States in a position to react to it, must have behaved so that their conduct is ‘evidence of a belief that this practice is rendered obligatory by the existence of a rule of law requiring it’. The need for such a belief, i.e. the existence of a subjective element, is implicit in the very notion of the opinio juris sive necessitatis.

Nicaragua case, quoted at p. 64

In Germany v Italy (Greece intervening) ICJ 2012 (the Jurisdictional Immunities case) the Court located opinio juris in three things: states claiming immunity asserting that international law gives them a right to it; states granting immunity acknowledging that international law obliges them to; and, conversely, states asserting a right to exercise jurisdiction over foreign states. Where a state grants more immunity than international law requires, that grant is not accompanied by the requisite opinio juris and sheds no light on the question.

Shaw's view

The Court has adopted and kept a high threshold for proving the subjective element. Convergent practice on its own is not enough. In the Diallo case ICJ 2007 (Guinea v Democratic Republic of the Congo, preliminary objections), the Court held that the existence of many investment-protection agreements, and the common inclusion of such provisions in contracts between states and foreign investors, “was insufficient to show that there has been a change in the customary rules of diplomatic protection: it could equally show the contrary” (p. 64).

fn Talmon on the ICJ's method of identifying custom: induction, deduction and assertion.

The paradox: how can custom ever change?

The great problem with opinio juris: if it requires states to act in the belief that their conduct is already lawful, how can new customary rules be created, since that requires conduct different from, or contrary to, what was law until then? If a country claims a three-mile territorial sea believing it legal, how can the rule change to allow claims of, say, twelve miles, since a twelve-mile claim cannot also be in accordance with existing law? A restrictive view of the psychological element would stultify the law, and that has demonstrably not happened. fn Akehurst surveys attempts to deny or minimise the need for opinio juris.

Shaw's view

Treat custom-formation as a process. States behave in a certain way in the belief that their behaviour “is law or is becoming law”, and it then depends on how other states react whether this “process of legislation” is accepted or rejected (p. 64). Rigid definitions of legality have to be modified to see whether state activity can be given the “legitimating stamp”. If a state proclaims a twelve-mile limit, believing that although three miles has been the accepted law, circumstances have changed so much that twelve miles might now be treated as becoming law, it is vindicated if other states follow suit and a new rule forms. If others reject the claim, the projected rule withers away and the original rule stands, reinforced by practice and common acceptance.

[r]eliance by a State on a novel right or an unprecedented exception to the principle might, if shared in principle by other States, tend towards a modification of customary international law.

Nicaragua case, quoted at p. 65

The difficulty is that it is sometimes hard to pinpoint exactly when one rule supersedes another, but that complication is inherent in custom: “Change is rarely smooth but rather spasmodic.”

This means taking a more flexible view of opinio juris and tying it more firmly to the overt manifestations of custom, in the context of national and international behaviour. That accommodates an action which, though contrary to law, “contains the germ of a new law”, and the difficulty of proving that a state acting in a certain way believes it is acting lawfully. The extreme version infers opinio juris from the material acts themselves. Judge Tanaka, dissenting in North Sea, said there was

no other way than to ascertain the existence of opinio juris from the fact of the external existence of a certain custom and its necessity felt in the international community, rather than to seek evidence as to the subjective motives for each example of State practice.

Judge Tanaka, quoted at p. 65

fn H. Lauterpacht: treat all uniform conduct of governments as evidencing opinio juris, except where the conduct was not accompanied by such intention; Cheng disagreed.

Shaw's view

Even so, other states must be made aware that when a state takes a course of action, it does so because it regards the action as within international law, not as a purely political or moral gesture. There has to be an aspect of legality about the behaviour, which the acting state confirms, so that the international community can easily distinguish legal from non-legal practices. This is essential to developing and presenting a legal framework among states.

fn D'Amato: a practice must be preceded or accompanied by the “articulation” of a rule, putting states on notice of its legal implications. Akehurst: practice must be accompanied by statements that something is already law before it can become law, though these need not be sincere beliefs; only duty-imposing rules need such statements, permissive rules do not. ILC Draft Conclusion 10 lists forms of evidence of acceptance as law: public statements on behalf of states, official publications, government legal opinions, diplomatic correspondence, national court decisions, treaty provisions, and conduct concerning resolutions of international organisations or intergovernmental conferences.

Evidence from international organisations

Faced with the practical difficulty of proving opinio juris, courts increasingly look at conduct within international organisations, particularly the UN. The ICJ has in several cases used General Assembly resolutions to confirm the existence of opinio juris, focusing on their content and the conditions of their adoption. The key is the attitude of the states concerned, whether as parties to a treaty or as participants in adopting a resolution. The Court has also relied on major codification conventions and on the work of the International Law Commission. fn Resolutions: Nuclear Weapons (1996), Western Sahara (1975), East Timor (1995), Nicaragua, Wall (2004). Codification conventions: the 1958 Continental Shelf Convention in North Sea; the 1969 Vienna Convention in Cameroon v Nigeria (2002). ILC work: Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros (1997).

Protest, acquiescence and change in customary law pp. 66–68

Customary law is established through a pattern of claim, absence of protest by the states particularly interested, and acquiescence by other states. Together with related notions such as recognition, admissions and estoppel, this conduct (or abstention from conduct) forms a complex framework within which legal principles are created and applied to states. fn Shaw's “good example” is El Salvador/Honduras (1992): joint sovereignty over the historic waters of the Gulf of Fonseca beyond the three coastal states' territorial seas.

  • Gulf of Maine ICJ Chamber 1984 defined acquiescence as “equivalent to tacit recognition manifested by unilateral conduct which the other party may interpret as consent”, founded on the principles of good faith and equity (p. 66). fn Burkina Faso/Mali: “the idea of acquiescence ... presupposes freedom of will”.

Generally, where states are seen to acquiesce in other states' behaviour without protesting, the assumption must be that the behaviour is accepted as legitimate. Some writers maintain that acquiescence can amount to consent to a customary rule and that absence of protest implies agreement: where states take action they declare to be legal, other states' silence can express opinio juris or concurrence in the new rule. On that view actual protests are needed to break the legitimising process. The Special Rapporteur put it this way: “Failure to react over time to a practice may serve as evidence of acceptance as law (opinio juris), provided that States were in a position to react and the circumstances called for some reaction” (p. 67).

Shaw distinguishes two situations:

  • Under Lotus, one cannot infer a rule prohibiting an action merely because states do not engage in it.
  • Not reacting when another state behaves in a certain way is different. Where a new rule is created in a new field, such as space law, other states' acquiescence reinforces it, whether it comes from real agreement or lack of interest, depending on the circumstances. Acquiescence in a new rule that deviates from an established custom is more problematic.

The persistent objector

Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries ICJ 1951 might suggest that where a state acts contrary to an established customary rule and other states acquiesce, that state is not bound by the original rule. The Court said: “in any event the ... rule would appear to be inapplicable as against Norway inasmuch as she had always opposed any attempt to apply it to the Norwegian coast” (p. 67). In other words, a state that opposes a custom from its inception is not bound by it. This is the persistent objector rule.

fn ILC Draft Conclusion 15: where a state objected to a rule while it was being formed, the rule is not opposable to that state for as long as it maintains the objection; the objection must be clearly expressed, made known to other states, and maintained persistently. Critics include Dumberry (“Incoherent and Ineffective”).

But the problem of states seeking to dissent from an already recognised custom, through adverse behaviour combined with other states' acquiescence or non-reaction, “remains unsettled” (p. 67).

Why silence is not always consent

States fail to protest for very many reasons. A state might not wish to give offence gratuitously, might want to reinforce political ties, or might have other diplomatic and political considerations in mind. Protesting over every single act a state disagrees with would be an excessive requirement, and it is unrealistic to expect every state to react to every act of every other state. If a failure to protest validated a derogation from established custom in every case, “scores of special relationships” would emerge between states depending on patterns of acquiescence and protest. And a protest may be purely formal, or part of diplomatic manoeuvring designed to exert pressure in a totally different field, and so not intended to alter legal relations at all.

How customs change

  • Where a new rule contradicting a prior rule is maintained by a large number of states, the protests of a few will not overrule it, and other states' abstention from reacting merely reinforces it.
  • Constant protest by one state, reinforced by the acquiescence of others, might create a recognised exception to the rule, but that depends greatly on the facts and the views of the international community.
  • Behaviour contrary to a custom “contains within itself the seeds of a new rule”. If other nations endorse it, the previous law disappears and is replaced. Alternatively, two customs may co-exist for a period until one of them is generally accepted, as happened for many years with the limits of the territorial sea (p. 68).

It follows that customary rules are binding on all states except those that dissented from the start. fn North Sea; the Third US Restatement; Stein's “The Approach of the Different Drummer”; Charney.

New states

This raises the position of new states. The traditional logic is that they are bound by all customs existing at the date of independence. The opposite view, based on the consent theory of law (Tunkin), would let them choose which customs to adhere to at that point, whatever other states think. Because that could be highly disruptive, the usual proviso is that by entering into relations with other states without reservation, new states signify their acceptance of the totality of international law.

Regional and local custom pp. 68–69

Rules can develop that bind only a set group of states, such as those in Latin America, or even just two states. This may be seen as part of the need for “respect for regional legal traditions”. fn Local custom is also called regional or special custom. Honduras claimed a “trilateral local custom of the nature of a convention” establishing a condominium (El Salvador/Honduras); Eritrea/Yemen is cited for regional legal traditions; Gros Espiell on Latin American doctrine.

  • Asylum ICJ 1950: Colombia claimed a regional or local custom peculiar to Latin American states that would validate its grant of asylum. The Court held that “the party which relies on a custom of this kind must prove that this custom is established in such a manner that it has become binding on the other party” (p. 68), and found it unproved because the evidence was uncertain and contradictory. In such cases the standard of proof, especially regarding the obligation accepted by the party against which the local custom is invoked, is higher than for an ordinary or general custom.
  • Right of Passage over Indian Territory ICJ 1960: Portugal claimed a right of passage over Indian territory between its enclaves. The Court upheld it over India's objection that no local custom could be established between only two states. It was satisfied that there had been a constant and uniform practice allowing free passage and that the “practice was accepted as law by the parties and has given rise to a right and a correlative obligation”.

Where therefore the Court finds a practice clearly established between two States which was accepted by the Parties as governing the relations between them, the Court must attribute decisive effect to that practice for the purpose of determining their specific rights and obligations. Such a particular practice must prevail over any general rules.

Right of Passage case, quoted at p. 69
Shaw's view

Local customs depend on one state's activity being accepted by the other state or states as an expression of a legal obligation or right. For a general custom the process of consensus is at work, so a majority, or even a substantial minority, of interested states can suffice to create it. A local custom needs the positive acceptance of both (or all) parties. That is because local customs are an exception to the fairly flexible, all-state character of customary law-making and a reminder of the older theory of consent, under which states are bound only by what they assent to. “Exceptions may prove the rule, but they need greater proof than the rule to establish themselves.” (p. 69)

Treaties

pp. 69–72 · the deliberate method · treaty and custom together

What treaties are pp. 69–70

Compared with custom, treaties (international conventions) are “a more modern and more deliberate method” of creating law (p. 69). Article 38 refers to “international conventions, whether general or particular, establishing rules expressly recognised by the contesting states” (Shaw's p. 69 renders this “contracting states”). Shaw deals with treaties in detail in his chapter 15; here he covers their role as a source. fn Oppenheim: “not only is custom the original source of international law, but treaties are a source the validity and modalities of which themselves derive from custom”.

Treaties go by many names: Conventions, International Agreements, Pacts, General Acts, Charters, Statutes, Declarations and Covenants. All describe a similar transaction: written agreements by which the participating states bind themselves legally to act in a particular way or to set up particular relations between themselves, laying out conditions and arrangements the parties oblige themselves to carry out. fn Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties 1969, art. 2(1)(a): a treaty is “an international agreement concluded between states in written form and governed by international law, whether embodied in a single instrument or in two or more related instruments and whatever its particular designation”. Non-binding agreements are discussed at p. 88.

The obligatory nature of treaties rests on the customary principle that agreements are binding, pacta sunt servanda. Treaties may be divided into law-making treaties, intended to have universal or general relevance, and treaty-contracts, which apply only between two or a small number of states. The distinction reflects how widely a treaty applies and the range of obligations it imposes, but it is not hard and fast; there are many grey areas.

Treaties are express agreements and “a form of substitute legislation undertaken by states” (p. 70). They superficially resemble contracts, since the parties create binding obligations for themselves, but they have a nature of their own that reflects the character of the international system. Their number has grown over the last century, as the expanding United Nations Treaty Series and United Kingdom Treaty Series show. As governmental controls increase and the technological and communications revolutions affect international life, the number of issues needing inter-state regulation multiplies.

For many writers, treaties are the most important source because they require the express consent of the parties; they are therefore seen as superior to custom, which is regarded as a form of tacit agreement. fn Tunkin; Soviet legal thinking. Important examples include the UN Charter, the Geneva Conventions on prisoners and on the protection of civilians, and the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations. Agreements range from regulating outer space exploration to controlling drugs and creating international financial and development institutions. It would be impossible to telephone abroad, post a letter overseas or fly to another country without the international agreements that lay down the necessary conditions.

How a treaty can bind beyond its parties pp. 70–71

A treaty, like a contract, binds its parties. How then can conventions be sources of international law beyond the obligations of the contracting parties? The answer is the law-making treaty. Such treaties are meant to have a general, not restrictive, effect; states use them to set out their view of international law on a topic, or to establish new rules to guide their future conduct. They necessarily require the participation of a large number of states, and they may produce rules that bind all. fn This depends on other states' attitude and is not a form of international legislation (Oppenheim; Reparation 1949; Namibia 1971; Baxter; Schachter; Dinstein).

  • Normative treaties prescribe rules of conduct: for example the Antarctic Treaty and the Genocide Convention.
  • Codifying treaties declare the existing law or codify customary rules: for example the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations of 1961.

The general rule is that states that do not sign and ratify a treaty are not bound by its terms. In North Sea West Germany had not ratified the relevant Convention and was under no obligation to heed it. But where a treaty reflects customary law, non-parties are bound, not because it is a treaty provision but because it reaffirms customary rules. Non-parties may also come to accept that a treaty's provisions generate customary law, depending on the nature of the agreement, the number of participants and other relevant factors.

Treaty provisions that become custom p. 71

In North Sea Continental Shelf ICJ 1969 the Court accepted that a treaty provision, combined with opinio juris, can create a binding custom governing all states, not just the parties, and treated this as one of the recognised methods of forming new customary law. But the provision had to be “of a fundamentally norm-creating character” (p. 71), that is, capable of forming the basis of a general rule of law. What this means will vary with time and place, but it confirms that treaty provisions can lead to custom provided other states, parties and non-parties alike, show compatible behaviour and opinio juris. fn The Court stressed that this result is not lightly to be regarded as attained; see also the minority opinions, Gulf of Maine and Libya/Malta.

D'Amato went further: generalisable treaty provisions could by themselves, without any need to show opinio juris and with little passage of time, generate customary rules ipso facto.

Shaw's view

This recognises the importance of treaties with potentially norm-creating provisions, particularly in human rights, but it “is clearly going too far”. The danger is that a small number of states could legislate for all, unless dissenting states actually entered into contrary treaties. That would be too radical a departure from the current process of law-formation in the international community (p. 71).

Treaty and custom co-exist pp. 71–72

It is now established that when a treaty rule covers the same ground as a customary rule, the custom is not simply absorbed; it keeps a separate existence. In Nicaragua ICJ 1986 the United States argued that the customary norms on self-defence had been “subsumed” and “supervened” by article 51 of the UN Charter. The Court rejected this:

even if a treaty norm and a customary norm relevant to the present dispute were to have exactly the same content, this would not be a reason for the Court to hold that the incorporation of the customary norm into treaty law must deprive the customary norm of its applicability as distinct from the treaty norm.

Nicaragua case, quoted at pp. 71–72

It concluded that “customary international law continues to exist and to apply separately from international treaty law, even where the two categories of law have an identical content” (p. 72). The practical effect: because of an American reservation, the Court could not analyse the treaty-based obligation, but it could examine the rule as established in customary law.

Shaw's view

Two rules with the same content may be subject to different principles of interpretation and application, so the Court's approach is theoretically correct and of practical value too. Often this dual existence suggests that the two versions are not in fact identical, as with self-defence in customary law and under article 51, but it always depends on the circumstances.

Regimes, constitutions and treaty-contracts p. 72

  • Regimes. Some treaties try to establish a “regime” that necessarily extends to non-parties. In creating a definitive framework for international peace and security, the UN Charter provides in article 2(6) that “the organisation shall ensure that states which are not members of the United Nations act in accordance with these Principles so far as may be necessary for the maintenance of international peace and security”. The 1947 General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade set up a common code of conduct in international trade that also affected non-party states; it has since been transmuted into the World Trade Organization.
  • Constitutive treaties create international institutions and act as their constitutions, setting out their powers and duties.
  • Treaty-contracts are not law-making instruments in themselves, since they involve few states and a limited topic, but they may provide evidence of customary rules. A series of bilateral treaties containing a similar rule may show that the rule exists in customary law. Shaw urges caution, because bilateral treaties often reflect discrete circumstances. fn Extradition treaties and bilateral investment treaties.

General principles of law

pp. 72–78 · gap-filling · municipal analogies

Why the category exists pp. 72–73

In any legal system a court may find that no law, neither statute nor precedent, covers the exact point before it. The judge then deduces a rule by analogy from existing rules, or directly from the general principles that guide the system, whether described as justice, equity or considerations of public policy. This is even more likely in international law, because the system is relatively underdeveloped compared with the needs it faces: there are fewer decided cases than in municipal systems and no method of legislating for new situations.

That is why “the general principles of law recognised by civilised nations” were inserted into Article 38 as a source of law: to close gaps that might be uncovered and to solve the problem known as non liquet (a court being unable to decide because no rule applies). The question of gaps is important. While there may not always be an immediate and obvious rule for every international situation, “every international situation is capable of being determined as a matter of law” (Oppenheim, p. 73).

fn The ICJ has treated “principles” and “rules” as essentially the same (Gulf of Maine), though adding “general” broadens the meaning. The “civilised nations” clause is regarded today as redundant (Pellet). But in Nuclear Weapons (1996) the Court said it could not conclude whether threat or use of nuclear weapons in self-defence, where a state's very survival was at stake, was lawful or unlawful (compare Judge Higgins's dissent).

Three views of what the category means p. 73

  • Natural law view. The principles affirm natural law concepts that underlie the system and supply the test for the validity of positive (man-made) rules. fn H. Lauterpacht, Waldock, Jenks, and Judge Tanaka dissenting in South-West Africa (1966).
  • Positivist view. General principles are a sub-heading of treaty and customary law and can add nothing new unless they reflect state consent. Soviet writers such as Tunkin treated them as restating fundamental precepts already set out in treaty and custom, such as the law of peaceful co-existence.
  • Middle view (most writers). General principles are a separate source of law, but of fairly limited scope, as the decisions of the PCIJ and ICJ reflect.

It is not always clear whether a principle comes from municipal systems, is a general principle of international law, or is, as it has been put, one of those principles of municipal law that apply to relations between states (Oppenheim).

The reservoir is the legal operations of 190 or so states, but judges need not be experts in every system, because there are common themes. Anglo-American common law has influenced many states, as have the French and Germanic systems; there are many common elements in Latin American law; and most Afro-Asian states borrowed heavily from European experience in modernising the structures that administer the state and westernising economic and other enterprises. fn David and Brierley, Major Legal Systems. In AMCO v Indonesia the tribunal held that French concepts of administrative unilateral acts and administrative contracts were not common enough to count.

Leading cases pp. 74–75

  • Chorzów Factory PCIJ 1928: after Poland seized a nitrate factory in Upper Silesia, the Court declared that “it is a general conception of law that every violation of an engagement involves an obligation to make reparation”, and that it is “a principle of international law that the reparation of a wrong may consist in an indemnity corresponding to the damage which the nationals of the injured state have suffered as a result of the act which is contrary to international law” (p. 74). fn Also the Chile–US Commission on the deaths of Letelier and Moffitt.

The most fertile fields for municipal-law analogies, however, have been procedure, evidence and the machinery of the judicial process.

  • German Settlers in Poland PCIJ 1923: approaching the matter negatively, the Court declared that “private rights acquired under existing law do not cease on a change of sovereignty ... It can hardly be maintained that, although the law survived, private rights acquired under it perished. Such a contention is based on no principle and would be contrary to an almost universal opinion and practice.” (p. 74) fn By contrast, South-West Africa (1966) held that the actio popularis (a right of any member of a community to sue in the public interest) is not part of international law and cannot be imported through general principles.
  • Corfu Channel ICJ 1949: on circumstantial evidence, “this indirect evidence is admitted in all systems of law and its use is recognised by international decisions” (p. 74).

Res judicata pp. 74–76

International courts have also referred to res judicata: the decision in the circumstances is final, binding and without appeal. fn Corfu Channel (compensation, 1949).

  • Effect of Awards of the UN Administrative Tribunal ICJ 1954: members of the UN Secretariat staff had been dismissed and the Tribunal had made awards to them; could the General Assembly refuse to give effect to the awards? No: “according to a well-established and generally recognised principle of law, a judgment rendered by such a judicial body is res judicata and has binding force between the parties to the dispute” (p. 75). fn Laguna del Desierto (Argentina/Chile) called res judicata “a universal and absolute principle of international law”.
  • Genocide Convention (Bosnia and Herzegovina v Serbia and Montenegro) ICJ 2007: the issue was the meaning of the Court's 1996 judgment rejecting preliminary objections to jurisdiction. The principle “signifies that the decisions of the Court are not only binding on the parties, but are final, in the sense that they cannot be reopened by the parties as regards the issues that have been determined, save by procedures, of an exceptional nature, specially laid down for that purpose” (p. 75). Two purposes underpin it, internationally as well as nationally: a general one, the stability of legal relations, which requires litigation to come to an end; and a specific one, that it is in each party's interest that an issue already decided in its favour is not argued again. Depriving a litigant of the benefit of a judgment it has obtained must in general be seen as a breach of the principles governing the legal settlement of disputes. The principle applies equally to judgments on preliminary objections and on the merits. Since jurisdiction had been established by the 1996 judgment, a party could not now argue that the Court had lacked power to give it because one of the parties could now be seen to have been unable to come before the Court; that would call into question the res judicata force of the judgment's operative clause. fn Also Nicaragua v Colombia (2011, 2016).
  • Right of Passage ICJ 1957 (preliminary objections): “it is a rule of law generally accepted, as well as one acted upon in the past by the Court, that, once the Court has been validly seized of a dispute, unilateral action by the respondent state in terminating its Declaration [accepting the Court's jurisdiction], in whole or in part, cannot divest the Court of jurisdiction” (pp. 75–76).

Estoppel p. 76

The principle of estoppel provides that a party that has acquiesced in a particular situation cannot then challenge it.

  • Applied in the Temple case ICJ 1962.
  • Held inapplicable in the Serbian Loans case PCIJ 1929, where French bondholders demanded payment on a series of Serbian loans in gold francs rather than paper money.
  • ELSI ICJ 1989 set limits: “although it cannot be excluded that an estoppel could in certain circumstances arise from a silence when something ought to have been said, there are obvious difficulties in constructing an estoppel from a mere failure to mention a matter at a particular point in somewhat desultory diplomatic exchanges” (p. 76).
  • Cameroon v Nigeria ICJ 1998 confirmed the meaning: “An estoppel would only arise if by its acts or declarations Cameroon had consistently made it fully clear that it had agreed to settle the boundary dispute submitted to the Court by bilateral avenues alone. It would further be necessary that, by relying on such an attitude, Nigeria had changed position to its own detriment or had suffered some prejudice.” (p. 76)
  • Mauritius v United Kingdom Arbitral tribunal 2015: “evidence of opportunities foregone in reliance upon a representation constitutes one of the clearest forms of detrimental reliance, although a benefit conveyed on the representing State will also suffice” (p. 76).

fn Also Eastern Greenland (PCIJ 1933), the Eritrea/Ethiopia Boundary Commission (2002) and Saiga (No. 2).

Other general principles pp. 76–78

  • Full compensation. In AMCO v Indonesia Arbitration the tribunal held that “the full compensation of prejudice, by awarding to the injured party the damnum emergens and lucrum cessans” (actual loss and lost profit) “is a principle common to the main systems of municipal law, and therefore, a general principle of law which may be considered as a source of international law” (p. 76).
  • Respect for acquired rights. fn Certain German Interests in Polish Upper Silesia (PCIJ 1926), Starrett Housing v Iran, the Shufeldt claim, AMCO.
  • Burden of proof. A party advancing a point of fact in support of its claim must establish that fact. fn Malaysia/Singapore (2008).
  • Proportionality (argued to be a general principle): in areas ranging from self-defence and the use of force in hostilities to maritime delimitation, counter-measures in WTO and investment law, and human rights, the application of a rule may be constrained by relevant factors. fn Article 5(4) of the Treaty on European Union: EU action “shall not exceed what is necessary to achieve the objectives of the Treaties”.
  • Pacta sunt servanda, a crucial general principle: international agreements are binding. The law of treaties “rests inexorably upon this principle”, since binding agreements presuppose that such instruments are commonly accepted as binding. fn VCLT art. 26.
  • Good faith, “perhaps the most important general principle, underpinning many international legal rules” (p. 77). It is enshrined in UN Charter article 2(2): “all Members, in order to ensure to all of them the rights and benefits resulting from membership, shall fulfil in good faith the obligations assumed by them in accordance with the present Charter”. The 1970 Declaration on Principles of International Law concerning Friendly Relations and Co-operation among States (GA resolution 2625 (XXV)) extended this to obligations under international law generally, including treaties. Good faith is therefore an indispensable part of international law. fn Oppenheim calls it “of overriding importance”.

One of the basic principles governing the creation and performance of legal obligations, whatever their source, is the principle of good faith. Trust and confidence are inherent in international co-operation, in particular in an age when this co-operation in many fields is becoming increasingly essential. Just as the very rule of pacta sunt servanda in the law of treaties is based on good faith, so also is the binding character of an international obligation assumed by unilateral obligation.

Nuclear Tests cases (ICJ 1974), quoted at p. 77

But the Court has also said that good faith is “not in itself a source of obligation where none would otherwise exist” (Border and Transborder Armed Actions ICJ 1988, Nicaragua v Honduras), and that it “relates only to the fulfilment of existing obligations” (Cameroon v Nigeria ICJ 1998). So good faith is a background principle that informs and shapes the observance of existing rules and constrains the manner in which those rules may legitimately be exercised. fn Fisheries Jurisdiction, North Sea, Lac Lanoux, Nuclear Weapons; Rio Declaration principles 19 and 27; Judge Ajibola in Libya/Chad.

  • Ex injuria jus non oritur: facts flowing from wrongful conduct cannot determine the law. fn Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros (1997); the Brčko arbitration.

The Court's discretion: Barcelona Traction p. 78

It follows that the Court has discretion over which principles to apply in a given case, and it resorts to them because customary and treaty law cannot provide the required solution. In Barcelona Traction ICJ 1970 (Belgium v Spain) the Court relied heavily on the municipal law concept of the limited liability company. Deciding the case in disregard of the relevant municipal institutions would, without justification, invite serious legal difficulties and lose touch with reality, because international law has no corresponding institutions to fall back on. But international law did not refer to the municipal law of a particular state; it referred to the rules generally accepted by municipal legal systems, which recognise the limited company.

fn Also the Abu Dhabi arbitration and Texaco v Libya. The Abyei award (2009) discussed review of judicial decisions as a general principle. Another general principle: whether an act is compatible with international law is judged by the law in force when it occurred (Germany v Italy 2012; ILC Articles on State Responsibility, art. 13).

Equity and international law

pp. 78–81 · a subsection of General principles in Shaw · value, method or source?

Beyond borrowing procedures and institutions from municipal systems, a number of cases refer to equity “as a set of principles constituting the values of the system” (p. 79).

fn Equity can mean adapting the law to particular areas or choosing between interpretations (infra legem), filling gaps in the law (praeter legem), or a reason for not applying unjust laws (contra legem) (Akehurst; Judge Weeramantry in Jan Mayen, 1993). Equity is used extensively in state succession (Shaw's chapter 16).

  • Diversion of Water from the Meuse PCIJ 1937, a dispute between the Netherlands and Belgium, contains the most famous statement. Judge Hudson said principles of equity have long been treated as part of international law and applied by courts: “Under article 38 of the Statute, if not independently of that article, the Court has some freedom to consider principles of equity as part of the international law which it must apply.” (p. 79) Shaw says this must be interpreted very cautiously, though at the broadest level equity, by analogy with domestic law, can be seen as “a creative charge in legal development”, producing dynamic change in a system made inflexible by the strict application of rules.

fn Tunisia/Libya: “equity as a legal concept is a direct emanation of the idea of justice”. Abi-Saab criticised the ICJ's “flight into equity”. Compare Judges Weeramantry and Schwebel in Jan Mayen.

  • Rann of Kutch Arbitration 1968 between India and Pakistan: the tribunal agreed that equity formed part of international law, so the parties could rely on it in presenting their cases. fn It used equity to fix the boundary in two deep inlets.
  • North Sea Continental Shelf ICJ 1969: the Court directed a final delimitation between West Germany, the Netherlands and Denmark “in accordance with equitable principles”. fn Equity was used to exclude the equidistance method in the particular circumstances. It also discussed the relevance of equity in Barcelona Traction.
  • South-West Africa (Second Phase) ICJ 1966: Judge Tanaka, dissenting, argued for a wider interpretation and treated the broad concept as a source of human rights ideas.
Shaw's view

What is really in question is the use of equitable principles in the context of a rule requiring such an approach. Courts are not applying abstract justice to cases; they derive equitable principles and solutions from the applicable law.

fn Under article 38(2) the ICJ may decide a case ex aequo et bono (on the basis of what is fair and good) if the parties agree, but it has never done so. Libya/Malta reaffirmed “the principle that there can be no question of distributive justice”.

the justice of which equity is an emanation, is not an abstract justice but justice according to the rule of law; which is to say that its application should display consistency and a degree of predictability; even though it also looks beyond it to principles of more general application.

Libya/Malta case (ICJ 1985), quoted at p. 80

Courts have used equity to mitigate certain inequities, not to refashion nature to the detriment of legal rules. Its existence as a separate and distinct source of law is therefore “at best highly controversial” (p. 80). fn North Sea; Anglo-French Continental Shelf (1978); Tunisia/Libya; Gulf of Maine.

it is bound to apply equitable principles as part of international law, and to balance up the various considerations which it regards as relevant in order to produce an equitable result. While it is clear that no rigid rules exist as to the exact weight to be attached to each element in the case, this is very far from being an exercise of discretion or conciliation; nor is it an operation of distributive justice.

Equity in treaties p. 80

  • The 1982 Law of the Sea Convention makes marked use of equitable principles. Article 59: conflicts between coastal and other states over the exclusive economic zone are resolved “on the basis of equity”. Article 74: delimitation of the zone between states with opposite or adjacent coasts is by agreement on the basis of international law “in order to achieve an equitable solution”. Article 83 applies the same to the continental shelf. These provisions are flexible, which matters, but also somewhat uncertain: how a dispute will be resolved, and on what principles, is far from clear, and an element of unpredictability may have been introduced. fn Art. 140: equitable sharing of benefits from the deep sea-bed.   • The 1997 Convention on the Law of the Non-Navigational Uses of International Watercourses (based on ILC draft articles) also stresses equity. Equity and land boundaries pp. 80–81

Equity may also be used to delimit non-maritime boundaries where there is no evidence of where a line lies.

  • Burkina Faso/Mali ICJ Chamber 1986: on the pool of Soum, the Court said it “must recognise that Soum is a frontier pool; and that in the absence of any precise indication in the texts of the position of the frontier line, the line should divide the pool of Soum in an equitable manner”. That meant dividing it equally. Equity does not always mean equality, but where there are no special circumstances equality is generally the best expression of it. The Court also emphasised that “to resort to the concept of equity in order to modify an established frontier would be quite unjustified” (p. 81). fn El Salvador/Honduras; in the Brčko arbitration the tribunal was expressly authorised by the Dayton Accords (Annex 2, art. V) to apply “relevant legal and equitable principles”.
  • Diallo ICJ 2012 (compensation): “Quantification of compensation for non-material injury necessarily rests on equitable considerations” (p. 81).

Values and “elementary principles of humanity” p. 81

Shaw's view

Generalised principles or concepts that may be called community value-judgements inform and pervade the political, and therefore the legal, orders in the broadest sense, but they are not in themselves binding legal norms. That can happen only if the international community has accepted them as legal norms through the mechanisms and techniques of international law creation. Nevertheless, “elementary principles of humanity” may lie at the base of such norms, help justify them, and give them additional force within the system.

In the Legality of the Threat or Use of Nuclear Weapons advisory opinion ICJ 1996, the Court said that at the heart of the rules and principles of international humanitarian law lies the “overriding consideration of humanity”. fn Also Corfu Channel (1949).

Judicial decisions

pp. 81–83 · subsidiary means that sometimes make law

In Article 38's words, judicial decisions are a “subsidiary means for the determination of rules of law” rather than an actual source, but they can be of immense importance. Under article 59 of the ICJ Statute, the Court's decisions bind only the parties and only in respect of the case decided. The common-law doctrine of precedent, under which the rulings of certain courts must be followed by others, does not exist in international law. Yet the Court strives to follow its previous judgments and insert a measure of certainty, and states in disputes and textbook writers quote PCIJ and ICJ judgments as authoritative.

The ICJ closely examines its previous decisions and carefully distinguishes cases it thinks should not apply. And just as English judges create law while interpreting it, ICJ judges sometimes do “a little more than merely ‘determine’ it” (p. 82):

  • Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries ICJ 1951, “one of the most outstanding instances”: its criteria for recognising the baselines from which the territorial sea is measured were later enshrined in the 1958 Geneva Convention on the Territorial Sea and Contiguous Zone.
  • Reparation ICJ 1949: recognised the legal personality of international institutions in certain cases.
  • Reservations to the Genocide Convention ICJ 1951: dealt with reservations to treaties.
  • Nottebohm ICJ 1955: considered the role and characteristics of nationality.
  • The range of cases on maritime delimitation.

A decision of the Court is not always accepted in later formulations of the law. Part of the Lotus decision was criticised and later abandoned in the Geneva Conventions on the Law of the Sea. But that is comparatively unusual: the Court examines its own case-law with considerable attention and departs from it rarely. At the very least its earlier decisions are the starting point of analysis. In Cameroon v Nigeria ICJ 1998 the Court said “the real question is whether, in this case, there is cause not to follow the reasoning and conclusion of earlier cases” (p. 82). fn Qatar v Bahrain (2001), Liechtenstein v Germany (2005), Wall (2004); Shahabuddeen, Precedent in the World Court.

Arbitral awards pp. 82–83

“Judicial decisions” also covers international arbitral awards and the rulings of national courts. There have been many arbitral tribunals, such as the Permanent Court of Arbitration created by the Hague Conferences of 1899 and 1907, and various mixed claims tribunals including the Iran–US Claims Tribunal. Although they differ from international courts in some ways, many of their decisions have been extremely significant, as the Reports of International Arbitral Awards, published by the UN since 1948, show.

  • Alabama Claims Arbitration 1872 opened a new era in the peaceful settlement of disputes, with increasing use of judicial and arbitral methods. The Alabama was built on Merseyside to the Confederate States' specifications and captured some seventy Federal ships during the American Civil War. After the war the United States sought compensation for the depredations of the Alabama and other ships, and the tribunal agreed: Britain had infringed the rules of neutrality and had to pay damages.
  • Island of Palmas Arbitration 1928 has been of immense significance for territorial sovereignty (Shaw's chapter 9). fn Also the Beagle Channel award (1977), the Anglo-French Continental Shelf case (1978), and the Abyei award (2009) between the Government of Sudan and its then southern province.

The case-law of the International Criminal Tribunal for the Former Yugoslavia and the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda is of growing significance. As a result, international courts of various kinds often cite each other, sometimes in support and sometimes to disagree. fn Support: Saiga (No. 2) (ITLOS 1999) citing Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros. Disagreement: the ICTY in Tadić disapproved of the ICJ's Nicaragua test for state responsibility over paramilitary units; the ICJ reaffirmed its approach in Bosnia v Serbia (2007).

National courts p. 83

  • Decisions of municipal courts may provide evidence of a customary rule.
  • They may also be evidence of the actual practice of states: not a description of the law as it has been held to apply, but examples of how states actually behave, “the essence of the material act” needed to establish custom. British and American writers in particular refer extensively to national court decisions. fn Thirty Hogsheads of Sugar, Bentzon v Boyle (1815), the Paquete Habana (1900), the Scotia (1871), Lotus; Congo v Belgium (2002).
  • Decisions of the highest courts of federal states such as Switzerland and the United States, resolving conflicts between their component units, are relevant to fields such as boundary disputes. A boundary dispute between two US states settled by the Supreme Court is in many ways analogous to the ICJ deciding a frontier dispute between two independent states. fn Vermont v New Hampshire (1933), Iowa v Illinois (1893).

Writers

pp. 83–84 · “the most highly qualified publicists”

Article 38 includes, as a subsidiary means for determining rules of law, “the teachings of the most highly qualified publicists of the various nations”.

  • The natural law era. Historically, academic writers' influence was marked. In the heyday of natural law, analyses and juristic opinions were crucial, while state practice and court decisions counted for less. Gentili, Grotius, Pufendorf, Bynkershoek and Vattel were the supreme authorities of the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries and determined the scope, form and content of international law.
  • The positivist era. With the rise of positivism and its emphasis on state sovereignty, treaties and custom took the dominant position and the importance of legal writings declined. Textbooks are now used to discover what the law is on a point, not as the fount of actual rules. Some writers still had a formative impact on particular areas, such as Gidel on the law of the sea, and some general works are referred to almost as classics, such as Oppenheim and Rousseau, but the general influence of textbook writers has declined.
  • What books still do. They arrange and focus the structure and form of international law, and explain the nature, history and practice of its rules. Academic writing also stimulates thought about the values and aims of international law, points out defects in the system and makes suggestions for the future.
  • A special responsibility. Because the international legal order lacks supreme authorities and institutions, the responsibility on publicists is all the greater: they must inject coherence and order into the subject and question the direction and purposes of the rules.
  • Who uses them. States presenting claims, national law officers advising their governments, international judicial and arbitral bodies, and municipal judges when needed all consult and quote leading juristic authorities.
Shaw's view

The claim that textbook writers merely reflect and reinforce national prejudices is often made, but “it is an allegation which has been exaggerated” (p. 84). It should not lead us to dismiss the value of writers, but to assess each writer correctly within his particular environment. fn Huber in the Spanish Zone of Morocco case; Carty.

Other possible sources of international law

pp. 84–91 · GA resolutions · soft law · ILC · other bodies · unilateral acts

Law-making, law-determining, law-evidencing pp. 84–85

Shaw distinguishes actual sources of rules, meaning devices capable of instituting new rules (law-making treaties, customary law, and many ICJ decisions, since they cannot be confined to merely determining or elucidating the law), from practices and devices that evidence rules (juristic writings, many treaty-contracts, and some international and municipal judicial decisions).

Shaw's view

In fact each source is capable, to some extent, of both developing new law and identifying existing law. This results partly from the disorganised state of international law and partly from the terms of Article 38 itself (p. 85). The same confusion between law-making, law-determining and law-evidencing runs through the other methods of developing law that have emerged since the Second World War.

General Assembly resolutions pp. 85–87

The foremost of these issues, and one that reflects the growing importance of developing (“Third World”) states and “the gradual de-Europeanisation of the world order”, is the legal standing of General Assembly resolutions and declarations (p. 85).

  • The classic position. The Security Council can adopt resolutions binding on all UN members (Shaw cites Charter articles 25 and 94). Assembly resolutions are generally not legally binding but merely recommendatory, expressing opinions with varying degrees of majority support. This reflects the plan that the Assembly would be a parliamentary advisory body and the Security Council would take binding decisions. fn Some Assembly resolutions of an administrative nature are binding, e.g. under art. 17 (the budget). Security Council binding force: Namibia (1971), Lockerbie (1992).
  • Today it is more complex. The Assembly has produced many highly important resolutions and declarations, which inevitably affect the direction of international law. The way states vote, and the explanations they give, are evidence of state practice and of their understanding of the law. Where a country has consistently voted for, say, the abolition of apartheid, it could not afterwards deny the existence of a usage condemning racial discrimination, and for that state the usage may even become a binding custom.

In Nicaragua ICJ 1986 the Court tentatively suggested that opinio juris could be derived from the circumstances in which a resolution is adopted and applied:

opinio juris may, though with all due caution, be deduced from, inter alia, the attitude of the Parties [the US and Nicaragua] and the attitude of States towards certain General Assembly resolutions, and particularly resolution 2625 (XXV) entitled ‘Declaration on Principles of International Law concerning Friendly Relations and Co-operation among States in accordance with the Charter of the United Nations’.

Nicaragua case, quoted at p. 86

Consent to resolutions like this “may be understood as acceptance of the validity of the rule or set of rules declared by the resolution by themselves”. Shaw notes that this comment may refer only to resolutions that define or elucidate an existing treaty (Charter) commitment.

  • Consistent majorities. Where the vast majority of states consistently vote for resolutions and declarations on a topic, that amounts to state practice, and a binding rule may well emerge provided the requisite opinio juris can be proved.
  • Self-determination. The 1960 Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples was adopted with no opposition and only nine abstentions, after a series of resolutions attacking colonialism and calling for self-determination of the remaining colonies. It seems to have marked “the transmutation of the concept of self-determination from a political and moral principle to a legal right and consequent obligation”, particularly read together with the 1970 Declaration on Principles of International Law (p. 86).
  • Outer space. The 1963 Declaration of Legal Principles Governing the Activities of States in the Exploration and Use of Outer Space is another example of state practice leading, or having led, to a binding customary rule.
  • Evidence of opinio juris, and interpretation. Besides being state practice, resolutions may evidence the existence of, or evolution towards, the opinio juris without which custom cannot arise. They can also be authoritative interpretations by the Assembly of the Charter's principles, depending on the circumstances. fn ILC Draft Conclusion 12 (2015): a resolution cannot of itself create customary law, but may provide evidence of a rule's existence and content or contribute to its development; 2016 proposals would say “does not” instead of “cannot” and drop “or contribute to its development”.
  • Speed. Resolutions can therefore speed up the legalisation of state practice and allow customary law to adapt faster to modern conditions. The presence of representatives of virtually all states in the Assembly greatly enhances its value politically and as a generator of state practice that may or may not lead to binding custom.

In Nicaragua the Court said “the wording of certain General Assembly declarations adopted by states demonstrates their recognition of the principle of the prohibition of force as definitely a matter of customary international law” (p. 87). And in the Nuclear Weapons advisory opinion ICJ 1996:

The Court notes that General Assembly resolutions, even if they are not binding, may sometimes have normative value. They can, in certain circumstances, provide evidence important for establishing the existence of a rule or the emergence of an opinio juris. To establish whether this is true of a General Assembly resolution, it is necessary to look at its content and the conditions of its adoption; it is also necessary to see whether an opinio juris exists as to its normative character. Or a series of resolutions may show the gradual evolution of the opinio juris required for the establishment of a new rule.

Nuclear Weapons advisory opinion, quoted at p. 87

Applying this, the Court examined the Assembly's resolutions on the legality of nuclear weapons. Several had been adopted with substantial numbers of negative votes and abstentions, and their focus had not always been constant, so they fell short of establishing an opinio juris on the illegality of nuclear weapons.

Shaw's view

One must be alive to “the dangers in ascribing legal value to everything that emanates from the Assembly”. Resolutions are often the result of political compromises and arrangements and, understood in that sense, were never intended to be binding norms. Great care must be taken in moving “from a plethora of practice to the identification of legal norms” (p. 87). Resolutions of other international organisations can be approached the same way, “tempered with a little more caution”: they may evidence an existing custom or constitute usage leading to one, and opinio juris may emerge from the surrounding circumstances.

Soft law pp. 87–88

It is sometimes argued that particular non-binding instruments or documents, or non-binding provisions in treaties, form a special category called soft law. The term indicates that the instrument is not itself “law”, but is so important within the general framework of international legal development that particular attention should be paid to it.

Shaw's view

“‘Soft law’ is not law. That needs to be emphasised” (p. 87). But a document need not be a binding treaty before it can influence international politics.

  • The Helsinki Final Act of 1975 is the prime example. It was not a binding agreement, but its influence in Central and Eastern Europe in emphasising the role and importance of international human rights “proved incalculable” (p. 88). fn The ICJ referred to it in Nicaragua.
  • Some areas generate more soft law than others, particularly international economic law and international environmental law.
  • Such documents, whether called recommendations, guidelines, codes of practice or standards, signal the evolution and establishment of guidelines that may ultimately become binding rules, either by formalisation into a binding treaty or by acceptance as a customary rule, provided the necessary conditions are met.
  • Soft law propositions are important and influential but are not in themselves legal norms. It can suit states to make agreements, directly or through international organisations, that are not intended to be binding or subject to formal legal implementation but reflect a political intention to act in a certain way. Such agreements can be more flexible, easier to conclude and easier to comply with for domestic reasons.

A US State Department study of non-binding international agreements noted that governments have long agreed joint statements of policy or intention that create no legal obligations. These have become a common way to announce the results of diplomatic exchanges, state common positions, record intended courses of action, or make political commitments, and are known as “non-binding agreements, gentlemen's agreements, joint statements or declarations”. What decides their status is not the title of the document but the intention of the parties, inferred from all the relevant circumstances, as to whether they meant to create binding legal relations.

The International Law Commission pp. 89–90

  • Creation and composition. Established by the General Assembly in 1947 to promote “the progressive development of international law and its codification”. It has thirty-four members from Africa, Asia, America and Europe, who serve five years and are appointed from lists submitted by national governments. It consults outside bodies including the Asian-African Legal Consultative Committee, the European Commission on Legal Cooperation and the Inter-American Council of Jurists. fn Judge ad hoc Sørensen, dissenting in North Sea, on the relationship between codification and progressive development; arts 2, 3 and 8 of the ILC Statute.
  • From draft to treaty. Many of the most important conventions grew out of its work. Having chosen a topic, the ILC prepares a draft, sends it to states for comment, and usually a UN conference follows, until eventually a treaty emerges. Examples: the Law of the Sea (1958), Diplomatic Relations (1961), Consular Relations (1963), Special Missions (1969) and the Law of Treaties (1969). It is not always smooth: a new Law of the Sea Convention needed many conferences, at Caracas in 1974 and in Geneva and New York from 1975 to 1982.
  • Other output. Reports and studies; the Draft Declaration on Rights and Duties of States (1949); the Principles of International Law recognised in the Charter of the Nuremberg Tribunal and in its Judgment (1950); draft articles on jurisdictional immunities (1991); a draft statute for an international criminal court (1994); and draft articles on state responsibility (2001).
  • Influence on the Court. ICJ judgments often refer to ILC drafts. In a 1997 speech to the General Assembly, President Schwebel said the Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros judgment ICJ 1997 was notable for the importance it gave the ILC's work: it drew on treaties concluded from the ILC's proceedings (the law of treaties, state succession in respect of treaties, international watercourses) and gave great weight to some of the ILC's Draft Articles on State Responsibility, as both Hungary and Slovakia did. “Just as the judgments and opinions of the Court have influenced the work of the International Law Commission, so the work of the Commission may influence that of the Court.” (p. 90)
Shaw's view

The ILC is involved in at least two of the major sources. Its drafts may become treaties binding the states that ratify them, which may also continue to form part of general international law. And its work is part of the range of state practice that can lead to new customary rules: its drafts may constitute evidence of custom, add to the usages that create new law, and evidence opinio juris. Finally, since it is composed of eminently qualified publicists, including many governmental legal advisers, its reports and studies can be used, like books, to determine what the law actually is.

Other bodies p. 90

The ILC is by far the most important body, but others share the mission. The UN Commission on International Trade Law (UNCITRAL) and the UN Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD) are expanding international law on economic, financial and development activities. Temporary organs such as the Committee on the Principles of International Law have produced declarations and statements. Specialised agencies like the International Labour Organization and UNESCO constantly develop international law in their spheres. Independent bodies are also active: the International Law Association and the Institut de Droit International are the best known, and the Harvard Research drafts produced before the Second World War are still valuable.

Unilateral acts pp. 90–91

In certain situations the unilateral acts of states, including statements by relevant state officials, may give rise to international legal obligations. Examples include recognition and protests intended to have legal consequences. Unilateral acts are not sources of international law as understood in Article 38(1), but they “may constitute sources of obligation” (p. 91).

  • Intention and publicity. The declaring state's intention to be bound is crucial, as is the element of publicity or notoriety. Intention is found by interpreting the act, and the principle of good faith plays a crucial role. fn Nuclear Tests (1974) and the 1995 request (New Zealand v France); Nova Scotia/Newfoundland; Eritrea/Ethiopia; commitments can arise in oral pleadings before the Court (Cameroon v Nigeria, 2002). The ILC's 2006 Guiding Principles: a declaration binds only if made by an authority with power to do so (Principle 4); it may be addressed to the international community as a whole, to one or several states or to other entities (Principle 6); it must be stated in clear and specific terms (Principle 7).
  • Restrictive interpretation. Where states make statements that limit their freedom of action, a restrictive interpretation is required. fn Nuclear Tests, Nicaragua, Burkina Faso/Mali; North Sea: a non-party's unilateral assumption of a convention's obligations is “not lightly to be presumed”; Malaysia/Singapore: a denial not made in response to a claim, or in the context of a dispute, is not a binding undertaking.
  • Recognition by third states matters for the act or statement to be opposable to them.
  • Evidence. Unilateral statements may also be used as evidence of the particular view a state takes. fn Jan Mayen: a Norwegian Foreign Ministry press release and a communication to Parliament; Judge Ajibola in Libya/Chad.

Hierarchy of sources and jus cogens

pp. 91–95 · lex posterior · lex specialis · erga omnes · peremptory norms

Ordering the sources pp. 91–92

The hierarchy of sources is more complex than it first appears. There is a presumption against normative conflict, but international law is not as clear as domestic law in listing the order of constitutional authority. The position is complicated by the proliferation of international courts and tribunals existing side by side without hierarchy, and by the significant substantive and procedural expansion of international law. fn The ILC Study Group report on fragmentation (finalised by Koskenniemi, 2006). Pellet: there is no formal hierarchy between conventions, custom and general principles, but the ICJ uses them in successive order and “has organized a kind of complementarity between them”. Dupuy: there is no hierarchy of sources. The ILC fragmentation study agrees that “treaties generally enjoy priority over custom and particular treaties over general treaties”.

  • Judicial decisions and writings clearly have a subordinate function, since Article 38(1) describes them as subsidiary means.
  • General principles, which complement custom and treaty, sit “fairly firmly in third place” (p. 92).
  • Custom versus treaty is more complex. As a general rule the later in time prevails: lex posterior derogat priori. Treaties are usually drafted to replace or codify existing custom, and treaties may themselves fall out of use and be replaced by new customary rules. Where the same rule appears in both a treaty and a custom, there is no presumption that the treaty subsumes the custom; the two may co-exist (Nicaragua).
  • The special rule prevails over the general: lex specialis derogat legi generali. So treaty rules between particular states, as lex specialis, take priority over general rules of treaty or customary law between the same states, but not if the general rule is one of jus cogens. fn Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros, Beagle Channel, Right of Passage, Nuclear Weapons, Tunisia/Libya, Nicaragua; the OSPAR arbitration (Ireland v UK).

Erga omnes and jus cogens: overlapping but different pp. 92–93

The position is further complicated by norms or obligations of a different or higher status, whether derived from custom or treaty: obligations erga omnes and rules of jus cogens (peremptory norms). Their content may overlap significantly, but they differ in nature:

  • Erga omnes concerns the scope of application of a rule: how far states generally are subject to it and can be seen as having a legal interest in it. Its focus is primarily procedural.
  • Jus cogens rules are substantive rules recognised as having a higher status as such.

fn ILC Articles on State Responsibility, art. 48; Furundžija (ICTY).

  • Barcelona Traction ICJ 1970: there is an essential distinction between a state's obligations towards the international community as a whole and those arising towards another state in the field of diplomatic protection. By their very nature the former concern all states: “all states can be held to have a legal interest in their protection; they are obligations erga omnes” (p. 93). Examples given: the outlawing of aggression and genocide, and protection from slavery and racial discrimination. To these one may add the prohibition of torture. fn Belgium v Senegal (2012); also Nicaragua and Judge Weeramantry in East Timor.
  • East Timor ICJ 1995: the right of peoples to self-determination “has an erga omnes character”.
  • Genocide Convention (Bosnia v Serbia) ICJ 1996: “the rights and obligations enshrined in the Convention are rights and obligations erga omnes”.

This easing, in certain circumstances, of the traditional rules on standing (locus standi) to pursue a legal remedy against an alleged offending state may be linked to the separate question of superior principles in international law.

Jus cogens in the Vienna Convention p. 93

  • Article 53 VCLT: a treaty is void “if, at the time of its conclusion, it conflicts with a peremptory norm of general international law”.
  • Article 64 VCLT: if a new peremptory norm of general international law emerges, any existing treaty in conflict with it “becomes void and terminates”.
  • The rule also applies to customary rules, so no derogation from such norms is permitted by local or special custom.

a norm accepted and recognised by the international community of states as a whole as a norm from which no derogation is permitted and which can be modified only by a subsequent norm of general international law having the same character.

VCLT art. 53 definition of a peremptory norm, quoted at p. 93

fn US v Matta-Ballesteros (9th Cir. 1995): jus cogens norms “enjoy the highest status within customary international law, are binding on all nations, and cannot be preempted by treaty”.

What jus cogens is. The concept rests on an acceptance of fundamental and superior values within the system. It is in some respects akin to public order or public policy in domestic legal orders, and it reflects the influence of natural law thinking. Rules of jus cogens are not new rules as such; they are existing rules recognised as having a particular and superior quality. fn The ILC began a study of jus cogens in 2015 and appointed a Special Rapporteur. Shaw also points to art. 26 of the ILC Articles on State Responsibility (2001), Not in Shaw which stops circumstances precluding wrongfulness (such as necessity) from excusing a breach of a peremptory norm.

Examples. Various examples were offered, particularly in the ILC's discussions: an unlawful use of force, genocide, slave trading and piracy. The International Court has said that in its opinion the prohibition of torture has become a peremptory norm. fn Belgium v Senegal (2012), though Judge Abraham called this obiter dictum. Torture has also been treated as jus cogens in Furundžija (ICTY), Siderman v Argentina, Ex parte Pinochet (No. 3) (Lords Hope, Hutton and Phillips) and Al-Adsani (ECtHR 2001); extrajudicial killing in Alejandre v Cuba; non-discrimination by the Inter-American Court (Undocumented Migrants advisory opinion, 2003). But there is no clear agreement about other areas, and even the examples given are by no means uncontroverted. fn Lord Slynn in Ex parte Pinochet (No. 1): no jus cogens rule requires state or head-of-state immunity to be overridden for international crimes. Still, the rise of individual responsibility directly for international crimes marks a further step in the development of jus cogens.

How a peremptory norm is created p. 94

Identifying the mechanism is particularly important, because once a rule of jus cogens exists no derogation is permitted. Article 53 implies a two-stage approach:

  1. the proposition is established as a rule of general international law; and
  2. that rule is accepted as a peremptory norm by the international community of states as a whole.
Shaw's view

The process is stringent, and rightly so, because establishing a higher level of binding rules has serious implications for the international community. The situation to avoid is “foisting peremptory norms upon a political or ideological minority”, which in the long run would devalue the concept. The appropriate test requires universal acceptance of the proposition as a legal rule by states, and recognition of it as a rule of jus cogens by an overwhelming majority of states, crossing ideological and political divides (p. 94). And only rules based on custom or treaties may form the foundation of jus cogens norms, especially given many states' hostility to general principles as an independent source and the universality requirement of jus cogens formation.

Consequences pp. 94–95

  • Existing peremptory norm (art. 53): a treaty contrary to it is void ab initio (from the start).
  • Newly emerging peremptory norm (art. 64): an existing treaty in conflict with it terminates from the date the rule emerges. It is not void ab initio, and under article 71 no right, obligation or legal situation created by the treaty before termination is affected, provided its maintenance is not itself contrary to the new norm.
  • State responsibility: article 41(2) of the ILC's Articles on State Responsibility (2001) provides that no state shall recognise as lawful a “serious breach” of a peremptory norm. fn A serious breach involves a gross or systematic failure to fulfil the obligation (art. 40(2)); see also art. 50(d).
  • Reservations that offend a rule of jus cogens may well be unlawful. fn Judges Padilla Nervo, Tanaka and Sørensen in North Sea; UN Human Rights Committee General Comment 24.
  • Immunity: it has been suggested that state conduct violating jus cogens may not attract a claim of state immunity. fn Cassese, citing Judge Wald's dissent in Princz v Federal Republic of Germany (US Court of Appeals, 1994); contrast Al-Adsani (ECtHR 2001).
  • Article 103 of the UN Charter gives Charter obligations precedence over obligations under other international agreements. In his separate opinion in the Bosnia case ICJ 1993, Judge Lauterpacht said that “the relief which article 103 of the Charter may give the Security Council in case of conflict between one of its decisions and an operative treaty obligation cannot – as a matter of simple hierarchy of norms – extend to a conflict between a Security Council resolution and jus cogens” (p. 95). Not in Shaw This was Judge ad hoc Elihu Lauterpacht, son of Hersch Lauterpacht, the jurist in the tutorial “people in portrait” list. fn The House of Lords in Al-Jedda (2007) gave article 103 obligations (Security Council resolutions) priority over article 5 of the European Convention on Human Rights.
  • Jurisdiction: establishing a rule of jus cogens does not as such give the ICJ (or any other court) jurisdiction. A rule's status, erga omnes or not, is a separate question from consent to the exercise of jurisdiction. fn DRC v Rwanda (2006); Germany v Italy (2012).

The chapter in ten lines

  • International law has no legislature, executive or compulsory court, so its rules are found through sources; Article 38(1) of the ICJ Statute is the accepted list (all UN members are parties to the Statute via Charter art. 93).
  • Treaties, custom and general principles are usually called law-creating; judicial decisions and writers are “subsidiary means”. Shaw: the functions overlap, and the formal/material distinction distracts.
  • Custom = state practice + opinio juris sive necessitatis (Gény). Practice must be constant and uniform (Asylum), extensive and virtually uniform including specially affected states (North Sea), but only generally consistent (Nicaragua). Duration is secondary; “instant” custom is possible in new fields (Cheng).
  • Power shapes custom: the major powers in a field must concur (de Visscher's heavier footprints). “Universality is not required, but some correlation with power is.”
  • Practice includes claims and statements: “any act or statements by a state from which views about customary law may be inferred” (Akehurst; Shaw agrees). Opinio juris is a process (“is law or is becoming law”), with a high evidential threshold (Lotus, North Sea, Nicaragua, Diallo).
  • Custom changes by claim, response and acquiescence. Persistent objectors are not bound (Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries); later dissent is unsettled. Local custom needs every party's positive acceptance (Asylum, Right of Passage).
  • Treaties bind parties (pacta sunt servanda); law-making treaties can codify or generate custom if “fundamentally norm-creating” (North Sea); treaty and custom co-exist even with identical content (Nicaragua, art. 51). D'Amato's ipso facto custom “goes too far”.
  • General principles fill gaps and prevent non liquet: reparation (Chorzów), res judicata, estoppel, good faith, pacta sunt servanda. Equity works inside rules, not as abstract or distributive justice; as a separate source it is “at best highly controversial”.
  • Judicial decisions bind only the parties (art. 59) but the ICJ follows itself and sometimes makes law. Writers mattered most in the natural law era. GA resolutions and soft law are not binding but can evidence practice and opinio juris; the ILC codifies and develops; unilateral declarations can bind (Nuclear Tests).
  • Hierarchy: decisions and writers subordinate, general principles third; lex posterior and lex specialis between treaty and custom; erga omnes (who can invoke) and jus cogens (no derogation; VCLT arts 53 and 64) sit above. Jus cogens needs near-universal acceptance and never by itself confers jurisdiction.

Shaw's own verdicts

  • Article 38(1) is the universally accepted list, but its categories overlap.
  • Each view on custom's value has truth; context decides; instant custom is possible in new fields.
  • “Law cannot be divorced from politics or power”: custom needs major-power concurrence.
  • The Lotus conscious-duty test ignores tacit acceptance of existing rules.
  • Claims are state practice: “clearly the correct” approach.
  • Opinio juris is a process, read flexibly with practice, but the acting state must signal legality.
  • Requiring protest against every act is unrealistic; silence is not always consent.
  • New states accept all existing law by entering relations without reservation.
  • Local custom needs more proof than general custom.
  • D'Amato's treaty-to-custom shortcut goes “too far”.
  • Treaty and custom co-existence is theoretically correct and practical.
  • General principles: a real but limited source. Equity: not a separate source of any certainty.
  • Values bind only once accepted through law-making mechanisms.
  • ICJ judges sometimes make law; writers still matter; the national-bias charge is exaggerated.
  • Every source both makes and evidences law.
  • GA resolutions can speed custom, but not everything the Assembly says is law. Soft law is not law.
  • Jus cogens needs universal acceptance of the rule and recognition by an overwhelming majority across divides; only custom or treaty can found it.

Traps

  • Article 38(1)(d) items are subsidiary means, not sources in the strict sense.
  • Article 59 rules out binding precedent, not consistency: the ICJ still follows itself.
  • Erga omnes is about scope and standing (procedural); jus cogens is about hierarchy and non-derogation (substantive).
  • Art. 53 (existing norm): void ab initio. Art. 64 (new norm): terminates from emergence; art. 71 protects earlier situations.
  • A persistent objector must object while the rule is forming; objecting afterwards is the unsettled “subsequent objector” problem.
  • Lotus is about abstention as practice; it does not mean silence never counts (acquiescence reinforces new rules).
  • Good faith is not an independent source of obligations.
  • Equity applied within the law is not a decision ex aequo et bono.
  • GA resolutions are generally recommendatory; Security Council resolutions can bind; some administrative GA resolutions (budget, art. 17) bind.
  • Codifying a custom in a treaty does not extinguish the custom (Nicaragua).
  • Non-parties are not bound by a treaty, but may be bound by the customary rule it reflects.
  • Jus cogens status does not confer jurisdiction on any court.
  • Judge Lauterpacht in Bosnia (1993) was Elihu, not Hersch Not in Shaw.

For INT301: the course lens

What is international law, and who makes it?
For Shaw, law is identified through a technical system of sources. States make it, above all through practice and consent (custom and treaties); courts, the ILC, international organisations and writers evidence it and sometimes make it. Law-making is a process, not a single act.
How does power shape it?
Openly, and Shaw accepts it as a fact of the system: custom requires the concurrence of the major powers in a field (de Visscher's heavier footprints; UK sea and prize law; US and Soviet space law; “some are more equal than others”). He notes the rise of Third World influence through GA resolutions and the “de-Europeanisation” of the world order, and calls the “civilised nations” clause redundant (fn). He describes power's role without treating it as a problem to be fixed.
Which approach?
The mainstream doctrinal textbook. Positivist-leaning (consent and state practice as the base), pragmatic about processes and soft law, and open about natural-law traces in general principles, equity and jus cogens.

Retrieval practice

Self-test

Answer out loud or on paper before revealing. If you miss one, reread that section of the summary.

Q1 · IntroWhy is identifying the law harder internationally than domestically?

No legislature, executive or structure of courts; no body can make law binding on everyone and no court has comprehensive, compulsory jurisdiction. Add the anarchic nature of world affairs and competing sovereignties. Domestic law has a hierarchy that gives certainty.

Q2 · Article 38Why is Article 38(1) treated as a general list of sources when it only tells the ICJ what to apply?

The Court decides disputes “in accordance with international law”, and every UN member is automatically a party to the Statute (Charter art. 93); non-members can join too (Switzerland before 2002).

Q3 · ClassificationWhat are the two classic classifications of sources, and what does Shaw think of them?

Law-creating (treaties, custom, general principles) vs law-determining (decisions, writers); and formal vs material sources. Shaw: functions overlap (treaties restate custom, courts make law), and the formal/material split distracts from real problems.

Q4 · CustomWhat are the two elements of custom, and why is the second needed?

State practice and opinio juris sive necessitatis (Libya/Malta). Without the belief in legal obligation, law could not be told apart from courtesy, goodwill, morality or social usage (tourists, satellites, foreign aid).

Q5 · TheoryWho first formulated opinio juris, and how do positivists and Kelsen differ on it?

François Gény. Positivists make it paramount (consent; even one act can suffice). Kelsen thought it unprovable and said courts decide whether usage becomes custom.

Q6 · AsylumWhat test did Asylum set, and why did Colombia lose?

A rule must follow “a constant and uniform usage practised by the States in question”, expressing a right of Colombia and a duty of Peru. Practice on unilateral qualification of the offence was uncertain and contradictory; as a regional custom it also had to be proved binding on Peru.

Q7 · North SeaWhy was Germany not bound by equidistance?

It had not ratified the 1958 Convention; art. 6 did not codify existing custom (the ILC proposed it hesitantly); and three years of later practice was not “extensive and virtually uniform”, including specially affected states, showing general recognition of a legal obligation. The provision also had to be “fundamentally norm-creating”.

Q8 · NicaraguaHow consistent must practice be?

Not “absolutely rigorous”. It is enough that conduct is generally consistent and that inconsistent conduct is treated as a breach, not as recognition of a new rule.

Q9 · SpeedWhat is “instant” custom, who argued for it, and what does Shaw conclude?

Custom without repetition where opinio juris is clear (Bin Cheng; outer space, airspace). Critics say custom needs a series of usages. Shaw: context decides; new fields with no contrary rules can produce custom quickly.

Q10 · PowerWhat does de Visscher's road analogy show?

Custom forms like a path worn across vacant land, with no precise moment of change; heavier states leave deeper footprints. So custom needs the concurrence of the major powers in the field: “Universality is not required, but some correlation with power is.”

Q11 · LotusWhat did France argue and what did the PCIJ require?

France said the absence of prosecutions by victims' states showed a custom of exclusive flag-state jurisdiction. The Court: abstention counts only if states were “conscious of a duty to abstain”; otherwise the practice “remained a practice, nothing more”. Shaw: the test ignores tacit acceptance.

Q12 · PracticeDo claims count as state practice?

D'Amato: no, until enforcement action (a minority view). Shaw: yes, “clearly the correct” view; claims and counter-claims communicate views on the law. Akehurst: practice is “any act or statements by a state from which views about customary law may be inferred”, weighted by nature and provenance.

Q13 · ParadoxHow can custom change if opinio juris requires belief that conduct is already lawful?

Treat it as a process: states act believing their conduct “is law or is becoming law”; others' reactions accept or reject it (3-mile to 12-mile territorial sea). Nicaragua: a novel right shared by others may modify custom. “Change is rarely smooth but rather spasmodic.”

Q14 · EvidenceHow has the ICJ dealt with the difficulty of proving opinio juris?

A high threshold (Diallo: many BITs prove nothing), but it looks at GA resolutions (content and conditions of adoption), codification conventions and ILC work. Tanaka would infer it from practice; Shaw wants the acting state to signal legality.

Q15 · ObjectorWhat is the persistent objector rule, and what are its conditions?

A state that opposes a custom from its inception is not bound (Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries, Norway). ILC Draft Conclusion 15: object while the rule forms, clearly, known to others, maintained persistently. Dissent from an established custom remains unsettled.

Q16 · SilenceWhy is a failure to protest not always acquiescence?

States stay silent to avoid gratuitous offence, protect political ties or for diplomatic reasons; protesting everything is unrealistic; otherwise “scores of special relationships” would arise; some protests are purely formal. Acquiescence also needs full knowledge (and freedom of will).

Q17 · New statesAre new states bound by existing custom?

Traditional view: yes, by all customs at independence. Consent view (Tunkin): they may choose. The usual proviso: by entering relations without reservation they accept the totality of international law.

Q18 · Local customWhat did Right of Passage decide, and how does local custom differ from general custom?

A local custom can exist between two states (Portugal's passage between enclaves); a particular practice prevails over general rules. Local custom needs positive acceptance by every party and a higher standard of proof; general custom can form by majority or even substantial-minority consensus.

Q19 · TreatiesDistinguish law-making treaties and treaty-contracts, with examples.

Law-making: general relevance, many parties, can bind all (Antarctic Treaty, Genocide Convention; codifying: 1961 Diplomatic Relations Convention). Treaty-contracts: few states, limited topic; not law-making but may evidence custom (with caution).

Q20 · Co-existenceCan a treaty rule and a customary rule with the same content co-exist?

Yes (Nicaragua): customary self-defence was not “subsumed” by art. 51. Because of a US reservation the Court could not apply the treaty but could apply custom. Shaw: theoretically correct and practical, since interpretation principles may differ.

Q21 · D'AmatoWhat was D'Amato's treaty argument, and why does Shaw reject it?

Generalisable treaty provisions create custom ipso facto, without opinio juris or time. Shaw: “clearly going too far”; a few states could legislate for all.

Q22 · PrinciplesName four general principles with a case for each.

Reparation (Chorzów Factory); acquired rights survive (German Settlers); circumstantial evidence (Corfu Channel); res judicata (UN Administrative Tribunal; Bosnia v Serbia 2007); estoppel (Temple; limits in ELSI, Cameroon v Nigeria); full compensation (AMCO); good faith (Nuclear Tests).

Q23 · Good faithWhat are the limits of good faith?

Charter art. 2(2) and res. 2625 enshrine it, and Nuclear Tests says it underlies all obligations. But it is “not in itself a source of obligation” (Nicaragua v Honduras) and relates only to existing obligations (Cameroon v Nigeria).

Q24 · EquityIs equity a source of law?

Courts apply equitable principles within rules (Meuse, North Sea, UNCLOS arts 59/74/83, Burkina Faso/Mali), not abstract or distributive justice (Libya/Malta, Tunisia/Libya). As a separate source it is “at best highly controversial”. Ex aequo et bono (art. 38(2)) needs consent and has never been used.

Q25 · CourtsDoes article 59 stop the ICJ making law?

No binding precedent, but the Court follows itself and sometimes makes law: baselines in Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries (codified 1958), personality in Reparation, reservations, Nottebohm. Lotus was partly abandoned. Cameroon v Nigeria: is there cause not to follow earlier cases?

Q26 · WritersHow has the role of writers changed?

Supreme in the natural law era (Gentili, Grotius, Pufendorf, Bynkershoek, Vattel); declined under positivism. They still organise, explain, critique and bring coherence; the national-prejudice charge is exaggerated.

Q27 · GA resolutionsWhen can General Assembly resolutions matter legally?

They are generally recommendatory, but votes are state practice and can evidence opinio juris. Nuclear Weapons: look at content, conditions of adoption and opinio juris on normative character; a series can show evolution. The 1960 Declaration helped make self-determination a legal right. Shaw warns against treating everything as law.

Q28 · Soft lawWhat is soft law, and how can it become law?

Influential non-binding instruments (Helsinki Final Act; economic and environmental guidelines). “Soft law is not law.” It becomes law through a treaty or acceptance as custom. Status depends on the parties' intention, not the title.

Q29 · Unilateral actsWhen does a unilateral declaration bind a state?

When there is intention to be bound and publicity (Nuclear Tests), read in good faith and restrictively if it limits freedom of action; ILC Guiding Principles add a competent authority and clear, specific terms.

Q30 · HierarchyDistinguish erga omnes and jus cogens.

Erga omnes: owed to the international community as a whole, so all states have a legal interest (scope, procedural; Barcelona Traction). Jus cogens: substantive norms from which no derogation is permitted (VCLT arts 53, 64). Content overlaps (genocide, aggression, slavery, torture).

Q31 · Jus cogensHow is a jus cogens norm created, and what are the treaty consequences?

Two stages: a rule of general international law, then acceptance as peremptory by the international community as a whole. Shaw's test: universal acceptance as law and recognition by an overwhelming majority across divides; only custom or treaty can found it. Art. 53: conflicting treaty void ab initio; art. 64: terminates when a new norm emerges (art. 71 saves prior situations).

Q32 · LimitsCan the Security Council override jus cogens, and does jus cogens give the ICJ jurisdiction?

No: Judge Lauterpacht (Bosnia 1993), art. 103 cannot extend to conflicts with jus cogens. And no: a rule's status is separate from consent to jurisdiction (DRC v Rwanda; Germany v Italy).
