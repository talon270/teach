The reading in one breath

Article 38 seems to leave the ICJ no room for anything but applying international law, yet Bedjaoui argues that expediency (opportunité) is “the faithful but often discreet companion of applied international law”. “Law is the science of security”, but legal security, “not just necessary but essential, is nevertheless not enough”: the Court must also “extinguish a dispute, not ... fan the flames of it”. Expediency lives inside legality: where a norm offers a choice of legal solutions, the Court has discretion, and a decision of expediency is one that, “while remaining legal, is inspired by feelings of appropriateness, wisdom or prudence”. It is never opportunism or arbitrariness. Part I shows why pure application is impossible. Judges are “situated” human beings; the Court runs its own procedure under norms that say “may”; the “international legislator” leaves gaps and vague concepts such as good faith, due diligence and the equitable; and the line between legal and political disputes is unreliable. Part II finds expediency in five functions: ruling when the norm fails (Frontier Dispute, a “judgment of Solomon”); declining to rule (South West Africa 1966, where expediency was “disastrous”, and the discretion to refuse advisory opinions); individualising the law (the “pendulum law”); giving content to indeterminate norms (due diligence, the reasonable, “equitable principles” from North Sea 1969 to Libya/Malta 1985); and supplementing even a clear norm (Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros 1997). His verdict: expediency belongs to judging “as inseparably as a man and his shadow”, and admitting it gives international justice “a human face”.

Intro

Why ask the question · pp. 1–5

Introduction

An international court is “above all the practitioner of the application of international law”. Under Article 38 of its Statute, the ICJ's function is to “decide in accordance with international law such disputes as are submitted to it”, applying conventions, custom and general principles, “without forgetting to draw inspiration from learned doctrine as and when appropriate”. So there would appear to be “no room for any paralegal or metalegal action by the International Court closely or even remotely akin to the elusive concept of expediency” fn 1 p. 1.

Investigating expediency may therefore seem “strange, not to say provocative or irreverent”, or a fashion “in this age of ‘glasnost’”. But the question whether the decisions, “or ‘some’ of the decisions”, conceal “a hint of expediency is neither malapropos nor inspired by fashion”. Answering it would “generate greater understanding and trust on the part of States and doctrine” in an institution “conceived as a cornerstone of the system for the peaceful settlement of disputes put in place by the United Nations Charter” p. 1.

Security, and peacemaking

“Law is the science of security.” The legislator, the administrator and the courts all contribute to it, and “the function of adjudicating” looks “simple and reassuring” in the light of this end of the law, “striving to protect established relationships”: read the concrete case and apply the relevant rules. As Perelman puts it, “a system of law designed on the basis of the sole ideal of legal security would dispense with the need for any judgment, replacing judges with computers” fn 2 pp. 1–2.

Bedjaoui's view: security is necessary but not sufficient

“We are far from the automation of the ‘function of judging’”, and “one might even wager that a ‘syllogisms machine’, a ‘machine for stating the law’, a ‘judging machine’, will never be invented.” In certain circumstances legal security, “which is not just necessary but essential, is nevertheless not enough”. It “sometimes jeopardizes the very order it is supposed to bring about and becomes a potential factor of instability”, and must then “reconcile themselves expediently with other values, such as the feeling of what is equitable or reasonable” p. 2.

Strict application of a rule (supposing one exists and is easy to apply) can guarantee legal security to the winning state, but the court “does not have the power to bring about this legal security in concreto”, so it feels a duty to help realise it. In short, a court “is not exclusively a dispenser of legal security. It is also one of its functions to extinguish a dispute, not to fan the flames of it. The judicial function is bound up with security, but also with peacemaking.” That is one reason courts “can never be replaced by automatons” p. 2.

His method: trace “the operational route taken by the International Court in its passage from the external world to the legal world”, to discern “whether, and, if so, when and how, elements of expediency surface in its decisions” p. 2.

What “expediency” means

Dictionaries are “no great help”. An expedient decision is one that is opportune or suits the case perfectly; opportunité “denotes something which occurs at just the right moment”; the Petit Larousse speaks of “the power afforded for taking the initiative in certain measures”; what is expedient “is advantageous and occurs reasonably and suitably or which fulfils a certain expectation”. Anyone searching the indexes of international law handbooks, in any language, for “opportunité” or “expediency” “will surely be frustrated”: to his knowledge no work deals with expediency except a brief reference in Shabtai Rosenne's book on the Court fn 3. So expediency seems part of an “‘inner sanctum’ peculiar to international courts, which is kept safe from the curious or prying eye of doctrine” pp. 2–3.

Administrative law does discuss it. Professor Chapus: “Legality ... is not all that counts. Expediency is also important.” “It would be distressing if the requirements of the principle of legality were so unwaveringly strict that too often they forced inappropriate behaviour on the administrative authorities.” When they have discretionary power, “it is precisely the freedom to assess what expediency recommends that is vested in these authorities” fn 4. This applies mutatis mutandis to the Court p. 3.

Discretion, legality and arbitrariness

Bedjaoui's view: inside legality

“Discretionary power and expediency are in fact quite intimately linked. Both of them ... form part of the tissue of legality, which is what distinguishes them both from arbitrariness.” When a norm gives a court “the choice between two or more solutions, all of them legal ones”, it gives it latitude: discretion. Within that freedom, “firmly framed by legality”, courts seek “the most expedient decision possible. Sometimes they are successful.” Discretion is “nothing other than the ability of international courts to take a decision freely but legally”, for “the sound administration of justice and not in deference to concerns foreign to their legal duty” p. 3.

A “deviation” from this course can arise, one that “can be checked and is in no way to be scorned”, steering courts “less in the direction of the expedient decision than in that of the decision of expediency”. Keeping to legality, they use legal technique “to avoid a solution which risks being misunderstood and not well received”, choosing the one that best satisfies “the circumstantial factors surrounding the case” p. 3.

Definition. “A decision dictated by expediency is therefore one which, while remaining legal, is inspired by feelings of appropriateness, wisdom or prudence.” These come from the Court's “desire to promote justice and peace between States”. It is not bias: “Considering that there are fifteen judges from different backgrounds, it is out of the question that they could simultaneously be biased in the same direction” pp. 3–4.

Forms it takes. A decision of expediency may be (1) a particular legal solution; (2) “a more appropriate and better-adapted formulation of a classical solution”; or (3) a specific choice of grounds for the operative provision, “rejecting or avoiding other grounds for decision which might be, abstractly, just as valid”. It has a further role: when the norm is “deficient, obscure or inadequate”, or its rigid application “risks producing an unreasonable solution”, the court, “in order to avoid declaring a non liquet, appeals to equity”, seeking “to adjust the abstract norm more effectively to the concrete case” p. 4.

Bedjaoui's view: a discreet companion, never arbitrariness

“Expediency thus appears as the faithful but often discreet companion of applied international law.” It claims a place in almost every decision, “however tenuous”, and that place is “nevertheless inevitable”. But “expediency is neither opportunism nor arbitrariness”. Many institutions share with the Court a power of appraisal on grounds of expediency without being arbitrary. “All administrations risk becoming arbitrary”, but it would be absurd to grant a court, “a body constituted precisely to thwart arbitrariness”, this “‘right to err’”. So what one author calls “a new form of pseudo-judicial arbitrariness” (Pirotte) “cannot conceivably be accepted” fn 5 p. 4.

The aim is “a difficult, even perilous undertaking”: to reveal, “without exaggerating or understating it”, an element “almost inevitably present in virtually any international legal decision, even one apparently founded on the strict application of international law”. “To realize this is not to knock international justice off some imagined pedestal; it is above all to give it a human face”, to show the effort made to render decisions “humanly acceptable”, and “ultimately to underpin the respect due to its decisions and the credibility of the justice it delivers” pp. 4–5.

The plan

  1. The norm the court must apply “may be non-existent, incomplete, imprecise, indeterminate in its content, vague and hazy”: “the corpus of international law is incomplete”. So (a) there are technical limits on applying the norm, and (b) only two approaches remain: push courts towards non liquet, “which would be disastrous”, or recognise an area of discretion. “Hence, the limitations on the duty to apply the norm also have a by-product: the right to apply discretionary power.”
  2. How, then, do courts use that power?

Hence a “two-phase approach”: I, the limits on the judicial function in applying the international positive norm; II, opening the judicial function to expediency, a power that is “discretionary but not arbitrary” p. 5.

I

Part I · p. 5

The limitations on the judicial function in the application of the international positive norm

The aim is to show that “one cannot, always and in all circumstances, strictly and exclusively apply the international legal norm”. There are areas of discretion, and the question is to discern the limits on the judicial function. These “may stem from the court, the legislator or the litigant”, which gives the three sub-parts below p. 5.

I.1

Limits from the court · pp. 6–10

The limitations stemming from the International Court in its human and its legal aspects

A Limitations stemming from the human condition of all judges pp. 6–9

He will not “lose any time probing the hearts and minds of international judges” to measure their subjectivity. He leaves that to authors, jurists or sociologists who build a “‘standard profile’ of the international judge”: Professor Lyndel Prott, whose Tübingen doctoral dissertation, Der internationale Richter im Spannungsfeld zwischen Rechtkulturen, appeared in English as The Latent Power of Culture and the International Judge, and Professor Rüdiger Lautmann's Soziologie vor den Toren der Jurisprudenz fn 6 p. 6.

Prott's view, as he reports it

In chapter II, ICJ judges “have split into groups which vote together in the same way in various cases, owing to a common view of law, ties of friendship and similarity of professional experience”. In chapter VI, training in national legal systems has an influence “particularly easy to detect” in the Court's case law: the approach to a case, the style of judgment and the remedies thought possible are “‘programmed’” by basic and professional legal training fn 7. Methods of interpretation, procedure, judicial philosophy and style thus “allegedly borrow something from the national professional background of the judge” p. 6.

Bedjaoui's view: the judge is “situated”, then “re-situated”

“If there is any truth in this, it should not be exaggerated.” What is certain is that the international judge is “situated”: he or she has “a ‘position’, in the sociological sense, which confers a status and assigns a role”, one that cannot be held “while completely erasing origins, education, and training”. Personal standards of behaviour come from the culture of origin, but also from what colleagues, friends and critics expect, and from the judge's roles “as all at once judge, member of a club, a family or a church”. The role is also shaped by working “colleagues” and by “transmitters”, and national experience “blends into the background of everyday working life in common and the collective international experience” pp. 6–7.

Whatever their cultural baggage, judges “are able to find the ‘common language’ for solving a dispute between States in the sole light of the norms of international law”. But the choice among legal techniques may be partly conditioned by their “diffuse notions” of what is “just” in the society they came from, “interiorized or memorized”. They constantly meet the “‘feeling of justice’ held by their colleagues”, and they perform two roles, “rule applier” and “rule adjuster”, “which creates a latent conflict in them” p. 7.

What limits this “particularity” (not “subjectivity”, even in the best sense) is “the attentive and critical gaze of the ‘rule transmitters’” fn 8: colleagues first, then the states parties, then “the entire international legal profession”. Judges also stay under the gaze of their home countries' legal elites, “who vaguely expect them to be the promoters of their own national legal culture”, and of jurists met at international fora. These jurists are those “in whose eyes they are keen to maintain their reputation”. They also heed learned academies, international organisations, senior international civil servants and the diplomatic corps, “particularly those at the headquarters of the Court and at the headquarters of the United Nations” p. 7.

Bedjaoui's view: international and domestic judges are alike

“There is nothing here to distinguish international judges from domestic ones”: both are shaped by background and by their professional community. The “function of adjudicating” fn 9 “is largely the same for both: all that changes is the setting”. This gives the international judge “a demanding international public spirit”, “so conscious is he of the importance of the duties entrusted to him” and of the “evident necessity to banish all subjectivity from his task and any approach that smacks of mere convenience”. “That nationally ‘situated’ individual, the international judge, is constantly ‘re-situated’ internationally”, braced by professionalism learned at home and by the urge to censure in himself any “particularistic approach” inherited from that education pp. 7–8.

Going further would be “venturesome”: “perilous, fruitless, and unfair”. Examples are a “standard profile” of a judge who is a national of a party, or of the judge ad hoc. Judges ad hoc are chosen by a party when the bench has none of its nationals, and “would at first seem to be more vulnerable than the other judges, who for their part are elected by the United Nations. However, the statistics which could be compiled in these cases provide ample proof of their independence of mind” fn 10 p. 8.

The international judge is “neither more nor less vulnerable than the domestic judge”. “Modern psychology would seem to have clearly established now that the judgment of the human being precedes the concept.” A fact becomes intelligible by reference to a conceptual framework, what specialists call “‘a-priori categories of the mind’” fn 11 p. 8.

Sentence and sentiment. Judging ends in a sentence, from the Latin sententia, derived from sentire (“to judge”). The same root gives “sentiment”, which also means “opinion” or “judgment”, “above all in its archaic sense” (“sentiment of right”, “national sentiment”). Grzegorczyk writes that the judge's sentiment “is a typically personal tool (for him) for discovering value and psychological mechanisms therefore have to be used for evaluating, with all the risks of distortion that any psychologization entails” fn 12 p. 8. He breaks down the judge's inner forces:

B Limitations stemming from the organization of the proceedings by the Court pp. 9–10

Other limits come from the Court's task of running cases. In its ordinary function it “enjoys a natural discretion” to take the steps it thinks most appropriate for organising proceedings, a “sovereign power” every court has. Does expediency surface here? “In a way, yes. But ... the International Court is applying a legal norm charging it to exercise all its discretion. It is a norm conferring an option.” p. 9

The Statute and Rules are “full of this discretion”, which the verb “may” expresses. The Court “may” pp. 9–10:

The Court is bound by procedural rules, “but it is perfectly clear that it has a certain margin of choice”. The optimum conditions for the sound administration of justice are “not codified ne varietur. Their attainment is a matter of circumstances. Expediency appears in them.” In general the Court “is here guided by the rules which it sovereignly assigns itself, but also by the exceptional circumstances of a specific case, which it appraises no less sovereignly”. Although the Court codified its internal practice on 5 July 1968 and again on 12 April 1976, it added that it “remains entirely free to depart from the present Resolution, or any part of it, in a given case, if it considers that the circumstances justify that course” p. 10.

Order of cases. Under Rules Art. 54 the Court fixes the order in which it hears cases, having regard to the priority required by Art. 74 but also to “any other special circumstances, including the urgency of a particular case”, which it is “totally free to decide”. Expediency arises when political events suggest “that a political settlement in the making should be patiently awaited”, and allowed to mature “without forcing the pace and upsetting it with possibly disastrous consequences”. The President's contacts with the parties keep the Court informed of the circumstances, so it can work with expediency in mind “without risk of damage” p. 10.

Bedjaoui's view: no ivory tower

“The Court has never viewed its function as a disembodied work of justice, delivered by a court in an ivory tower, deaf to all information from outside and entrenched behind the barricades of international law.” Its discretion is exercised in the light of the whole context. This is not about the merits, which “must be judged through the application of the rules of international law alone”, but about the “‘organization’ of the proceedings”, which is “the very opposite of the definitive programme or something that is inviolably laid down in advance”. The Court “remains attentive to the concerns of the parties and in touch with the international community” p. 10.

I.2

Limits from the legislator · pp. 10–13

The limitations stemming from the international legislator

To apply international law, “the appropriate norm must exist in that law, and must be clear and not difficult to apply” p. 10. Two things can go wrong.

A Cases of a loophole or legal vacuum p. 11

Domestic law usually regulates stays of proceedings and refusals to rule, and many states forbid courts to decline to rule, “to avoid a denial of justice”. International law might seem more fluid, since the texts governing the Court are silent. But “neither international law in general nor the United Nations Charter, which makes the Court a principal organ, nor the Statute of the Court expressly provides that the Court may decline to rule on a contentious case”. The Court never forgets its raison d'être, settling disputes between states, and its mission of helping states strengthen “a peace-loving international community founded on the rule of law” p. 11.

Bedjaoui's view: consent given, duty follows

“The Court's jurisdiction is admittedly subject to States' consent, but once that consent has been given, the Court is under a duty to fulfil its entire judicial function and nothing but its judicial function.” So it is “not free to refuse to settle a legal dispute whatever its nature, unless ... a gap in international law forces it to make a finding of non liquet”. One may ask whether there are gaps and, if so, whether the Court's peacemaking role should let it claim “a residual rule-making power to fill in any such gaps” p. 11.

In any case the Court “acts at all times out of a concern to safeguard the integrity of its judicial function and not to venture beyond the bounds of that function”. Part II shows how expediency surfaces in its advisory and contentious work. “It should be noted that this is a question not of jurisdiction but of declining to rule.” p. 11

B Limitations stemming from the indeterminate and fluid nature of certain concepts of varying content introduced by the international legislator pp. 11–13

Here the rule exists and is applied, but “its content is imprecise and variable from one case to another”. Faced with such a concept and a choice between actions, a court's margin of discretion “is obviously broader”, and it grows “on a par with that of States whose conduct is also governed by this highly plastic concept”. Eisemann, quoted by Rials: “The source of discretionality ... is the absence of any rigorously mandatory, strict determination of the action ... All it needs is for any one of the concepts to which the regulation appeals to be indeterminate in itself for the discretionary power to appear” fn 14 pp. 11–12.

Bedjaoui's view: two discretions clash

“The discretionary power feeds on the vagueness of a legal norm.” Once a rule gives a state, or a court, freedom of choice, discretion surfaces, and “two discretionalities clash”: the state's, which gives the vague norm a content that “corresponds to its own interests”, and that of the court “which monitors it”, giving it a content “more in line with a standard, or with equity, or with reasonable conduct”. As Stéphane Rials notes, the norm's indeterminability “quite clearly confers an area of discretion and a possible margin of expediency” on the court p. 12.

Against formalism. In the nineteenth century, when international life and law were simpler, “the school of exegesis flourished, which under the leadership of a certain Mourlon favoured stripping courts of all discretion and expediency, demanding their total submission to the letter of the law”. That school “doomed itself by leading the way to what Roscoe Pound called ‘mechanical jurisprudence’” fn 15. It is even less justified now that international life has grown complex and “of necessity” produced many norms with indeterminate content. “The international legislator can hardly require an international court to apply strictly a norm whose content it has not rigorously determined for that court.” p. 12

Six factors of variation. Doctrine has begun to systematise these concepts fn 16. A legal concept's content may vary through these factors pp. 12–13:

Spatiallegal systems each characterise the concept their own way
Linguisticone word means different things in the several authentic languages of a treaty
Materialthe same concept means different things from one convention to another
Temporalthe concept develops and is “transformed” over time: inter-temporal law
Extensionor dispersion: the range of factual situations one concept covers
Comprehensionambiguity or doubt about the concept's constituent elements

Examples of such “vague, ambiguous, imprecise, undefined or indefinable, indeterminate or indeterminable concepts”: international ordre public, good faith, due diligence, urgency, and what is just, equitable or reasonable p. 13.

I.3

Limits from the case · pp. 13–14

Limitations stemming from the nature of the case (the problem of political disputes and legal disputes)

He raises this “though it may not be wholly appropriate”, and without dwelling on this “‘old chestnut’”. Respondents have sometimes argued the “non-justiciability” of a dispute: that it touches their “political and military defence needs”, their “national security” or “vital interests”, or simply “does not lend itself to legal appraisal”. There is no need to labour the point: “As early as the 1930s, Hersch Lauterpacht wrote that the distinction between legal disputes and political disputes had lost its national usefulness and now constituted an obstacle to the progress of law” fn 17 p. 13.

Bedjaoui's view: no reliable line

Under its Statute the Court hears only disputes of a legal nature. “Yet there is no certain, reliable criterion for making this distinction”, and even if there were it would be useless, “since the facts of the dispute merely have to be put to the Court in legal terms, regardless of how political it is”. “All legal disputes of which the Court may rightfully be seised have political implications with which, in principle, it must not concern itself, and every political dispute has legal aspects which may be brought before the International Court.” p. 13

His real question is whether appraisals of expediency have slipped into decisions that turn on the distinction. Two findings p. 14:

II

Part II · p. 14

Opening the judicial function to the field of expediency; discretionary but not arbitrary power

The Court “enjoys a discretionary power of appraisal, which is sometimes extended, in its case, by a certain deference to expediency”. It can be detected through five things, “among many others which might have been mentioned” p. 14:

  1. agreeing to rule, despite the existence of the norm;
  2. declining, directly or indirectly, to rule, despite the existence of the norm;
  3. the Court's function of individualizing the norm;
  4. the Court's function of correcting the norm;
  5. the Court's function of creating the norm.

Watch the labels: when he reaches items 4 and 5, the headings become “adding to or clarifying a norm with indeterminate content” and “supplementing the norm with a well-defined content”.

II.1

Rule · pp. 14–16

The decision to rule, despite the existence of the norm

One decision only: Frontier Dispute (Burkina Faso/Republic of Mali), decided by “the Chamber of the Court over which I had the honour to preside”. Under the Special Agreement the Chamber had “to make a ruling in law and refrain from deciding the case ex aequo et bono”. The parties asked it to apply uti possidetis juris, “thanks to which the newly-independent States were to be confirmed in the frontiers inherited from the colonial period” pp. 14–15.

The Chamber “gallantly applied this principle”, using “colonial law texts, geographical maps, and effectivités” to identify the colonial line, which in places was undetermined. The applicable law proved “vague, inconsistent, and even disconcerting owing to the existence (existence is not exactly the word) of what I termed ‘phantom pools’ and ‘phantom villages’”. “In uti possidetis juris the Court had a clear principle, one undisputed by the parties, yet a principle impotent when faced with an uncertain colonial frontier legacy in several places.” p. 15

“Neither uti possidetis, nor colonial law, nor cartography, nor the effectivités, nor the evidence, were of any help” for a few points, above all “a pool called Kétiouaire, a veritable nightmare to locate”. The Court could have declared “a comprehensive non liquet, to avoid having to present a fragmented frontier”. Instead “it granted itself the power to locate this pool itself and one or two segments of frontier, by appealing to equity”, completing the line with “the most reasonable and equitable” solutions p. 15.

Bedjaoui's view: expediency that worked

The Chamber “incontestably made use of its discretion and of its desire to act with all due prudence in order to adopt the most expedient and acceptable courses” for segments that would otherwise have defeated all attempts at determination. It was “especially anxious not to offend the parties but at the same time to see that equity and justice prevailed”, as other tribunals did in sensitive cases such as the Frontiers of the Andes or the Rann of Kutch fn 18 p. 15.

“Discretion and expediency were successful”: the international press welcomed the judgment as a “judgment of Solomon”, and both parties publicly expressed satisfaction and demarcated the frontier along the Chamber's line. Given these elements of expedient discretion, “the judicial settlement arrived at was akin to an arbitral settlement” pp. 15–16.

II.2

Decline · pp. 16–19

The expediency in declining, directly or indirectly, to rule, despite the existence of the norm

“A distinction must be drawn between the jurisdiction of the Court in contentious cases and its advisory function.” p. 16

A Jurisdiction in contentious cases pp. 16–18

Again, only one case. “The Court has never, to my knowledge, had to decline to rule and frontally make a finding of non liquet.” Events were subtler. In South West Africa (1966), a dispute “with potentially explosive political implications” was “transposed to another domain than that of lack of jurisdiction or non liquet. The application was rejected on other grounds.” p. 16

The Court had already declared that it had jurisdiction over the application by Ethiopia and Liberia against South Africa for its handling of the mandate over South West Africa, “present-day Namibia”. But by its judgment of 18 July 1966 it rejected the application on the ground, “which caused surprise”, that the two African states, “already independent at the time of the League of Nations, which had granted the mandate”, did not have a “‘legal interest to act’” p. 16.

Most commentators' view, as he reports it

The Court “had taken refuge in this unexpected solution to avoid upsetting certain States and a certain public opinion. It was as though the Court, afraid of facing their criticisms, had as it were censored itself in advance” fn 19 p. 16.

Bedjaoui's view: here expediency failed

If so, the Court “had jumped out of the frying-pan into the fire”, meeting “the far more vociferous displeasure of the other States and the rest of international opinion”. “If expediency did play a role in this case, its role would seem to have been disastrous.” The “extremely unpopular decision had deep and lasting negative repercussions, notably in the United Nations, where it precipitated the loss by South Africa of its mandate over Namibia” fn 20 p. 16.

Why caring about reception is legitimate. In his dissent, the American Judge Philip Jessup wrote that international law must not be treated like “an antiquated collection of dead letters” from a remote period, and that “[The] standard to be applied by the Court must be one which takes account of the views and attitudes of the contemporary international community” fn 21. Another author fn 22 p. 17:

Sturgess and Chubb, Judging the World (1988), quoted at p. 17

“The case in question demonstrated [that] law is not something that is apart from reality, apart from politics . . . The case highlights the delicate line the Court must tread. It generally attempts in its judgment to include some statements that make the decisions at least partly acceptable to the losers; it is never highly critical or abusive. The trick, indeed, is to try to make the decision carry as little pain for the loser as possible, to try to avoid making the Court itself unpopular with any part of the International Community.”

Bedjaoui's view: acceptance matters to every court

This is not peculiar to the ICJ: within states, public rejection of judicial decisions can range “from popular outcry to acts of violence”. “Whether national or international, justice cannot avoid concerning itself with earning acceptance through decisions which, while expressing the state of the law, do not completely neglect the current standards of morality. It is this approach which is also called expediency.” Redlich agrees: “Judges do not live in isolation. Their opinions are subject to criticism, and they are, and should be, influenced by popular reaction. The process often works in subtle ways” fn 23 p. 17.

In South West Africa the Court “did not act outside the bounds of international legality. Yet, apparently sensitive to political expediency, it opted for an approach which, while legal, was outmoded”. It was all the more surprising because it let the Court avoid a ruling on the merits, which had been keenly awaited since jurisdiction and admissibility were already settled p. 17.

This bears out Chaïm Perelman: “It is very rare for legal reasoning to lead, like ... mathematical proofs, to a binding conclusion”, so the problem of “the social acceptability” of a decision arises for domestic and international courts alike fn 24. Stéphane Rials adds: “despite the majesty surrounding its decisions, the court cannot dispense with techniques which will make its handiwork more persuasive and legitimate” fn 25 pp. 17–18.

This is truer still of an international court that is “excessively dependent, where the matter of its seisin and jurisdiction are concerned, on the consent of States”. In South West Africa the Court had “sterilized in advance” its own technical solution, which “could but strike the public as a derisory expedient”, since jurisdiction and admissibility were already confirmed. “Opinion and the expectations of the ‘public’ are not a negligible element, constituting what certain authors term ‘infra-law’” fn 26 p. 18.

B The advisory function pp. 18–19

Here the Court has “clear discretionary power regarding the expediency even of giving an advisory opinion”. In its words, “Article 65, paragraph 1, of the Statute, which establishes the power of the Court to give an advisory opinion, is permissive and, under it, that power is of a discretionary character” fn 27, an interpretation it has “on several occasions reverted to” and calls “of course well established” fn 28. In Western Sahara it “firmly laid down the principle that it was under no obligation to rule and that it was free to decline to reply” p. 18.

Protecting its integrity: UNAT and ILOAT. No obligation can be imposed if it “risks prejudicing the integrity of its judicial function”. The UN sought, “by a laborious and inappropriate procedure”, to make the Court, which rules on disputes between states, “a sort of appeals body against the rulings of UNAT and ILOAT concerning international civil servants”. The Court showed its “lack of enthusiasm” for requests on UNAT rulings and detailed a “number of criticisms of this procedure”, which “scarcely complies with the due process of law”, always noting its power to decline p. 18.

Bedjaoui's view: freedom to do well, opportunity to do better

“But there then appeared, in that freedom to do well, an opportunity to do better.” The Court gave reasons why answering was nonetheless “a good opportunity”, all coming down to “the expediency of protecting the international civil servant and the expediency of serving the international organization by taking part in its action and advising it”. In 1995 the General Assembly ended the UNAT procedure “in view of its shortcomings, which the Court had helped to highlight” p. 19.

Other requests. For opinions requested by a competent UN body or authorised institution, the Court “always seeks to establish whether it has a ‘compelling reason’ ... for declining to reply” p. 19.

State opposition. A state's objection does not automatically oblige the Court to decline. When a state such as Spain in Western Sahara or Romania in Mazilu “‘does not consent, it says, to the exercise of the advisory jurisdiction of the Court, [this] does not concern that jurisdiction, but the expediency of its exercise’” fn 29. The test: “[w]hen the circumstances disclose that to give a reply ... would have the effect of circumventing the principle that a State is not obliged to allow its disputes to be submitted to judicial settlement without its consent”, the Court refuses that abuse of procedure. It “cannot lend itself to a manoeuvre making it possible, through the advisory procedure, to circumvent the failure of a State to agree to submit to contentious proceedings” p. 19.

A first refusal. On 8 July 1996, on the WHO's request about the Legality of the Use by a State of Nuclear Weapons in Armed Conflict, the Court “for the first time declined to give an advisory opinion, for lack of an essential condition of its jurisdiction”. So it never reached the arguments about its discretion p. 19.

II.3

Individualize · p. 20

Expediency in the Court's function of individualizing the law (or the process of choosing a solution perfectly suited to a specific case)

Bedjaoui's view: a discreet dose is necessary

“We must take care to understand the message. A discreet dose of expediency is not only inevitable and inescapable but is also indispensable and salutary.” We know “the Faustian power of computing and robotics”, but it cannot eliminate the Court, whose function's “very stuff is nuance and prudence” p. 20.

The pendulum law. Between legislator and court runs what he calls “the pendulum law”. “The law of the jurist is not the law of the scientist.” The scientist discovers laws “by taking care never to become divorced from reality and by always analysing and experimenting with that reality”. The jurist, by contrast, “must necessarily distance himself from it in order to conceptualize it”, giving the law generality, abstraction and chosen purposes. “In relation to reality, the law is thus a construct, subjecting international or domestic relations to a certain chosen order. That is the first swing of the pendulum, or the first movement away from the point of equilibrium represented by reality.” p. 20

When the Court's turn comes, “it reverses the movement of the pendulum, bringing it towards that reality” which it must take into account through a particular case. A latent conflict develops between the legislator, divorced from reality by abstraction, and the Court, whose efforts to adapt and adjust make it take that reality into account p. 20.

Bedjaoui's view: the conflict is productive

“The latent conflict between the legislator, who sees reality as it ought to be, and the Court, which views it as it is presented, is quite simply necessary and beneficial, as it is the agent of legal progress.” The discreet dose of expediency “is like a little leaven giving rise to legal progress”. The abstract norm must keep international society in order, “but in no way ... tyrannize it, overtly at least, otherwise there will be rejection”. Applying the law, a court preserves order while “taking on the tasks of maintenance, cleaning and restoration and sometimes remodelling” p. 20.

“It is not the law which makes and remakes international society, but international society which makes and remakes the law.” Courts help, since their daily work reveals how far the law is adapted “to the contemporary world the law seeks to govern” p. 20.

II.4

Clarify · pp. 21–25

Expediency in the function of adding to or clarifying a norm with indeterminate content

A precise and complete norm gives a court “the necessary and sufficient guidelines” to rule without difficulty. “Yet this is not always so” p. 21:

Perelman, quoted at p. 21

“Bearing in mind the variety of circumstances, the fact that it is not capable of foreseeing everything and regulating everything with precision, since rigid rules can only be applied with difficulty to changing situations, the legislator can intentionally introduce into the text of the law concepts with varying, vague, indeterminate content, such as equity, what is reasonable, [due diligence, etc.], leaving to the court the task of clarifying them in each particular case.” fn 30

In international law, the reasonable, the equitable, the just, the urgent, due diligence, good faith and international ordre public all have variable content. European Community law shows “even a small explosion of indeterminate concepts”: “normal” possibilities of remuneration, “normal” conditions of competition, “fair” limits on prices, “rational” exploitation of resources, “comparable” conditions of production, the “equalization” principle p. 21. An expert on that law, Bauer-Bernet, writes fn 31:

Bauer-Bernet, quoted at p. 21

“Lack of normative determination in a legal system is a means of delegation or reservation of power. It is necessary to the legislator who cannot or will not concern himself with the detail, and necessary to the administrator and the court, in that it leaves them a freedom of action.”

So “the changing search for normality” lets a court “to justify (its) decision by an effort to achieve coherence and generalization founded on the (direct or indirect) reference to normal conduct” p. 21.

Due diligence and the reasonable

In the Alabama case the arbitrator gave content to “due diligence” “by recourse to discretion”. Its extent depends on the state's territorial and personal jurisdiction, the vigilance the situation demands, the risks and how foreseeable they are, the means available to the state, the capacity of the person to be protected, and so on. All this “must be freely appraised by an international court, which may undertake an assessment of the circumstances and expediency which may dictate diligent conduct in a concrete situation”. This is “review of discretion”, which some domestic courts decline to exercise over their administrations, “but which the International Court in no way declines to exercise” pp. 21–22.

“Reasonable” appears in many conventions that contain states' freedom within acceptable proportions: reasonable time-limits or notice, quantities, prices. Courts must weigh the circumstances to decide what merits the label, “in an appraisal where the degree of expediency is hard to measure” p. 22.

Equitable principles

It is the concept of “equitable principles” that most easily reveals the Court's “small areas of expediency”. Three points p. 22:

  1. It is “essentially a judge-made creation”: the Court's 1969 judgment in North Sea Continental Shelf “truly established the pedigree of this concept”.
  2. Having “experienced the fleeting favours of treaty law, and the more robust ones today of customary law”, it “constitutes a positive norm”.
  3. Its content is “still imprecise and elusive”, though this flexibility, which gives the Court some freedom of appraisal, “does not thereby deprive it of all its normativity”.

Equity has made “enormous inroads” into law: in “the formation and elaboration of the legal norm, whose very substance is equity”, and in applying it, where it serves “to temper the disproportion between the legal rule, which is slow to evolve, and changing social needs”, so as “to humanize the law, to relax its rigidity” and adapt it to the case p. 22.

  • Equity infra legem is “a technique for the interpretation of the law, distinguishing itself from the decision ex aequo et bono by virtue of the fact that it intervenes within the strict limits of positive law in order to adjust it to the case and not to usurp its function”. The court's discretion here is as wide as “the margin separating the legal rule from the concrete situation” p. 22.
  • Equity praeter legem plays a complementary role: “complementing positive law, either in cases where the law is silent or in cases where the law is inadequate or obscure”. Expediency is exercised “on the occasion of a loophole or shortcoming in the legal norm” pp. 22–23.

Either way, recourse to equity is “an operation which is all nuance and finesse” fn 32, and it is “impossible to contain in any formula whatever the infinitely supple and diverse mental operations which guide the court towards what it envisages as a just solution” fn 33 p. 23.

The law of the sea

Here equity is a substantive rule. The law of the sea is said to be “still at a rudimentary stage” with weak normativity, “and yet the contemporary law of maritime delimitations is awash with equity”, in the norm (its entire substance) and in its application. Since equity's content is variable, the task is “to increase the degree of normativity of this law by making the content of equity ever more precise” p. 23.

“It is the international legislator who, in the Montego Bay Convention of 1982, conferred on the court a high degree of discretion”, handing it a norm that “ill deserved to be baptized a ‘fundamental’ norm especially as it appeared virtually empty of content”. E. David: “the discretion enjoyed by courts very faithfully reflects the situation of a law whose tensions and contradictory movements criss-crossing it result in compromises where flexibility sometimes verges on vacuity” fn 34 p. 23.

Case law then showed that “equitable principles”, having vanished from the positive norm, “which now refers only to an ‘equitable result’”, and then been reintroduced as customary law, risked failing without the security a rule of law gives, left to “institutionalized unpredictability”. “So in the end the Court reacted positively, by conferring upon equity and equitable principles a normative, security-inducing, predictable dimension, general in its application. That was the Libya/Malta judgment of 1985.” p. 23

The Court's power, “a real one, with a few choices of expediency still detectable”, is not so great as to make delimitation “a ‘lottery’”, as Prosper Weil says fn 35: “The 1985 Judgment restored the normative function to the law of delimitation. Equity ceases to fluctuate on the whim of judicial subjectivities” fn 36 p. 24.

Bedjaoui's view: not absolute certainty

“This must not be regarded as an absolute certainty.” Despite “great progress in the conquest of normativity”, no one can remove “all trace of personal discretion”. The tool, equitable principles, is “not rigorously precise, indeed anything but”, so weighing relevant circumstances cannot be “an operation of mathematical precision”. “The quality of this intellectual operation and the equity of the final result depend on the existence of a minimum of flexibility in the discretion left to the International Court.” p. 24

Equitable principles “have the appearance of being a legal arsenal from which the Court selects the tools” to identify, evaluate, understand and satisfy circumstances “recognized as being legally relevant by reference to an equitable principle taken by the Court as a postulate”. No rigid rule gives “an instantaneous reading of the exact and invariable weight to be attributed to each element in the case”. They are full of “‘individualizing action’, as Charles de Visscher put it”, adapting the law to a concrete case. The gains, the “juridicity” of equitable “principles” today and the normativity of applicable “methods” tomorrow, “were neither easy nor irreversible”. “Equity, even that equity which is the most firmly tied to the law, by its very essence conceals an element of subjectivity. Equity is a fundamentally contingent concept, which varies according to individuals, prejudices, morals, society and also time.” p. 24

So the three signatories of the joint separate opinion in Libya/Malta, “one of them myself”, could legitimately write fn 37 pp. 24–25:

Joint separate opinion, Ruda, Bedjaoui and Jiménez de Aréchaga, Libya/Malta (1985)

“The entire process of maritime delimitation law is dominated by a ‘fundamental norm’, that of the equitable result, which is as uninstructive as it is all-embracing. That being so, a judge can but anxiously, humbly, gauge and compare his crushing responsibility and the modest means at his disposal for assuming it. He undergoes what Verlaine called ‘l'extase et la terreur de celui qui a été choisi’. He cannot see how to escape from the frustrating tyranny of a certain ‘praetorian subjectivism’ when the very margin of indeterminacy responsible for it originated in a law still young and permeated with equity—which, though a highly respectable legal concept, is inevitably measured with a ‘human’ yardstick. The finest legal dissertations on equity will never succeed in completely eliminating what is perhaps an irreducible core of the judicial subjectivism mentioned above. The utmost, in all honour, that a judge can then do is modest: to summon up all his resources with a view to reducing its scope and effects to a minimum.”

Conclusion

“Expediency is a subject which it is difficult, if not impossible, to study.” It resists clarification and system and “has the air of a challenge. The discretion of shadow suits it better than the glare of the footlights.” p. 26

The Court's basic function is “to apply the law almost exclusively in its task of ‘stating the law’”, dispensing legal security, “the role for which it was created”. But the case's circumstances remind it of “its subsidiary role, which is to extinguish the dispute, to help to pacify and normalize relations between States”. Its decision “must not be counterproductive and exacerbate emotions”, so circumstances will prompt it “to add a more or less discreet note of expediency whenever legality so permits” p. 26.

The forms vary with the cases: from “the manner of presenting or formulating a passage with a view to giving it the most acceptable style”, to supplementing a deficient norm, giving content to an indeterminate one, or adjusting a norm to the case. Not all of these operations amount to expediency: “Let us be wary of giving the impression that expediency envelops them completely in its cloak, and say more modestly that ‘nuggets’ of expediency appear here and there” p. 26.

Bedjaoui's verdict

“The discretionary power of the International Court, and its fitting episodic corollary—expediency—necessarily belong to the judicial function as inseparably as a man and his shadow.” Used for “the smooth administration of justice”, they add to legal security “a quality of justice and equity liable to extinguish disputes and harmonize relations” p. 26.

Should the ebb and flow of normativity cause “disquiet or regret? I do not think so.” Legal security “deserves to be invigorated and consolidated from time to time, when necessary, by a discreet legal impressionism of the Court, in order to achieve a sound dialectic of progress” p. 26.

Where concepts are indeterminate, legal security, “composed of a guarantee of equality, uniformity, impartiality, predictability”, sometimes cannot be kept. That does not mean the Court “makes a triumphant legal impressionism prevail over a sacrificed legal security. These are not uncertain ebbs and flows of normativity. It is normativity giving itself a spiritual boost, and enhancing its life force, in itself organizing, in the legal context which it clearly signposts, the conditions for its fertilization by the International Court” pp. 26–27.

  1. Art. 38 seems to leave no room for expediency, yet ICJ decisions carry “a hint” of it, and admitting this builds trust in the Court.
  2. “Law is the science of security”, but security alone “is nevertheless not enough”: the Court must also extinguish disputes, so its function is peacemaking too.
  3. Expediency (opportunité) is a decision that, “while remaining legal, is inspired by feelings of appropriateness, wisdom or prudence”. It sits inside legality with discretion, never with arbitrariness.
  4. Part I: pure application fails because of limits from the court (the “situated” judge; procedural norms that say “may”), from the legislator (gaps; concepts of varying content) and from the case (no reliable legal/political line).
  5. II.1 Ruling when the norm fails: in Frontier Dispute, uti possidetis was “impotent” at the “phantom pools”, so the Chamber used equity; the result was a “judgment of Solomon”.
  6. II.2 Declining: South West Africa (1966) dodged the merits through “legal interest”, and expediency proved “disastrous”. Advisory discretion (Art. 65(1)) is real but may not be used to get round a state's consent.
  7. II.3 Individualizing: under the “pendulum law” the legislator abstracts away from reality and the Court swings back; their conflict is “the agent of legal progress”.
  8. II.4 Clarifying: due diligence (Alabama), the reasonable, and equitable principles from North Sea (1969) to Libya/Malta (1985). Equity works infra legem or praeter legem, and an “irreducible core” of subjectivism remains.
  9. II.5 Supplementing: in Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros (1997) the 1977 Treaty stands, but it is applied to the facts as they stood in 1997.
  10. Verdict: expediency belongs to judging “as inseparably as a man and his shadow”. It gives justice “a human face” and is “normativity giving itself a spiritual boost”.

Terms

Expediency (opportunité)
A legal decision inspired by appropriateness, wisdom or prudence, aimed at justice and peace between states. The English word may not match the French exactly (fn 1).
Legal security
What law exists to provide: “equality, uniformity, impartiality, predictability” (p. 26). Essential, but not enough.
“Judging machine”
Also a “syllogisms machine” or “machine for stating the law”: the automated judge that he wagers will never be invented.
Discretionary power
The freedom to choose between two or more legal solutions: “to take a decision freely but legally”.
Expedient decision vs decision of expediency
The first is the best-suited legal solution; the second is the legal solution chosen so as not to be “misunderstood and not well received”.
Arbitrariness
Outside legality; expediency is “neither opportunism nor arbitrariness”. Pirotte's “pseudo-judicial arbitrariness” is unacceptable.
Non liquet
A finding that no law applies, so the court cannot rule. He calls pushing courts towards it “disastrous”; the ICJ has never made one frontally.
“Situated” / “re-situated”
The judge holds a sociological position shaped by origins, but is constantly re-situated by the international setting.
Rule applier / rule adjuster
The two roles each judge performs, creating “a latent conflict”.
Rule transmitters
Prott's term: colleagues, parties and the profession whose gaze checks a judge's particularity.
Standard profile
A sociological portrait of the typical judge, national judge or judge ad hoc; going that far is “perilous, fruitless, and unfair”.
Judge ad hoc
A judge chosen by a party with no national on the bench. They look more vulnerable, but the statistics prove their independence.
A-priori categories of the mind
The conceptual framework through which a judge makes facts intelligible (Bachelard).
Sententia / sentire
The Latin roots of “sentence” and “sentiment”: judging involves feeling.
Norm conferring an option
A procedural rule that says the Court “may”; using it is discretion under law.
Ne varietur
Fixed once and for all. The conditions of good justice are not codified that way.
Denial of justice
What domestic bans on refusing to rule prevent.
Concepts of varying content
Notions à contenu variable: good faith, due diligence, urgency, ordre public, the just, the equitable, the reasonable.
Discretionality, indeterminability
Discretion “feeds on the vagueness” of a norm; Rials's word for the norm that cannot be pinned down.
School of exegesis
The nineteenth-century school (Mourlon) that demanded total submission to the letter of the law.
Mechanical jurisprudence
Roscoe Pound's name for where the exegetical approach led.
Non-justiciability
A respondent's claim that a dispute is political (security, “vital interests”), which he calls an “old chestnut”.
Jurisprudential policy
The Court's appraisals of expediency about the political capital that could be made of its decisions.
Uti possidetis juris
New states keep the frontiers inherited from the colonial period.
Effectivités
Evidence of actual administration on the ground, used to trace a frontier.
Phantom pools, phantom villages
His term for places in colonial texts that could not be found, such as the pool of Kétiouaire.
Legal interest to act
The ground on which South West Africa (1966) rejected Ethiopia's and Liberia's application.
Infra-law
Public opinion and expectation (Arnaud's l'avant-dire-droit) that shape a decision before delivery.
Compelling reason
What the Court looks for before declining to give an advisory opinion.
Pendulum law
The legislator swings the norm away from reality into abstraction; the Court swings it back.
Review of discretion
Judicial control of how a state used its latitude, for example due diligence. The ICJ exercises it.
Equitable principles / equitable result
A judge-made concept (North Sea) that became treaty and custom; the 1982 Convention speaks of an equitable result.
Equity infra legem
Equity inside the law: interpreting and adjusting the rule to the case.
Equity praeter legem
Equity beside the law: filling gaps where the law is silent, inadequate or obscure.
Ex aequo et bono
A decision on fairness outside the law, which equity infra legem is not.
Individualizing action
De Visscher's phrase for how equitable principles adapt law to a concrete case.
Praetorian subjectivism
The judge-made subjectivity that the “equitable result” norm forces on delimitation (joint opinion, 1985).
Nuggets of expediency
His modest claim: expediency appears “here and there”, not as a cloak over everything.
Legal impressionism
The Court's discreet touch that invigorates legal security, “a sound dialectic of progress”.

Traps

  • Expedient decision ≠ decision of expediency. Both are legal. The second aims at how the decision will be received.
  • Expediency is not arbitrariness, nor opportunism, nor bias: fifteen judges cannot all lean the same way.
  • No frontal non liquet: to his knowledge the Court has never made one. South West Africa was decided on “legal interest to act”, not on jurisdiction.
  • South West Africa order of events: jurisdiction and admissibility were upheld first; the 1966 judgment then rejected the claim. Fn 20 misprints the date as 1996.
  • WHO 1996: the first refusal of an advisory opinion, for lack of jurisdiction, not by using discretion.
  • State non-consent in advisory cases (Spain, Romania) goes to “the expediency of its exercise”, not to jurisdiction.
  • The list vs the headings: p. 14 says “correcting” and “creating” the norm; sections 4 and 5 are headed “adding to or clarifying” (indeterminate content) and “supplementing” (well-defined content).
  • Equitable principles are judge-made (North Sea 1969), then treaty and custom. The 1982 Convention refers “only to an ‘equitable result’”.
  • Infra legem vs praeter legem: inside the law (interpretation) vs filling gaps. Neither is ex aequo et bono.
  • Weil vs Bedjaoui on 1985: Weil says Libya/Malta “restored the normative function”; Bedjaoui adds that it is not “an absolute certainty”.
  • The pendulum: the legislator makes the first swing, away from reality; the Court makes the second, back towards it.
  • Frontier Dispute was a Chamber that Bedjaoui presided. Its Special Agreement excluded ex aequo et bono, yet it used equity within the law.
  • Organising proceedings involves expediency, but through “a norm conferring an option”; the merits are for law alone.
  • Who is “situated”: the judge. Bedjaoui accepts that, but rejects sociological “standard profiles”.

The course lens

What international law is, and who makes it

Formally, it is the Art. 38 law the Court applies. But the “international legislator” (states drafting treaties such as the 1982 Convention) often leaves norms vague, and indeterminacy is “a means of delegation or reservation of power” (p. 21). So the Court clarifies, individualizes and supplements. One key concept, equitable principles, is “essentially a judge-made creation” (p. 22). Law is “a construct” imposing “a certain chosen order”, and “international society ... makes and remakes the law”, with courts contributing (p. 20).

How power shapes it

Consent is the frame: the Court is “excessively dependent ... on the consent of States” (p. 18), and the advisory route may not bypass a state's refusal (Spain, Romania). Reception by states and public opinion (“infra-law”) shapes decisions. Empire runs through the cases. Uti possidetis confirms “frontiers inherited from the colonial period”, drawn from colonial law texts (p. 15). South West Africa (1966) turned away two African states' challenge to South Africa's mandate, a decision the rest of the world met with indignation (pp. 16–18). Race and apartheid go unnamed.

Not in Bedjaoui: unlike the 1991 piece, this text sometimes says “his or her”. Rosalyn Higgins, cited here as a scholar, had joined the Court in 1995.

The approach

Mainstream, from a judge on the inside, and anti-formalist. He rejects the “judging machine”, the school of exegesis and “mechanical jurisprudence”, and draws on sociology of law (Prott, Lautmann, Arnaud), rhetoric (Perelman) and French administrative law (Chapus). The frame stays positivist: expediency is “part of the tissue of legality”, and equity infra legem works “within the strict limits of positive law”. That puts him near legal realism's interest in how judges actually decide, without giving up legality. Values such as equity, justice and “current standards of morality” enter through legal concepts, not natural law. He is a Third World jurist, but this piece is not written as TWAIL.

Retrieval practice

self-test

Answer aloud or on paper first, then open the card. The questions follow the reading's order.

01Why does Art. 38 seem to leave no room for expediency?

It tells the Court to “decide in accordance with international law”, applying conventions, custom, general principles and doctrine, so there seems no room for “paralegal or metalegal action” (p. 1).

02Why is legal security “not enough”?

Rigid security can become “a potential factor of instability”, and a court cannot guarantee security in concreto. It must also extinguish disputes: the judicial function is “bound up with security, but also with peacemaking” (p. 2).

03What does Bedjaoui wager will never be invented?

A “syllogisms machine”, a “machine for stating the law”, a “judging machine”; courts “can never be replaced by automatons” (p. 2).

04Which handbook mentions expediency, and what happened to the entry?

Rosenne's Law and Practice of the International Court (2nd edn 1985), briefly; in the 1997 third edition “the word ‘expediency’ has vanished from the general index” (fn 3).

05How do discretion and expediency differ from arbitrariness?

Both “form part of the tissue of legality”; discretion is the freedom to choose between legal solutions, “freely but legally”. Arbitrariness is outside the law (p. 3).

06Distinguish the “expedient decision” from the “decision of expediency”.

The expedient decision is the most suitable legal solution. The decision of expediency uses legal technique to avoid a solution “misunderstood and not well received”, choosing what best fits the circumstances (p. 3).

07Give Bedjaoui's definition of a decision dictated by expediency.

One “which, while remaining legal, is inspired by feelings of appropriateness, wisdom or prudence”, suggested by the desire “to promote justice and peace between States” (pp. 3–4).

08Why is expediency not bias?

With “fifteen judges from different backgrounds, it is out of the question that they could simultaneously be biased in the same direction” (p. 4).

09What three forms can a decision of expediency take?

A particular legal solution; a better-adapted formulation of a classical solution; or a choice of grounds that avoids others “just as valid” in the abstract (p. 4).

10What does the plan say is the “by-product” of an incomplete corpus of law?

Since pushing courts towards non liquet “would be disastrous”, the limits on the duty to apply the norm yield “the right to apply discretionary power” (p. 5).

11From what three sources do limits on the judicial function stem?

The court, the legislator and the litigant (the nature of the case) (p. 5).

12What does Prott claim about ICJ judges, and how does Bedjaoui respond?

Judges form blocs voting together (shared view of law, friendship, experience), and national training “programmes” their approach. It “should not be exaggerated”: judges are “situated” but constantly “re-situated” internationally (pp. 6–8).

13What two roles does a judge perform, and who checks the judge's particularity?

“Rule applier” and “rule adjuster”, in latent conflict. The “rule transmitters”: colleagues, the parties, the whole legal profession, home legal elites, academies, organisations, diplomats (p. 7).

14What does he say of judges ad hoc?

They “would at first seem to be more vulnerable”, but statistics give “ample proof of their independence of mind”. Prott over-generalised about Van Wyk, Riphagen and Chagla (p. 8, fn 10).

15Why is organising proceedings a case of discretion under law?

The Statute and Rules say the Court “may” (chambers, provisional measures, experts, joinder and so on): “a norm conferring an option”. The internal-practice Resolutions (1968, 1976) also leave it free to depart from them (pp. 9–10).

16When might expediency affect the order of cases?

Under Rules Art. 54, when political events suggest “a political settlement in the making should be patiently awaited”; the President's contacts keep the Court informed (p. 10).

17May the ICJ decline to rule on a contentious case?

No text allows it. Once states consent, the Court must fulfil “its entire judicial function and nothing but its judicial function”, unless a gap forces a non liquet. It is “a question not of jurisdiction but of declining to rule” (p. 11).

18What happens when a norm has indeterminate content?

Discretion widens: “the discretionary power feeds on the vagueness of a legal norm”, and “two discretionalities clash”, the state's and the court's (p. 12).

19Who led the school of exegesis, and where did it lead?

“A certain Mourlon”; it demanded total submission to the letter of the law and led to what Roscoe Pound called “mechanical jurisprudence” (p. 12).

20Name the six factors that make a concept's content vary.

Spatial, linguistic, material, temporal (inter-temporal law), extension (dispersion) and comprehension, from Salmon (pp. 12–13, fn 16).

21What did Lauterpacht say about legal and political disputes?

In the 1930s (The Function of Law, 1933), that the distinction had lost its usefulness and “now constituted an obstacle to the progress of law” (p. 13).

22What two trends does Bedjaoui find in politically charged cases?

The Court has strengthened its jurisdiction even when political bodies are also seised, and it increasingly considers “the political capital” made of its decisions: “a certain jurisprudential policy” (p. 14).

23What happened at Kétiouaire, and how was the judgment received?

Uti possidetis, colonial law, maps and effectivités could not locate the pool. Instead of a non liquet, the Chamber located it by equity. The press called it a “judgment of Solomon” and the parties demarcated the line (pp. 15–16).

24On what ground was South West Africa rejected in 1966, and what does Bedjaoui conclude?

Ethiopia and Liberia lacked a “legal interest to act”. If expediency played a role, it “would seem to have been disastrous”: the decision was legal but “outmoded”, and it precipitated South Africa's loss of the mandate (pp. 16–17).

25What did Jessup say in dissent?

International law is not “an antiquated collection of dead letters”; the Court's standard “must be one which takes account of the views and attitudes of the contemporary international community” (p. 17).

26What is “infra-law”?

Arnaud's term for public opinion and expectation, “l'avant-dire-droit”, which shape a decision's contours before it is delivered (p. 18, fn 26).

27What is the Court's position on its power to give advisory opinions?

Art. 65(1) is “permissive” and the power “discretionary” (Western Sahara); it declines only for a “compelling reason” and will not let the advisory route circumvent a state's refusal of consent (pp. 18–19).

28Why did the Court still answer UNAT review requests, and what ended them?

“In that freedom to do well, an opportunity to do better”: to protect the civil servant and serve the organisation. The General Assembly ended the procedure in 1995 (pp. 18–19).

29What was new on 8 July 1996?

For the first time the Court declined an advisory opinion (the WHO's request on nuclear weapons), for lack of an essential condition of its jurisdiction, not on discretion (p. 19).

30Explain the pendulum law.

The legislator distances law from reality to conceptualise it (the first swing); the Court brings it back to the case. Their latent conflict (“as it ought to be” versus “as it is presented”) is “the agent of legal progress” (p. 20).

31What three points does he make about equitable principles, and how do equity infra and praeter legem differ?

They are judge-made (North Sea 1969), a positive norm (treaty, then custom), and imprecise. Infra legem interprets within positive law (not ex aequo et bono); praeter legem fills gaps where law is silent, inadequate or obscure (pp. 22–23).

32What did Libya/Malta (1985) and Gabčíkovo (1997) show?

Libya/Malta gave equity a “normative, security-inducing, predictable dimension”; for Weil delimitation is no “lottery”, though Bedjaoui keeps an “irreducible core” of subjectivism. Gabčíkovo applied the 1977 Treaty to the 1997 facts, since the right answer in 1989 “could be a miscarriage of justice” in 1997 (pp. 23–25).
