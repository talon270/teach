Charlesworth on Sources

Hilary Charlesworth, “Law-making and sources”, chapter 8 of James Crawford and Martti Koskenniemi (eds), The Cambridge Companion to International Law (Cambridge University Press, 2012), pp. 187–202.

The chapter in one breath

Talking about “sources” comforts international lawyers that they practise a technical, legal craft, but the doctrine is “selective and partial”. Article 38(1) of the ICJ Statute has become a “pithy mantra” that postpones the politics of who designates sources, and sources are shaped by those with the power to designate them (the Third World and feminist critiques). International law is a volatile mix of state consent and abstract values, Koskenniemi's apology and utopia. Charlesworth walks through each Article 38 source to show how each blurs: treaties bind non-parties, custom's elements merge and its paradox resists solution, general principles were a drafting compromise, and courts and writers matter more than the Statute admits. Soft law then stretches all the categories without replacing them. Her conclusion: legitimacy has shifted from formal sources towards values, and individual state consent matters less than it did.

Her four moves

  1. Sources talk is a formalist comfort that masks politics (Iraq 2003; Koskenniemi).
  2. Article 38(1) is canonical but “dated and increasingly misleading”.
  3. Every source leaks into the others, and soft law blurs them all.
  4. Legitimacy is moving from formal sources to values, and from state consent to wider participation.

Introduction

pp. 187–190Why sources, Iraq, Article 38(1), hierarchy, apology and utopia

Why lawyers obsess over sources p. 187

International law is constantly challenged as a legal system. Charlesworth lists the charges:

  • it is weak, mutable and unstable Morgenthau 1948;
  • it is the mere product of states maximising their interests Goldsmith & Posner 2005;
  • more positively, it is the framework of mundane activities such as airline travel and international postal services Henkin 1979;
  • or it is valuable as a “placemarker for justice” or a vehicle for the “regulative ideal of the international community” Koskenniemi 2007.

Because there is so much anxiety about whether international law really counts as law, the making and sources of international law dominate most introductory works. “It is as if pinning down the well-springs of international law will provide certainty and authority for the discipline.” (p. 187)

So where does international law come from? The sources are “a complex tangle of ideas, commitments and aspirations”. In national legal systems, law comes from legislatures or court systems; identifying the legal principle in a dispute is relatively straightforward even when its application is debated; and institutions such as police forces and civil authorities enforce the law, reinforcing the significance of legal status. Modern international law, by contrast, is partly the product of the behaviour and agreement of states and partly the product of abstract values such as “humanity” Peters 2009, “fairness” Franck 1998 or “communitarian values” Tasioulas 1996. Jurists debate the proper contributions of state consent and moral values, which sometimes coincide.

Charlesworth

“However defined, this mixture is a volatile one, which, together with the less-certain enforcement of international law, makes it appear more negotiable and uncertain than domestic law.” (p. 187)

Iraq 2003: sources as argument p. 188

The controversy over the invasion of Iraq in March 2003 shows both how states resort to sources and how contestable they are.

  • Proponents cited UN Security Council resolutions, a customary rule allowing pre-emptive self-defence, and a moral principle that dictatorial regimes should be removed from office.
  • Critics cited treaty commitments, particularly article 2(4) and Chapter VII of the UN Charter, to argue that the use of force was illegal.

Inquiries into the invasion showed that politicians felt constrained to make public arguments about legality while privately regarding the rules as vague and malleable, “capable of endorsing almost any move”. The talk of sources masked an array of political decisions, but it also allowed people to assess how plausible and weighty the proffered sources were. Sources doctrine was used as a formalist device:

to verify or validate the argumentative materiel that enables the legal profession to continue to carry out its legal job without having to transform itself into a legislative agency ... or a priesthood of rights and wrong.

Koskenniemi 2000, quoted at p. 188

Who designates the sources? p. 188

Charlesworth

“Sources of international law are inflected by the identities of those who have the power to designate them.” (p. 188) That is why Third World and feminist scholars have pointed to biases built into traditional accounts of sources and argued for extending the notion and categories of sources to support their projects.

  • Third World (TWAIL) critique. Some Third World international lawyers criticise the development of customary rules from the practices of colonial powers, and argue for the legal status of resolutions of international institutions where developing states hold large majorities, such as the UN General Assembly Anghie & Chimni 2004.
  • Feminist critique. Feminist international lawyers point to the lack of women in norm-generating institutions and to the narrowness of the categories of sources, which effectively excludes women's experiences and interests Charlesworth & Chinkin 2000.

Article 38(1): the canonical list pp. 188–189

International lawyers traditionally start with article 38(1) of the ICJ Statute, which lists the types of principles the Court should apply in deciding cases. The catalogue was first endorsed in 1922, in the Statute of the Permanent Court of International Justice (PCIJ). Technically it is simply a direction to the Court to apply certain categories of rules, but it “has achieved an almost canonical status as a codification of the sources of international law” (p. 189).

Why does the list exercise such sway? Recourse to it lets international lawyers sidestep complex debates about the function of international law and the relative legitimacy of state consent and claims of justice.

Charlesworth

“It is a pithy mantra that offers a quasi-scientific formula for practitioners of international law, postponing (possibly indefinitely) discussion of the politics of the designated sources.” Its formal nature obscures the fact that international law is generated by “a multi-layered process of interactions, instruments, pressures and principles” (p. 189). Specialised fields (trade law, human rights law, the law of armed conflict, environmental law) also differ in the priority they give to different sources and in how they approach them.

Article 38(1) records four categories: international treaties, international custom, general principles of law, and (together) judicial decisions and the work of scholars. The Statute also contains an “intriguing” provision, article 38(2), letting the Court decide a case ex aequo et bono if the parties agree, meaning on the basis of equity and justice without reference to international law. It has never been used in the International Court.

Soft law enters p. 189

The language of article 38 has been called “dated and increasingly misleading” Boyle & Chinkin 2007, and the considerable growth in significance of “soft” international law has challenged the clarity of its neat typology. “The notion of soft law is famously slippery.” It covers instruments that have legal implications but lack the imagined “hardness” of binding obligations:

  • resolutions of international institutions;
  • declarations of high-level meetings;
  • statements of bodies that monitor treaties;
  • voluntary codes of conduct;
  • treaty provisions that contain no commitments to action.

Is there a hierarchy of sources? pp. 189–190

The ICJ Statute designates judicial decisions and scholarly writings as “subsidiary means for the determination of rules of law”, but otherwise does not rank the categories. The first three (treaties, custom, general principles) are all manifestations of state consent to particular principles.

  • In practice, a relevant treaty is likely to be given priority, because of the apparent certainty of the written word and the formal display of consent by its parties.
  • Custom is harder to establish: it requires intricate tracking of state actions plus evidence that the actions were considered legally required.
  • General principles are more obscure in content than treaties.
Charlesworth

There may be a hierarchy in terms of ease of identification, but in theory treaties, custom and general principles are all equally capable of generating legal norms of comparable weight. Each source can have “hard” and “soft” manifestations, and whether a principle is legal depends on context. This equality reflects the range of international law-makers and the legally and morally pluralist nature of international society Besson 2010. By contrast, the category of peremptory norms (jus cogens) suggests a hierarchy of norms rather than sources (p. 190).

Apology and utopia p. 190

Other chapters of the Companion (for example chapter 2) stress that international law mixes justifications for state behaviour with commitment to apparently universal values such as justice and peace. This is the tradition of apologetic and utopian thought entwined in international legal argument that Martti Koskenniemi describes Koskenniemi 2005. The sources reflect the same tension: between the political reality that state consent is integral to the system's efficacy, and the idealistic force of abstract principles such as justice and fairness.

Charlesworth

“The increasing role of international institutions in law-making has, however, given values a boost and reduced the significance of individual state consent.” (p. 190)

International conventions, whether general or particular, establishing rules expressly recognised by the contesting states

pp. 190–192Treaties

International conventions (treaties) are agreements between two or more states, and may contain binding or non-binding terms. States can make formal agreements verbally through their officials, but this is rare. Drafting, adoption and interpretation are governed by the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties 1969, which Charlesworth does not address directly. The binding nature of treaties rests on pacta sunt servanda (agreements must be observed), incorporated in article 26 of the Vienna Convention. The same principle is also the basis of contract law in many national legal systems.

Charlesworth

“Treaties, then, may be sources of obligation, but how do they become sources of law?” (p. 191)

Bilateral treaties and the jus cogens limit p. 191

Treaties “come in all shapes and sizes”. Bilateral treaties, such as free trade agreements, may be quite specific and technical. They are a source of law only in the limited sense that they bind the two parties and provide redress if one party breaches; they have no implications for the development of international law more generally.

But there are limits on what a treaty may contain. The Vienna Convention declares void treaty provisions that violate the jus cogens, principles to which the international community allows no exception (article 53). It describes such norms as those “accepted and recognised by the international community of States as a whole as a norm from which no derogation is permitted and which can be modified only by a subsequent norm of general international law having the same character”.

  • The text sits in the normative tradition of international law, giving the “international community” a central role in defining fundamental values that limit individual states.
  • The content of jus cogens is contested, with various lists offered Restatement (Third) of US Foreign Relations Law (1990), §102 Brownlie 2008. There is wide support for including the prohibitions of slavery, genocide and racial discrimination.
  • Some jurists challenge the very existence of jus cogens, or “superlaw”, arguing that it disrupts the unity of traditional international law, which did not distinguish between types of legal principles Weil 1983.

Multilateral treaties that bind beyond their parties pp. 191–192

Multilateral treaties drafted by international institutions often have a more obviously legal character than bilateral ones, since they set out broad statements of principle to be applied in many contexts.

  • Codifying treaties restate customary rules and so have a status beyond mere contract: the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations 1961 and indeed the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties.
  • Fundamental-principle treaties, such as human rights treaties, attain special status by defining fundamental normative principles and attracting significant participation. The UN human rights treaties bind the states that have formally accepted them, but can also have a legal effect on non-parties.

Depending on its language, the moral force of the normative order it prescribes, and its global level of acceptance, a treaty provision may become legally binding generally, as custom, and govern even states that never accepted it.

Charlesworth

“This is anathema to those who insist that state consent is the critical element of international law-making” Weil 1983 (p. 192).

Fragmentation p. 192

Modern multilateral treaty-makers in particular areas tend to develop specialised concepts and techniques. The International Law Commission calls this the fragmentation of international law and regards it as problematic because it creates potential conflicts between treaty regimes: trade law principles may clash with environmental treaty rules, and human rights norms may be inconsistent with the law of armed conflict. The ILC recommends a principle of harmonisation: interpreting treaty obligations on a subject, as far as possible, to be compatible with each other.

MOUs and soft arrangements p. 192

Another modern feature is the growth of memoranda of understanding (MOUs) and other soft law arrangements not intended as treaties. They can still produce legal effects: later agreements or understandings can affect how a treaty is interpreted, so soft law can modify apparently hard law. Drafters often find it easier to adopt specific and precise terms in non-binding agreements, and such instruments bypass national treaty-ratification processes Boyle & Chinkin 2007.

Vs Shaw

Shaw also accepts that treaty provisions can generate custom binding non-parties, but only if “fundamentally norm-creating” and backed by practice and opinio juris, and he rejects D'Amato's shortcut as “going too far”. Charlesworth presents the same possibility through human rights treaties and the moral force of their normative order, and frames resistance to it as a consent-based position (Weil).

International custom as evidence of a general practice accepted as law

pp. 192–195Practice, opinio juris, the paradox, persistent objectors

“International custom” has intrigued jurists. It is sometimes likened to the common law developed by judges in Anglo-American systems, but the analogy is imperfect: customary international law is created primarily through evidence of state practice and state belief, not as the product of a legal or moral principle or of judges making law case by case.

Charlesworth

“So international custom in its traditional form gives priority to state consent as the source of the law over normative concepts.” (p. 192)

Standard definitions give custom two elements: state practice and opinio juris sive necessitatis.

State practice p. 193

Practice is established by states' actions. The evidence can come from many sources but must show consistent and uniform state practice over time.

  • Narrow view D'Amato 1971: practice should be limited to physical actions, such as withdrawing an ambassador, monitoring a border or a military strike. The advantage is ease of proof.
  • Broad view (more widely held) Brownlie 2008: practice includes national laws, claims, correspondence, statements and other positions attributable to a state, and also omissions or failures to act.
  • High levels of treaty participation can evidence practice of a rule, and it is even more significant if non-parties behave in accordance with the treaty's terms.

International courts and tribunals have taken widely divergent approaches to what practice is adequate:

  • North Sea Continental Shelf ICJ 1969: the ICJ rejected evidence of states' use of the equidistance principle in maritime boundary delimitation as numerically insignificant.
  • Other factors are the uniformity and duration of practice, which have an inverse relationship: the shorter the record of a practice, the greater the expectation of consistency, and vice versa.
  • Yet in Nicaragua the Court was prepared to identify a customary rule “on the basis of slender and equivocal state practice” (p. 193).

Opinio juris p. 193

Opinio juris is the sense held by states that they are conforming to a legal obligation. That makes it subtler and harder to establish than practice, since it needs evidence that a state acted because it considered the action legally required. How can we know what a state was thinking? Three answers:

  • Presumption. Hersch Lauterpacht suggested presuming that all uniform conduct of governments evidences opinio juris unless the contrary is proved Lauterpacht 1958, cited by Judge Sørensen dissenting in North Sea.
  • “Objective” test. Others (D'Amato 1971) require the articulation of a claim of international legality in advance of, or at the same time as, the relevant practice.
  • Diffuse consensus. More recent accounts treat opinio juris as largely independent of individual state belief, depending instead on a diffuse consensus about what is legally required Henckaerts 2005.

When the two elements merge p. 194

“To add to the confusion”, the two elements are often merged in particular cases.

  • Nicaragua ICJ 1986: the Court had to decide whether a customary prohibition on the use of force made the US mining of Nicaraguan harbours illegal. It accepted states' votes and statements in the UN General Assembly condemning the use of force as both state practice and opinio juris supporting a customary rule against force.
  • Texaco arbitration 1978: the arbitrator treated the General Assembly's 1962 Declaration on Permanent Sovereignty over Natural Resources as evidence of customary international law. A resolution's legal character depended on its type, form and the conditions under which it was adopted.

So soft law can influence the development of customary principles. It has been suggested that this is typical of modern approaches to custom, which start from general statements of rules, whereas traditional approaches focused on specific instances of practice Roberts 2001.

The custom paradox and Tasioulas's answer pp. 194–195

Opinio juris creates an apparent paradox: it seems to require that states believe (mistakenly) that something is already law before it can become law, which makes the definition circular. How should we understand state actions taken speculatively, without a sense of legal compulsion?

John Tasioulas proposes a resolution drawing on ethical reasoning. He distinguishes two cases:

  • Creating or revising custom: custom requires general state practice that can be ethically justified and in fact receives ethical endorsement by states.
  • Continuing an existing custom: the custom keeps its legitimacy if it can be ethically justified.

The ethical, for Tasioulas, is “the domain of reasons that bear on an agent that derive from proper regard for human interests, both his own and, especially, those of others” Tasioulas 2007. He claims his approach “solves the problem of transparency [in the creation of international custom] without implausibly attributing overly complex attitudes to states”.

Charlesworth

The concept of “proper regard for human interests” “inserts a highly contestable element that may destabilise the solution” (p. 195).

Vs Shaw

Shaw answers the same paradox with a process view: states act believing their conduct “is law or is becoming law”, and other states' reactions accept or reject it. Charlesworth instead reports Tasioulas's ethical solution and doubts it. Both cite Lauterpacht's presumption and D'Amato's articulation test.

Who custom binds: regional custom and persistent objectors p. 195

Unlike a treaty, customary international law is understood to bind all states, new and old, whether or not they took part in creating it. But the ICJ has accepted regional customary law applying in a limited geographic area, for example rights of passage through another state's territory built up over centuries of practice: Rights of Passage over Indian Territory (Portugal v India) ICJ 1960.

The voluntary, or apologetic, nature of international law shows in the persistent objector idea. “Persistently objecting to a rule will not get you far in a national legal system”, but international law lets a state opt out of customary rules while they are developing, through public objections over a long period.

  • Fisheries (United Kingdom v Norway) ICJ 1951: in the early 1950s Norway argued that it had resisted the development of rules for measuring its territorial sea that would disadvantage it. Its objection had run over the preceding century, through decrees and refusals to join treaty regimes that precluded its claims, and it successfully defended itself against the UK's claim to reduce Norway's sovereignty “over the North Sea”. Not in Charlesworth The dispute concerned the baselines of Norway's coastal waters (see Shaw).
  • Persistent objection is most powerful when other states do not themselves protest against the objection.
Charlesworth

The principle's effectiveness and coherence have been challenged, and “its dwindling status today reflects the vitality of the utopian tradition in international law” Charney 1993. James Crawford (in chapter 5 of the same volume) notes that, empirically, persistent objection to a rule widely supported by the international community is very likely to fail. Attempts to articulate a “subsequent objector” principle, from Third World jurists and from US scholars, have had little impact so far (p. 195).

The general principles of law recognised by civilised nations

pp. 195–197A drafting compromise, then a rescue project

The drafters of the PCIJ Statute saw general principles as a safety net for when neither treaty nor custom supplied the rules needed to resolve a dispute.

The 1920 drafting fight p. 196

The wording was a compromise between a utopian and an apologetic approach within the Advisory Committee of Jurists that drafted the PCIJ Statute in 1920.

  • Utopian: the Belgian jurist Baron Descamps proposed letting the Court apply “the rules of international law as recognized by the legal conscience of civilized nations” as a supplement to consent-based sources such as treaties.
  • Apologetic: Elihu Root of the United States strongly opposed this as promoting “subjective conceptions of the principles of justice”. He asked whether “it was possible to compel nations to submit their disputes to a Court which would administer not merely law, but also what it deems to be the conscience of civilised peoples?” van Hoof 1983

The final wording of article 38(1)(c) removed the idea of a legal conscience and instead emphasised states and their legal systems in identifying principles. The reference to “civilised nations” reflects the era, just after the adoption of the Covenant of the League of Nations. Proponents had in mind principles found in many national legal systems, such as good faith and res judicata (the finality of judgments). So the category kept a consensual basis: states accept the principles in their own legal systems.

Open questions, and seldom used p. 196

  • Are general principles a compilation of rules common to national legal systems, or a category of international rules to which all right-thinking states would subscribe?
  • How many states must recognise a rule for it to qualify?

Despite, or perhaps because of, these debates, the category is seldom invoked explicitly in international legal argument.

Rehabilitating general principles pp. 196–197

Some jurists want to revive general principles as a way around the technical difficulties of proving customary law:

  • Oscar Schachter Schachter 1991: national-law general principles are ripe for recruitment into international law, particularly for individual rights, contractual remedies, liability for extra-hazardous activities and restraints on the use of common property.
  • Bruno Simma and Philip Alston Simma & Alston 1988: use general principles as a “modern” source of human rights law, derived particularly from consensus in resolutions of international organisations. This keeps international law's traditional consensual basis while avoiding the complexities of custom.
  • The development of global administrative law is an example of national general principles being treated as absorbed into the international legal order.
Charlesworth

This renewed interest can also be understood as a way of finding a recognised legal umbrella for the vast array of soft law instruments (p. 197).

Vs Shaw

Shaw sorts views of general principles into natural law, positivist and middle positions and piles up cases (Chorzów, res judicata, estoppel, good faith). Charlesworth gives the drafting history (Descamps against Root), reads “civilised nations” as a product of its era, and notes how rarely the category is used.

Judicial decisions and the teachings of the most highly qualified publicists of the various nations

pp. 197–198Subsidiary on paper, central in practice

Judicial decisions p. 197

The last two sources are designated subsidiary. Judicial decisions are subject to article 59: “[t]he decision of the Court has no binding force except between the parties and in respect of that particular case”. That gives ICJ decisions a lower status than the decisions of final courts in many legal systems. The drafting history shows the aim was to stop international judges regarding themselves as quasi-legislators.

Charlesworth

But the ICJ constantly refers to its previous decisions, and national and international judicial opinions are scrutinised and used in all international law-making fora “to a much greater degree than suggested in article 38(1)(d)” (p. 197).

The weight a judicial opinion carries depends on the status and independence of the court, the availability of the written and oral pleadings, and the quality and detail of the reasoning Boyle & Chinkin 2007.

Writers pp. 197–198

Writers such as Suárez and Grotius influenced, and indeed defined, the early development of international law. Scholarly writings are regularly cited in legal argument today, but the ICJ itself has been wary of endorsing particular jurists, perhaps because in the intensely political context of international disputes the nationality of scholars may be assumed to colour their views. An English court raised another objection in 1905:

In many instances the ... pronouncements [of international legal scholars] must be regarded ... as the embodiments of their views as to what ought to be, from an ethical standpoint, the conduct of nations inter se, [rather] than the enunciation of a rule or practice so universally approved or assented to as to be fairly termed, even in the qualified sense in which the word can be understood in reference to the relations between independent political communities, ‘law’.

West Rand Central Gold Mining Co. v The King [1905] 2 KB 391, 402, quoted at pp. 197–198

The sources of soft law

pp. 198–199Not a source, but it blurs them all

What soft law is p. 198

Soft law has attracted increasing attention over the last twenty-five years. The traditional sources were framed in 1922 in terms of the actions of individual states; soft law, “a creature of the UN Charter era”, is a product of multilateral processes, institutions and even individuals operating internationally.

  • Who makes it: the International Law Commission, treaty-interpreting bodies such as the Human Rights Committee, and Special Rapporteurs appointed by the Human Rights Council.
  • What it covers: its binding character may be debated, but it often addresses issues of almost universal agreement, such as sustainable development or human rights, and can provide powerful justifications for action.
Charlesworth

“Soft law is not a source of law in the sense of article 38(1) of the ICJ Statute, but rather a category of principles, articulated through instruments or documents not binding as such, whose status is more contested and negotiable than those of hard legal norms.” (p. 198)

  • Soft law principles may sometimes be “probationary” candidates for eventual recognition as law Besson 2010, though not always.
  • State consent still matters, but it is filtered through institutions and entities that do not necessarily echo the interests of particular states or groups of states.
  • In essence the sources of soft law are no different from those of hard law, but the idea of soft law stretches the familiar groupings and may ultimately collapse them. Besson: soft law instruments show “an increasing convergence in terms of law-making procedures and law-makers amongst the different sources of international law”.

Resolutions of international organisations pp. 198–199

  • Resolutions differ in legal effect. Under article 25 of the UN Charter, Security Council resolutions made under Chapter VII are binding on all UN members.
  • General Assembly resolutions do not bind states as such, but may be legally significant as state practice, evidence of opinio juris, or both. That depends on the nature of the resolution and its voting pattern: a resolution with a polarised vote or many abstentions may have no legal implications.
  • Hersch Lauterpacht made a “stern riposte” to claims that the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) could accrue legal authority. Almost all states had stressed in the debates that it was not legally binding, and he rejected arguments that it could have indirect legal effect as incongruous and inconsistent with its wording Lauterpacht 1950.

Blur, not replacement p. 199

Modern law-making still invokes the four Article 38 categories as the primary sources and understands soft law in relation to them, not as a separate source.

Charlesworth

“Soft law thus interacts with and blurs the boundaries of the traditional sources of international law, but it does not replace them.” (p. 199)

The debate over soft law:

  • Redundancy. Courts and tribunals tend to recast soft law principles in Article 38(1) language, which has led some scholars to dispute whether the category makes sense Klabbers 1996.
  • Impatient idealism. Anthony D'Amato attacks soft law as a vehicle used by impatient idealists to push international law beyond a consensual order into human rights and the environment, laments the detachment of sources from state behaviour, and dismisses soft law as “a head without a body” D'Amato 2009.
  • Democracy deficit. If law can be made softly and informally at the international level, it bypasses the usual national processes of acceptance.
  • Democratisation. Soft law can be read the other way, as a democratisation of international law-making. It is more inclusive than hard law, emerging not just from state interests but from multilateral fora involving international organisations, NGOs and individuals. That increases democratic markers such as equality and deliberation, and brings international law-making closer to the national sphere Boyle & Chinkin 2007 Besson 2010.
Vs Shaw

Shaw: “‘soft law’ is not law. That needs to be emphasised.” It matters only when converted through a treaty or custom. Charlesworth agrees it is not an Article 38 source, but stresses that it modifies hard law, feeds custom, may collapse the categories, and raises questions of democratic legitimacy.

Conclusion

p. 200From formal sources to values

To a domestic lawyer used to a recognised hierarchy of legal sources, the sources of international law seem “disconcertingly negotiable”. Domestic legal systems typically draw legitimacy from the implicit consent of the governed, expressed through legislatures and sometimes restrained by higher constitutional norms. In international law the consent of the legal subject has been far more explicit, which makes international law look unstable and manipulable.

Charlesworth

The contrast between international and domestic legal systems is often exaggerated. It ignores the indeterminacy of the principles of any legal order and the porosity of their implementation (p. 200).

  • Less state consent. Since the founding of the UN, the prominence of individual state consent in law-making has been reduced. International organisations, NGOs and individuals now play a significant part, bringing the makers closer to the subjects of international law Besson 2010.
  • New ideas of legitimacy. “We have moved from an obsession with formal sources as the marker of legitimacy in international law to one with values.” Prosper Weil's cri de coeur against the “relative normativity” produced by a retreat from state consent Weil 1983 “has now become a whisper”. There are even calls to recognise ethical principles, or universal norms that stop states quarantining themselves from principles vital to human survival Charney 1993.
  • Still torn. But most arguments about the content of international law still contain contradictory elements of normative aspiration and assertions of state freedom of action and the priority of state consent.

A focus on sources of law comforts international lawyers that they are part of a truly legal system and that they are engaging in a technical, rather than political, craft. The international legal doctrines about sources, however, are selective and partial. They embody unease about whether international law should reflect the behaviour of states or a system of deeply human values.

The argument in ten lines

  1. Anxiety about whether international law is “law” makes lawyers fixate on sources, as if they supplied certainty and authority.
  2. International law mixes state consent and abstract values (humanity, fairness, communitarian values); the mix is volatile.
  3. Iraq 2003: both sides argued sources (SC resolutions, pre-emptive self-defence, art. 2(4), Chapter VII); sources talk masked politics yet disciplined argument (Koskenniemi's “formalist device”).
  4. “Sources ... are inflected by the identities of those who have the power to designate them”: the TWAIL critique (custom from colonial powers' practice; GA resolutions) and the feminist critique (no women in norm-making; narrow categories).
  5. Article 38(1) (first in the 1922 PCIJ Statute) is canonical but a “pithy mantra”, a “quasi-scientific formula” that postpones politics; Boyle and Chinkin call it “dated and increasingly misleading”. Article 38(2) ex aequo et bono has never been used.
  6. No formal ranking of treaties, custom and general principles: they are hard to identify to different degrees but can make norms of comparable weight; jus cogens is a hierarchy of norms, not sources.
  7. Treaties: pacta sunt servanda (VCLT art. 26); bilateral treaties bind only parties; art. 53 voids treaties against jus cogens (slavery, genocide, racial discrimination; Weil calls it “superlaw”); human rights treaties can bind non-parties as custom; fragmentation and harmonisation; MOUs can modify hard law.
  8. Custom: practice (D'Amato narrow, Brownlie broad) + opinio juris (Lauterpacht's presumption, D'Amato's articulation, Henckaerts's diffuse consensus); the elements merge (Nicaragua, Texaco); the paradox, and Tasioulas's ethical fix, which Charlesworth finds “highly contestable”; the persistent objector (Fisheries) is dwindling.
  9. General principles came from the 1920 compromise between Descamps (legal conscience) and Root (subjective justice). They are rarely used, but Schachter, Simma and Alston, and global administrative law try to revive them. Courts and writers are subsidiary under art. 59 but used far more than art. 38(1)(d) implies (West Rand, 1905).
  10. Soft law is not an Article 38 source, but it blurs and may collapse the categories. D'Amato calls it “a head without a body”; others call it democratisation. Legitimacy has moved “from an obsession with formal sources ... to one with values”; sources doctrine is “selective and partial”.

For INT301: the course lens

What is international law, and who makes it?
A volatile mix of state consent and abstract values, generated by a multi-layered process of interactions, instruments, pressures and principles. States remain central, but international organisations, treaty bodies, Special Rapporteurs, NGOs and individuals increasingly make it.
How does power shape it?
Directly through who designates sources: customary rules built from colonial powers' practice, women absent from norm-generating institutions, Article 38(1) masking politics, Iraq 2003 legality talk covering political choices. Where Shaw treats power's weight in custom as a fact, Charlesworth treats it as bias to be exposed.
Which approach?
Critical and feminist, drawing on Koskenniemi's critical legal studies (apology/utopia) and citing TWAIL. Here she writes as a critical surveyor, not a doctrinal textbook author.

Retrieval practice

Self-test

Answer before revealing. Miss one? Reread that section.

Q1 · Intro
Why, according to Charlesworth, do introductory works obsess over sources?

Anxiety about whether international law is really law: “as if pinning down the well-springs of international law will provide certainty and authority for the discipline”.

Q2 · Intro
What two things is modern international law a product of, and why is that a problem?

State behaviour and agreement, and abstract values (humanity, fairness, communitarian values). The mix is “volatile” and, with weaker enforcement, makes it look more negotiable than domestic law.

Q3 · Iraq
How does the 2003 Iraq invasion illustrate her argument?

Both sides argued sources (SC resolutions, pre-emptive self-defence, the moral case against dictators; versus art. 2(4) and Chapter VII). Politicians felt they had to argue legality while seeing the rules as malleable. Sources talk masked politics but still allowed arguments to be weighed: a formalist device (Koskenniemi).

Q4 · Power
Complete and explain: “Sources of international law are inflected by ...”

“... the identities of those who have the power to designate them.” TWAIL: custom from colonial powers' practice; push for GA resolutions. Feminists: no women in norm-generating institutions; narrow categories exclude women's experience.

Q5 · Article 38
Why does Article 38(1) hold such sway, and what does it hide?

It lets lawyers sidestep debates about international law's function and about consent versus justice: “a pithy mantra ... quasi-scientific formula”. It hides the “multi-layered process of interactions, instruments, pressures and principles” and differences across specialised fields.

Q6 · Article 38
When was the Article 38 list first endorsed, and what is article 38(2)?

In the 1922 PCIJ Statute. Art. 38(2): deciding ex aequo et bono (equity and justice, without law) if the parties agree; never used.

Q7 · Soft law
List the kinds of instrument Charlesworth counts as soft law.

Resolutions of international institutions; declarations of high-level meetings; statements of treaty-monitoring bodies; voluntary codes of conduct; treaty provisions without commitments to action.

Q8 · Hierarchy
Is there a hierarchy of sources?

Only decisions and writings are ranked (subsidiary). In practice treaties win on certainty; custom is harder to prove and general principles obscure. But in theory all three can make norms of comparable weight, and jus cogens is a hierarchy of norms, not sources (Besson on pluralism).

Q9 · Theory
How does Koskenniemi's apology/utopia appear in the sources?

As the tension between state consent (political reality, efficacy) and abstract principles (justice, fairness). Institutions' growing role has boosted values and reduced individual state consent.

Q10 · Treaties
“Treaties may be sources of obligation, but how do they become sources of law?” Give her answer.

Bilateral treaties bind only parties. Multilateral treaties can codify custom (VCDR 1961, VCLT) or define fundamental principles (UN human rights treaties) and so affect non-parties, becoming binding generally as custom depending on language, moral force and acceptance. That is anathema to consent purists (Weil).

Q11 · Jus cogens
What does she say about jus cogens?

VCLT art. 53 voids conflicting treaty provisions; it reflects the normative tradition (the “international community” defines values); content contested (Restatement §102, Brownlie), with wide support for slavery, genocide and racial discrimination; Weil attacks it as “superlaw”.

Q12 · Treaties
What are fragmentation and harmonisation?

ILC: specialised treaty regimes (trade vs environment, human rights vs armed conflict) may conflict; harmonisation means interpreting obligations on a subject to be compatible.

Q13 · Treaties
How can soft law modify hard law?

Later agreements or understandings (MOUs) can change how a treaty is interpreted. Non-binding agreements also allow more precise terms and bypass national ratification (Boyle and Chinkin).

Q14 · Custom
Why is the common-law analogy for custom imperfect?

Custom comes from evidence of state practice and belief, not from legal or moral principle or case-by-case judicial law-making, so traditionally it privileges state consent over normative concepts.

Q15 · Practice
Narrow vs broad state practice: who holds which?

D'Amato: only physical acts (withdrawing an ambassador, border monitoring, military strikes), easy to prove. Brownlie (more widely held): laws, claims, correspondence, statements, and omissions. Treaty participation, and especially non-parties' conformity, also counts.

Q16 · Courts
Show that courts are inconsistent about practice.

North Sea (1969) rejected equidistance practice as numerically insignificant; Nicaragua found custom on “slender and equivocal” practice. Uniformity and duration trade off inversely.

Q17 · Opinio juris
Give three approaches to proving opinio juris.

H. Lauterpacht's presumption from uniform conduct (cited by Judge Sørensen); D'Amato's articulation of a legal claim before or with practice; recent diffuse-consensus accounts (Henckaerts).

Q18 · Merging
How did Nicaragua and Texaco merge the two elements?

Nicaragua: GA votes and statements against force counted as both practice and opinio juris. Texaco (1978): the 1962 Permanent Sovereignty Declaration evidenced custom, depending on its type, form and conditions of adoption. So soft law feeds custom (Roberts: modern custom starts from general statements).

Q19 · Paradox
What is the custom paradox and Tasioulas's solution?

States must seemingly believe something is law before it is (circular). Tasioulas: new or revised custom needs practice that is ethically justifiable and ethically endorsed by states; continuing custom needs ethical justification. Charlesworth: “proper regard for human interests” is highly contestable.

Q20 · Objector
What does the persistent objector show, and what is its status?

International law's voluntary, apologetic side: opting out while a rule develops (Norway, Fisheries 1951). Now dwindling, reflecting the utopian tradition (Charney); very likely to fail against widely supported rules (Crawford); the subsequent objector idea had little impact.

Q21 · Principles
Who fought over article 38(1)(c) in 1920, and over what?

Descamps (Belgium) wanted rules recognised by “the legal conscience of civilized nations” (utopian); Root (US) objected to “subjective conceptions of the principles of justice” (apologetic). The final text anchors principles in states' legal systems (good faith, res judicata), keeping a consensual basis.

Q22 · Principles
Who wants to revive general principles, and why?

To avoid custom's difficulties: Schachter (rights, remedies, extra-hazardous liability, common property); Simma and Alston (human rights from IO resolutions); global administrative law. Also an umbrella for soft law.

Q23 · Courts & writers
How far do judicial decisions and writers really matter?

Art. 59 limits ICJ decisions (to stop judges acting as quasi-legislators), but decisions are used far more than art. 38(1)(d) suggests; weight depends on the court's independence, the pleadings and the reasoning. Writers (Suárez, Grotius) once defined the field; the ICJ is wary of naming them; West Rand (1905) called their views statements of what ought to be.

Q24 · Soft law
What is soft law's relation to the Article 38 sources?

Not an Article 38 source but principles in non-binding instruments, with contested status; sometimes “probationary” (Besson). It interacts with and blurs the sources but does not replace them. Klabbers: redundant. D'Amato: “a head without a body”. Others: democratisation, or a bypass of national democracy.

Q25 · Resolutions
When do resolutions of international organisations have legal effect?

SC Chapter VII resolutions bind (art. 25). GA resolutions can be practice and/or opinio juris depending on nature and voting (polarised or abstention-heavy votes may count for nothing). H. Lauterpacht denied the UDHR legal force.

Q26 · Conclusion
What are her concluding claims?

Sources look “disconcertingly negotiable” but the domestic contrast is exaggerated (all law is indeterminate); state consent has declined since the UN; legitimacy has moved from formal sources to values (Weil now “a whisper”); arguments still mix aspiration and consent; sources doctrines are “selective and partial”.
