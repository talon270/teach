The three weeks on one table

Week 4 asks whether international law’s central rule (no force, no conquest) and one of its strongest protections (against genocide) hold up against power. Week 5 looks inside the institution that declares what the law is, the ICJ, and finds judges using discretion. Week 6 supplies the theories that explain both: realism (law is what decision-makers do, and power matters) and positivism (law is made up of valid norms traced to sources and kept separate from morality).

§ 1

Week 4

International law and the use of force

Hathaway & Shapiro, “Might Unmakes Right”

Oona A. Hathaway and Scott J. Shapiro (Yale Law School), “Might Unmakes Right: The Catastrophic Collapse of Norms Against the Use of Force”, Foreign Affairs, July/August 2025, pp. 80–93. An essay; the authors also wrote The Internationalists. Full notes →

In one breath

Before 1928 war was a legal way to enforce rights and conquest was lawful: “Might made right.” The Kellogg–Briand Pact (1928) outlawed war, and the UN Charter (1945) built institutions around the ban. For eight decades conquest became rare, the number of states tripled and trade boomed, with economic outcasting replacing war as the way to enforce the law and the US acting as its imperfect enforcer. Trump now threatens Greenland, the Panama Canal, Canada and Gaza and pressures Ukraine to cede land, reviving the pre-1928 logic and breaking the shared expectations that held the system up. Their remedy is to admit the system’s flaws (the P5 veto, US impunity over Iraq) and let middle and small states, the General Assembly and regional coalitions enforce the ban themselves, against the US if need be.

The argument in order

  1. Opening pp. 80–82
    • Trump’s threats to seize Greenland and the Panama Canal, to take Gaza after expelling two million Palestinians, and to make Ukraine cede land are “a cohesive assault” on the ban on force.
    • The norm had already eroded: the US in Iraq (2003, on WMD claims that were false), Chinese bases in the South China Sea, Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine (2022). But Trump is shredding what is left of the norm against using force.
    • The order rested on “a shared set of expectations” more than on total compliance. Others are already adjusting: Netanyahu backed the Gaza idea, Panama accepted deportation flights and US troops along the Canal, and Kyiv signed a minerals deal.
  2. “Entrenched Warfare” pp. 82–84
    • War “was the order”. Conquest, gunboat diplomacy, immunity for leaders and duties of neutrality are set out in the table above. The result was constant conflict, empires growing by force, and colonial possessions … won and lost like damages in a lawsuit.
  3. “From War to Peace” pp. 84–88
    • World War I: more than 20 countries fought and about 20 million died, half of them civilians. The League (1920) was hobbled when the US Senate kept the US out.
    • Kellogg proposed a treaty to Briand in late 1927. The Pact (1928), formally the General Treaty for Renunciation of War as an Instrument of National Policy, gained 58 signatories. Parties “condemn recourse to war … and renounce it, as an instrument of national policy” and settle disputes “by pacific means”.
    • The Pact was mocked for failing to stop WWII, but it “set in motion” the modern order: the Stimson Doctrine of nonrecognition, Nuremberg’s first count, and Jackson’s new law of neutrality.
    • The UN codified it. Afterwards: more than 150 successful conquests in the century before WWII against fewer than 10 since; territory conquered per year fell below 6% of its old rate; the number of states tripled; great powers have not openly fought one another since 1945.
    • Alternative explanations (nuclear deterrence, democracy, trade) “fail to account” for this. On Iraq–Kuwait (1990) the Security Council authorised “all necessary means” and states learned that violations have consequences.
    • Wealth now comes from trade, not conquest: trade grew from about 10% of world GDP in 1945 to 58% in 2023, alongside tens of thousands of IOs and more than 250,000 treaties. The dollar gave the US great power to enforce the rules. It was imperfect (Vietnam, Iraq 2003 and counterterrorism relied on “overly broad claims of self-defense”) but never broke the ban on conquest, and it led on Kuwait even without a treaty duty.
  4. “Legal Jeopardy” pp. 88–90
    • Trump’s approach is “not mere realism or a new form of transactional politics”; it is a throwback to the time before Kellogg–Briand.
    • He warned Zelensky he risked “losing [his] whole country” over a plan that could cede 20% of Ukraine (per the FT). Military threats helped extract concessions from Canada and Mexico.
    • Tariffs of 25% imposed “on a whim” blunt sanctions as a tool for enforcing law. An executive order threatening to sanction ICC judges and lawyers turned the tool against the law. Isolationism weakens outcasting.
    • Threats alone erode expectations, and the system will fall piece by piece until it collapses altogether.
  5. “The Right Fight” pp. 90–93
    • The risk is spheres of influence (Putin, Trump, Xi) and Thucydides’ world where the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must.
    • The alternative: 142 states joined the US in the 2022 UNGA vote condemning Russia’s attempted annexation. Europe rallied (Starmer’s “coalition of the willing”, von der Leyen’s plan) but cannot be the world’s policeman.
    • They admit the flaws: the P5 veto, and a US that went unpunished for Iraq in 2003. These delegitimized the order “particularly in the eyes of states in the global South.”
    • Their proposals: coalitions of middle and small states (the 27-member EU as a model); a stronger General Assembly (193 equal votes) through the veto initiative; the Council of Europe’s aggression court; the Hague Group (Bolivia, Colombia, Cuba, Honduras, Malaysia, Namibia, Senegal, South Africa) enforcing ICJ and ICC decisions on Gaza; an AU–EU partnership; and an “outcasting council” with automatic joint sanctions, which could be used against the US if it seized the Canal.
    • Doubts remain: will France, Germany and the UK share power, and will excluded states trust the order? Developing states that play the great powers off risk becoming “the spoils of great-power rivalries”. The system “is not self-sustaining”, and leaders must act before Trump “rewinds the clock.”

Kourtis, “A Racialized Existence”

Dimitrios A. Kourtis, “A Racialized Existence: Lessons from Palestine and the Genocide Convention”, ch. 7 in Mohsen al Attar and Claire Smith (eds), Emancipating International Law (OUP 2026), pp. 140–168. Full notes →

In one breath

The Genocide Convention (1948) protects national, ethnical, racial and religious groups, but no one has worked out what makes a group racial. The drafters used “race” loosely, mixing biology, nation and ethnicity, and each of the three current readings fails: visible hereditary traits, the perpetrator’s view, and the 1940s meaning (Schabas). Kourtis argues that race is a dynamic, relational social construct. A racial group is one whose members are perceived, and perceive themselves, as having a distinct identity that is hybrid, situational, fragmentary and negotiable. Applied to Palestinians, this reveals a racial contract written into international law (Balfour, the Mandate, UNSCOP, Partition) and Israeli law (the Law of Return, the Absentees’ Property Law, the Nation-State Law). Decades of this racialization were genocidal priming, and the 7 October 2023 attacks became the activation event after which officials framed all Palestinians as an existential threat. Apartheid, he concludes, has been a prelude to genocide.

The sequence Kourtis builds

2 · pp. 152–60Racialized subjectBuilt by law: land seizure, denial of entry, de facto denationalization.

4 · pp. 163–66Activation“Architects” use a trigger event to drop legal and moral constraints.

The argument in order

  1. 1. Introduction pp. 140–42
    • Little work exists on the protected groups, who are the holders of a collective “right of existence”. Should the groups be read subjectively or objectively? Race is scientifically discredited, yet Art. II protects racial groups.
    • Hypothesis: the four groups are “dynamic and relational social constructs”. ICTR Akayesu (1998) protected the Tutsi as “permanent and stable” (by an ejusdem generis formula) without calling them ethnic or racial, yet conceded that everyone, including their killers, saw them as a distinct ethnic group.
    • Caveat: he does not analyse Israel’s apartheid regime in detail but acknowledges it.
  2. 2. The multiple meanings of race pp. 143–49
    • Lemkin (Axis Rule, 1944) built “genocide” on the Greek genos (“race” or “tribe”) and read Nazism as a racial masterplan. Race “de-politicized and essentialized” the debate. Racial groups went in with “practically no resistance”, while the drafters kept out political groups, liberation movements and colonized peoples.
    • World Jewish Congress petitions to ECOSOC (late 1940s) used genocide claims about Palestine “to conceal the true political and colonial character of the conflict”.
    • In the travaux, Norway and Haiti took race as biological (the Haitian delegate spoke of “intermingling”), and the UK said no one should be persecuted for “the accident of his birth”. Egypt saw “no great difference” between racial and ethnic, Belgium called them identical, Iran equated racial with national, and Russia spoke of “distinct, clearly determinable communities”. The interwar minorities treaties (Eden) used race culturally, as did Greece (1948) and Dean Rusk (1950).
    • A tautology: genocide began as a crime of motive, so race was both what defined the group and the reason it was targeted.
    • Biology lingers: India in Right of Passage (“of Indian race”), Judge Tanaka in South West Africa (1966) on colour and hair, the ECtHR in Timishev (2005).
    • In human rights law race is “the by-product of discrimination”; in genocide law it is “the reason for recognizing the group’s collective right to exist” p. 148. ICERD Art. 1(1) defines discrimination broadly, and CERD treats membership as self-identification. Under the Apartheid Convention (1973) the oppressor’s perception defines the group.
    • Three trends: (1) objective, visible hereditary traits (the Proxmire Act, Akayesu); (2) subjective, the perpetrator’s ascription; (3) historical, the 1940s meaning (Schabas).
  3. 3. A critique of the existing interpretations pp. 149–51
    • The “visible minority” reading cannot explain how skin colour alone creates solidarity. Pure subjectivism fails because Right-holders cannot be hypothesized.
    • Schabas’s view has the most historical merit but rests on three assumptions: (primo) the referent was fixed; (secundo) every delegate meant the same thing; (tertio) the meaning is frozen at the time of drafting.
    • Instead, generic terms in long-lasting treaties evolve: Namibia AO (1971) para 53, Aegean Sea (1978), and Costa Rica v Nicaragua (2009) para 66. So “racial” takes today’s meaning.
  4. 4. The racialized subject pp. 151–60
    • Race is “visibility and interaction”, a “normative phantasm” (Butler), produced by a racial contract (Mills), with local versions at the edges of Europe.
    • In Palestine, the Balfour Declaration (1917) and the Mandate (1922) erased Arab Palestinians as “existing non-Jewish communities”. UNSCOP (1947) and Partition (Res. 181) continued the erasure.
    • In Israeli law: the Declaration of Independence (1948); Supreme Court rulings (1972, Ornan 2013) that the only nationality is Jewish; the Law of Return (1950) and Citizenship Law (1952), whose “return” route is for Jews only, making Palestinians “citizen strangers”; the Nation-State Basic Law (2018), under which only the Jewish people hold self-determination; and the Absentees’ Property Law (1950) with its four confiscation grounds and its “present-absentees”.
    • Further: a “law of the enemy” (Penal Law art. 91), a bar on family reunification since 2003, and Goldberg’s “racial Palestinianization”. The Palestinian National Covenant (1968, art. 4) and the 2007 election decree show racialization shaping Palestinian self-definition too. UNGA Res. 194(III) (return) is subordinated to raison d’état, and the Israel Lands Basic Law (1960) makes land res extra commercium. Settlers fall under civil law, Palestinians under military law.
    • Race is performative (the “Palestinian qua terrorist”, Du Bois’s “double-consciousness”), situational (Mizrahi Jews won an “entry ticket into Israeliness”, per Shenhav), hybrid (Knesset Basic Law art. 7A, 2008) and negotiable (“good Arabs” vs “bad Arabs”; the 2011 budget law penalising Nakba memory, a form of “forced forgetting”). Racial definitions are about power as much as belonging. p. 159
    • Definition: a racial group is “a human figuration whose members are perceived and perceive themselves as having some different characteristics ascribing to them a distinct identity, the latter being hybrid, situational, fragmentary, and negotiable.” p. 160
  5. 5. A racialized existence: genocidal priming and activation pp. 160–67
    • Earlier genocide claims: Syria’s rejected amendment to Art. II (forcing people to abandon their homes, 1948), and UNGA Res. 37/123D (1982) calling Sabra and Shatila genocide.
    • Hinton distinguishes essentializing from annihilating othering. A “trigger event” is really a series of events. Priming examples: the Nakba, the 1960s–80s, the Gaza blockade, Cast Lead (2008–09).
    • Activation: 7 October 2023. The primers were settler-colonialism, Gaza’s isolation, normalized force, impunity and demographic change. Existential rhetoric came from Eiland, Strook, Netanyahu (“civilization” vs “barbarism”) and even judge ad hoc Barak at the ICJ (“existential threat”, “second war of independence”). Gallant “removed all restraints”, and phrases like “children of Amalek” circulated. Anderson’s “humanity gap”.
    • Apartheid and genocide: apartheid’s special intent is to maintain an oppressive regime, genocide’s dolus specialis is to destroy. But apartheid can be a prelude (compare Rwanda). Said: the other “is not quite as human as we”.
  6. 6. Conclusion pp. 167–68
    • Palestinians fit the Convention’s criteria. The ICJ’s provisional measures order in South Africa v Israel (26 Jan 2024) relied on Israeli leaders’ statements. Context changes the meaning of an act (Kirsch). One architect, the spiritus rector, can bridge subjugation and annihilation if “willing executioners” are available.

Week 5

International organisations and IL: the ICJ

Both readings are by Mohammed Bedjaoui (Algeria): a judge of the ICJ from 1982, later its President. Before that he was Algeria’s Minister of Justice (1964–70), an ambassador to France and the UN, and a member of the ILC (1965–82).

Bedjaoui 1991, “The ‘Manufacture’ of Judgments”

Mohammed Bedjaoui, “The ‘Manufacture’ of Judgments at the International Court of Justice”, Pace Yearbook of International Law 3 (1991), pp. 29–61. Translated by B. Noble, Deputy Registrar, and printed unedited. Full notes →

In one breath

A judge’s job comes down to four verbs: to read, to listen, to deliberate and to decide. Material piles up in stages: the document that institutes the case, the written pleadings, the oral hearings, and each judge’s anonymous “written note”. Then come the deliberation, a Drafting Committee’s preliminary draft, a first and second reading, an oral yes-or-no vote, and publication. The judgment is a collegiate compromise, “the highest common denominator of the Judges’ views”, which is why separate and dissenting opinions are part of it. Along the way Bedjaoui defends the Court against charges of slowness (states choose the stately pace) and of clumsy style (fifteen hands write it), and criticises the “forbidden city” of a few repeat advocates.

Points that get asked

  1. Instituting pp. 30–33
    • Since North Sea Continental Shelf (a tripartite protocol between Denmark, the FRG and the Netherlands), special agreement has been the most common way to bring a case. That makes up for the disappointing optional clause (Art. 36(2)): a case brought by special agreement cannot face a jurisdictional challenge. Heavy advisory files: Namibia (1970–71), Western Sahara (1974–75).
  2. Written proceedings pp. 33–40
    • An objection to jurisdiction suspends the merits (Rules art. 79(3)) and becomes “a case within the case”. In Nicaragua v Costa Rica (1986) the respondent accepted jurisdiction from the start, the first to do so in 28 years (fn 11).
    • A unilateral application gives an adversarial sequence. A special agreement gives parallel, simultaneous pleadings (4, optionally 6), with each side “groping in the dark”. Eight of the ten or more special-agreement cases ran to three rounds, typically six months per pleading. States like the “stately pace”, so the Court should not be blamed for slowness.
    • Barcelona Traction: 25 kg of pleadings, 60,776 pages, and the first case where only a selection of annexes was printed (still ten volumes). At least two-thirds of the bulk is annexes. Judges face “palimpsests” without being a Champollion. Maps and charts need hired cartographers or hydrographers.
    • Everything is translated into both official languages, English and French, so it is the translators more than anyone who have all the details of the case at their fingertips. Judges have no law clerks, unlike the US Supreme Court and the court in Luxembourg: the loneliness of the long-distance reader p. 40.
  3. Oral proceedings pp. 40–46
    • No judge is ever unus judex (a lone judge). The Court does not enter into dialogue with the parties. South West Africa (1962) ran to 112 sittings and 6,818 pages of records; Gulf of Maine had about 70 advocates.
    • The “forbidden city”: in a world of five billion, a handful of lawyers plead case after case. There is no bar of the Court and no qualification requirement, but states other than France, the US, UK, Italy, Belgium and Spain tend to hire foreigners, at the cost of young national talent.
    • The 1978 Rules: Art. 60 (be succinct) and Art. 61 (the Court may flag points and ask questions). The Court has never used its power to declare a point sufficiently argued. In Tunisia/Libya the geology and geomorphology proved beside the point, yet listening must “sow doubt” (skepsis). A Berlin court held that a judge is not asleep unless he snores.
  4. Notes, deliberation, drafts pp. 46–56
    • Tea at 3 pm is an informal institution. Written notes circulate under Roman numerals to keep them anonymous, but le style c’est l’homme, so it is La Précaution inutile (the useless precaution).
    • The deliberation (Resolution Art. 5) lasts 4–5 days. The Drafting Committee is elected by secret ballot and absolute majority, with two members from the judges who best reflected the majority (fn 48). The Registry keeps the two language texts aligned, drafts the “qualities” (procedural history) and checks facts.
    • Drafts are double-spaced with numbered lines and set en regard (the two languages on facing pages). At the first reading every paragraph is read aloud in both languages. If, for the sake of votes, you try to please everyone, you finally satisfy no-one and see your majority evaporate, but The closer the Court comes to unanimity, the more the world will respect its authority p. 54. A judge must still reject any compromise against their “innermost sense of justice”.
  5. Vote, judgment, publication pp. 56–61
    • Votes are oral, no abstention is allowed, and each answer is a simple “yes” or “no”, so issues may be split. Opinion writers then say whether they are separate (concurring) or dissenting. The authoritative text is usually in the President’s working language (Art. 39(2)). The judgment’s date is the date of its public reading, not of the vote.
    • Answering an American critic who “translated” a paragraph: judgments are collective, with no rapporteur or advocate-general, so the reasoning is the “highest common denominator” and the opinions are “integral with it”.
    • Records: the shortest deliberations were Anglo-Iranian Oil and Haya de la Torre (provisional measures), and more recently the Burkina Faso/Mali order of 10 Jan 1986. Corfu Channel had the longest deliberation but the shortest judgment. Nicaragua (27 June 1986) set the record on both counts: 1,100 pages.
    • A Judgment has to be eaten hot, hence mimeographed copies (the official copies carry ribbon and a red wax seal). A pending case is itself an injustice, and leaks are a risk. A deliberation is “the opposite of a liberation”, like a papal conclave. Statute Art. 54(3): deliberations “shall take place in private and remain secret.”

Bedjaoui 2001, “Expediency in the Decisions of the ICJ”

Mohammed Bedjaoui, “Expediency in the Decisions of the International Court of Justice”, British Yearbook of International Law 71 (2001), pp. 1–27. Written as a former President. “Expediency” translates the French opportunité (fn 1). Full notes →

In one breath

Formally the ICJ just applies Art. 38 law, so there should be no room for expediency. Bedjaoui says there is, in “nuggets”. “The judicial function is bound up with security, but also with peacemaking” (p. 2), so wherever legality leaves room, the Court picks the solution that will end the dispute and be accepted. That is discretion, not arbitrariness. It arises because judges are human and “situated”, because the Court organises its own procedure (every “may” in the Statute), because norms have gaps or vague content (equity, the reasonable, due diligence), and because legal and political disputes cannot be separated. It appears in five functions: ruling despite the norm (Burkina Faso/Mali), declining to rule (South West Africa 1966, a disaster; advisory discretion), individualizing, clarifying (equitable principles, Libya/Malta) and supplementing the norm (Gabčíkovo). Expediency belongs to judging as inseparably as a man and his shadow (p. 26).

Part I: where the limits on applying the norm come from

  1. The Court as human pp. 6–9
    • Lyndel Prott claims judges vote in blocs and are “programmed” by their national legal training. Bedjaoui answers: true, but don’t exaggerate. The judge is “situated” and constantly “re-situated” internationally under the gaze of “rule transmitters”. Judges ad hoc (Van Wyk, Riphagen, Chagla) have proven independent. “The judgment of the human being precedes the concept”, and sententia comes from sentire, to feel.
  2. The Court as organiser of proceedings pp. 9–10
    • The Court “may” form a chamber (Art. 26(2)), indicate provisional measures (41), give advisory opinions (65), and so on. Its 1968/1976 practice Resolution lets it depart “if … the circumstances justify”. It may wait for a political settlement to mature. It is no “ivory tower”.
  3. The legislator pp. 10–13
    • (A) Gaps: no text lets the Court decline a contentious case. (B) Indeterminate concepts: “discretionary power feeds on the vagueness of a legal norm”, and two discretions clash, the state’s and the court’s. The 19th-century school of exegesis (Mourlon) led to what Pound called “mechanical jurisprudence”. Salmon lists six factors that make a concept vary: spatial, linguistic, material, temporal, extension, comprehension.
  4. The nature of the case pp. 13–14
    • Political vs legal disputes: Lauterpacht said as early as 1933 that the distinction had become an obstacle to the progress of law. Every legal dispute has political implications and vice versa. The Court has gradually strengthened its jurisdiction and now practises a “jurisprudential policy”.

Ratner, “Legal Realism School”

Steven R. Ratner, “Legal Realism School”, Max Planck Encyclopedia of Public International Law (last updated July 2007), paras 1–30. Full notes →

In one breath

Legal Realism was a US movement (Yale and Columbia, first half of the 20th century) that rejected law as a logically connected body of rules. It studied law as the output of what judges and officials actually do, the law in action not merely the law on the books (para 1). Realists were rule sceptics and behaviourists but not anti-positivist: law is a social fact, and lex lata is distinct from lex ferenda. McDougal and Lasswell carried Realism into IL as the New Haven School, which treats law as a process of authoritative and controlling decisions serving human dignity. Scandinavian Realism reduced law to psychology, and Lundstedt called IL “an empty nothing”. Political realism (Carr, Morgenthau) called law feeble against power. International lawyers answered through New Haven (authority plus control) and the Legal Process school (Chayes), and later IR institutionalists and constructivists conceded that norms matter.

The argument in order

  1. A. Background paras 1–8
    • Roots: American Pragmatism (Dewey), the German Free Law Movement, Marxism, Hohfeld, and Holmes as forerunner. Roscoe Pound attacked “mechanical” interpretation and proposed sociological jurisprudence, though he later criticised the Realists.
    • Llewellyn’s six common points: (a) law in flux, with judges creating law through policy choices; (b) law as instrumental, a means to social ends; (c) objective, social-scientific inquiry; (d) distrust of rules as predictors or as normative bases; (e) narrower categories than conventional doctrine; (f) evaluating law by its effects on society.
    • Agendas: Oliphant and Llewellyn on how judges manipulate precedent; Moore on institutions’ reactions; Cook on a scientific framework; Frank leading the “fact sceptics” (with Douglas and Arnold), for whom outcomes are unpredictable because fact-finding depends on fallible witnesses and prejudiced judges and juries.
    • Anti-formalist but not anti-positivist (para 7): they accepted that law is social fact and that lex lata and lex ferenda are distinct. Hart criticised them but shared their sense of what law is. Their heirs include law and economics, critical legal studies and feminist jurisprudence.
  2. B. Realism in IL scholarship paras 9–19
    • European anti-formalists reached similar premises without engaging the Americans: Max Huber, Georges Scelle, Alejandro Alvarez, Nicolas Politis, and later Charles de Visscher.
    • McDougal and Lasswell (New Haven School, “policy-oriented approach”): law is a process of authoritative (legitimate) and controlling (effective) decisions. Map the participants, perspectives, arenas, bases of power, strategies and outcomes. The seven phases are intelligence, promotion, prescription, invocation, application, termination and appraisal. “Prescription” replaces “sources”.
    • Lasswell’s eight values: security, wealth, respect, skill, enlightenment, well-being, rectitude, affection. Five intellectual tasks, after self-scrutiny of one’s own standpoint: clarify goals; describe past trends; identify conditioning factors; project future trends; propose alternatives. Two goals: minimum public order (non-coercive settlement, as in the Charter) and optimum public order (human dignity).
    • McDougal moved beyond Realism: he did not centre courts (IL lacks compulsory adjudication), rejected moral agnosticism, described a process rather than a rule, and made power an overt criterion. Critics said this dissolved law into social process, betrayed the is/ought distinction, and served US policy (nuclear testing, Vietnam).
    • A US–Europe divide: Europeans call US scholarship a “handmaiden of policy” advancing “an American agenda”. Ratner (“including this author”) rejects any link to a political posture and charges Europe with “overemphasis on doctrine for its own sake” (para 19).
  3. C. Scandinavian Realism paras 20–23
    • Logical-positivist anti-metaphysics. Lundstedt: IL is “an empty nothing” built on superstition, and force alone governs. Ross went less far: legal terms are shorthand for how people react. In his Textbook (1947) he exposed the circularity of defining state by sovereignty and sovereignty by IL, and saw judges as creators of law. His detailed analyses nonetheless stayed traditional.
  4. D. Legal Realism and Political Realism paras 24–30
    • Carr blamed interwar disasters on idealism about law. Morgenthau, originally a German international lawyer, saw that treaties such as the Kellogg–Briand Pact “often bore no relation to the way States behaved” (para 25). His 1940 AJIL article called for a functionalist approach, and Politics Among Nations (1948) followed. Structural realists rejected law altogether, and by the 1960s IL and IR had split.
    • Responses: McDougal’s “Law and power were … not antipodes, but co-present” (para 28), along with Schwarzenberger and Stone. The Legal Process school (Hart and Sacks at Harvard) produced Chayes, “Charles” [Thomas] Ehrlich and Lowenfeld, with case studies such as Chayes on the Cuban Missile Crisis showing that IL constrains choices. At Columbia: Friedmann, Schachter, Henkin. From the 1970s–80s, institutionalists (regimes) and constructivists (norms shape state identities) came round.

Kammerhofer, “Legal Positivism”

Jörg Kammerhofer, “Legal Positivism”, Max Planck Encyclopedia of Public International Law (last updated January 2023; it replaced Frauke Lachenmann’s 2011 entry), paras 1–36. Full notes → · Tutorial portrait: Kelsen

In one breath

“Legal positivism” is a muddled label, so Kammerhofer, himself a Kelsenian, splits it. Typical theses: law is identified by sources (form, not content); positive, human-made law is the object; law and morals are separable (lex lata vs lex ferenda); natural law is rejected; values are relative; is and ought are distinct. Classical IL positivism (Oppenheim and others, c. 1880–1914) added “sovereigntist” theses: states as the only subjects, consent, the Lotus presumption against obligation, and restrictive interpretation. Oppenheim was subtler than the caricature: no state can opt out unilaterally, and his science served natural-law ideals of peace. Today “positivism” is international lawyers’ default practice, better called a culture of orthodoxy: deference to the ICJ, peer conformity, pragmatism. Philosophical positivism survives in two schools, Kelsen (norms valid through empowering norms; the Grundnorm) and Hart (primary and secondary rules; the rule of recognition; IL only “a set of rules”). He rejects the critics’ charge that positivism is merely a political project.

The argument in order

  1. A. Notion paras 1–4
    • Saying the term is too vague to help is a “well-worn cliché”, yet Kelsen and Hart espoused it. The six typical theses are listed in the summary above.
    • Classical IL positivism is “sovereigntist” (Collins): (1) states are the original or only subjects; (2) consent is the source; (3) when in doubt, obligations do not exist (the Lotus principle); (4) interpret treaties to minimise obligations (in dubio mitius).
  2. B.1 History paras 5–10
    • Ius positivum (human-made law) as opposed to ius naturale and ius divinum. Forebears: Moser, Vattel, possibly Hobbes. The canon: Hall, Jellinek, Oppenheim, Triepel, Anzilotti (printed “Dioniso”).
    • The story of “naïve late 19th-century voluntarists” is too simple (Koskenniemi, von Bernstorff, Collins).
    • Oppenheim: states are “solely and exclusively” the subjects; IL is “a law not above but between States”; enforcement is self-help. But common consent means an “overwhelming majority”, and a state cannot unilaterally stop submitting to a rule. The first task of scholarship is exposition, rid of “bias of all kinds”, yet the science serves peace and requires “a deep-rooted faith in the progress of the nations towards peace and civilization”. Critics call it an “apology of the Eurocentric status quo” (para 6).
  3. B.2 Current practice paras 11–15
    • Positivism is the “default” mode of arguing, but as a practice: compare Koskenniemi’s “culture of formalism”. Define it inductively, since self-declarations are rare and labels mostly accusatory.
    • Example: Armed Activities (DRC v Uganda) 2005. Kooijmans read SC Res. 1368 and 1373 as recognising self-defence against non-state attacks; Simma, an “enlightened positivist”, agreed under Art. 51. Kammerhofer’s objection: the Council cannot amend the Charter, and pure practice without opinio iuris cannot override a treaty.
    • So call it a “culture of orthodoxy”: (1) privileging ICJ pronouncements over the formal sources; (2) conformity with peers and “epistemic” authorities; (3) pragmatism and accommodation (e.g. proportionality). When it becomes instrumentalism, it is furthest from positivism. Reconstructed as theory, it is closer to statist natural law (para 15).
  4. C. Modern philosophical positivism paras 16–31
    • Only two genera survive, Kelsen and Hart, possibly on “incomparable and incompatible” foundations (see the table).
    • Kammerhofer’s own work: “orthodox generalists” vs “political activists”; macro-structural (norms and sources) and micro-structural (interpretation as a frame of possible meanings) analysis; his 2021 expropriation book on customary law not being incorporated into BITs by default.
    • Hart is closer to Bentham and Austin. The rectifiers of his view of IL: van Hoof, Beckett, Payandeh, d’Aspremont, Bisogni. D’Aspremont widens law-applying authorities to all “social actors” (after Tamanaha) and reduces scholars to “grammarians”.
  5. D. Evaluation paras 32–36
    • Debates are full of “confusion, misunderstanding and quite a bit of malice”.
    • The two “how dare” charges (para 33): positivists say natural lawyers hide personal preferences as “human nature”, “in effect always a force to stabilize the status quo”; 20th-century natural lawyers say positivism panders “to the power of the strong”.
    • Critical scholars (Koskenniemi, García-Salmones, Vinx) call positivism a political project. That has some merit, but the radical Überbau version is a “foisting of premises” and amounts to “the ideology that ideology reigns supreme” (para 35).
    • Positivist practice is “as ineradicable as the concept of law based on human fiat” (para 36).

Six typical positivist theses

  • Sources thesis: form, not content, identifies law
  • Positive law is the object (positus, or empirical social fact)
  • Separability of law and morals; lex lata ≠ lex ferenda
  • Anti-naturalism: no realm of absolute norms
  • Value relativism
  • Is–ought dichotomy

How the readings connect

This is for the end-sem exam, which asks for synthesis across weeks and approaches. The threads join what the readings say. Where a link is my synthesis and no author draws it, the card says so.

The Kellogg–Briand Pact, read two ways

For H&S the Pact “set in motion” the postwar order, even though critics mocked it for failing to stop WWII (p. 85). In Ratner, Morgenthau turned to realism because interwar treaties like the Pact “often bore no relation to the way States behaved” (para 25). This is a clean essay contrast: does law work?

How law constrains

H&S: law works by changing expectations of how others will respond, and outcasting enforces it. That sits close to the Legal Process school (IL “take[s] certain policy options off the table”, para 29) and to the IR institutionalists (para 30). Morgenthau’s condition, a community of interests and a balance of power (para 26), mirrors H&S’s reliance on a US guarantor. Synthesis, not in either text

Who makes the law?

State consent (Oppenheim) · an empowering norm (Kelsen) · the rule of recognition accepted by officials (Hart) · decision-makers and judges (Realists; Ross’s “mouthpiece of the community”; McDougal) · Bedjaoui: the judge as both “rule applier” and “rule adjuster”, with equitable principles “judge-made”. Kammerhofer’s “orthodoxy” shows practitioners treating ICJ pronouncements as the law.

Anti-formalism at the Court

Bedjaoui cites Pound’s “mechanical jurisprudence” (2001, p. 12), the same Pound Ratner names as the Realists’ forerunner (para 3). Both also draw on de Visscher (Ratner para 9; Bedjaoui 2001 pp. 23–24). Bedjaoui is realist in method (judges are “situated”; judgment precedes concept) but positivist in frame, because discretion stays “within legality”.

The South West Africa / Namibia thread

SW Africa 1962 (112 sittings, Bedjaoui 1991) → the 1966 rejection on “legal interest”, which Bedjaoui 2001 calls an expediency “disaster”. Judge Tanaka’s 1966 opinion defines race by colour and hair (Kourtis p. 147) → the Namibia AO 1971, voluminous (Bedjaoui 1991) and used by Kourtis for evolutive interpretation (p. 151).

Meaning over time

Kourtis reads “racial” in today’s sense (Costa Rica v Nicaragua), against Schabas’s 1940s meaning. Bedjaoui lists a “temporal factor” among concepts of variable content and quotes Gabčíkovo (“a miscarriage of justice if prescribed in 1997”). Classical positivists preferred in dubio mitius, reading obligations narrowly (Kammerhofer para 3).

The ICJ in current events

Kourtis: the South Africa v Israel order (26 Jan 2024) relied on leaders’ statements, and judge ad hoc Barak spoke of an “existential threat”. Bedjaoui 2001: judges ad hoc have shown “independence of mind” (p. 8). H&S: the Hague Group works to enforce ICJ and ICC decisions on Gaza, and Trump threatened to sanction ICC judges.

Power, empire, race

H&S concede global-South distrust (the P5 veto, Iraq 2003). Kourtis: the League Mandate institutionalised a racial contract. Bedjaoui 1991: the bar is a “forbidden city” of a few repeat advocates, and the six states he names as pleading through their own nationals are all Western. Kammerhofer: Oppenheim is critiqued as “apology of the Eurocentric status quo”. Ratner: McDougal was accused of defending US policy.

Natural law vs positivism

H&S’s Grotius (a war is just as “the execution of a right”) belongs to the pre-positivist just-war world. Kammerhofer gives the core contrast (ius positivum vs ius naturale, para 5; the “how dare” charges, para 33). Realists sat within positivism (Ratner para 7), and the Scandinavians attacked natural-law “metaphysics” (para 20).

Portrait jurists who appear

Grotius (H&S p. 82), Lauterpacht (Bedjaoui 2001 p. 13: the political/legal distinction is “an obstacle to the progress of law”, 1933), Kelsen (Kammerhofer paras 1, 17–25). Also cited: Abi-Saab, to whom Bedjaoui’s 2001 essay was a tribute (French version in his Essays in Honor, p. 1 fn *).
