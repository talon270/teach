W8Critical historiography

d'Aspremont: Turntablism

JHIL 22 (2020) · 4 sections · 68 fns

The "historical turn" (watershed: Koskenniemi, Gentle Civilizer, 2001) is a misnomer. Lawyers had been historically conscious since the Enlightenment and the 19th-century professionalisation of the field. What changed was their historiographical appetite. Even this new work is turntablism: it spins the same records, keeping the same markers, periodisation and causal sequencing. Post-colonial and TWAIL histories do this too. His answer is a radical historical critique that puts new records on the turntable.

Lens: IL is partly the stories lawyers tell about its past. Eurocentrism survives in the markers, even in anti-colonial histories. Approach: critical legal studies and critical historiography, close to TWAIL.
W9Critical Race Theory

Girijashanker: Interest convergence

Emancipating IL (OUP 2026) ch 15 · 5 sections · 116 fns

He applies Derrick Bell's interest convergence to India. Nehru's UN fight against South African racism (Res 44 (I), 1946 → Res 1761, 1962) was real, but it served India's geopolitical and reputational interests and kept hierarchies of India's own. When the spotlight turns on India, it makes a double-move: it invokes its antiracist history and denies that the instruments apply. Examples: caste ≠ "descent" before CERD (1996), non-interference at Durban (2001), and denying racism in attacks on African students. His fix is a cross-bloc coalition that makes India's reputation the lever.

Lens: IL here means UNGA resolutions, CERD and the UPR, used by states for their interests. A postcolonial state can be both the victim and the author of hierarchy. Approach: CRT in a TWAIL-adjacent frame.
W9Dalit lens

Vishwanath: Bodies of knowledge

Emancipating IL (OUP 2026) ch 20 · 6 sections · 140 fns

She takes as given that caste is an IL problem (Burra, Venkatesh, Memon). Her question is "where is the body in international law?" IL treats Dalits as passive recipients, not active producers of legal discourse, and so erases a Dalit epistemology produced from the body. Dalit praxis follows the Three Rs: resilience, resourcefulness and resistance. The Dalit body is both subject and object. IHRL rests on a disembodied liberal subject (Mutua, Kapur). Dalit poetry and life-writing carry an emancipation that rights language cannot hold.

Lens: IL is a mechanism of knowledge production that decides who counts as a knower. Power works through caste, race and Enlightenment rationality. Approach: a Dalit lens drawing on TWAIL, feminist and CRT work.

02d'Aspremont · Turntablism

Jean d'Aspremont (Sciences Po and Manchester). An argument about how international lawyers write history. It is not a history of any rule.

The metaphor

DJs scratching someone else's record

Turntablism is "the art of creating new music and sound effects by using one or several turntables on which a record is placed" (pp. 472–473). The DJ makes new sounds but never changes the record. Lawyers writing history are "talented turntablists".

The refrain: what stays the same"markers, periodisation, and causal sequencing". Markers = canonical figures, events, texts. Periodisation = the cutting of time into eras. Causal sequencing = the story of what led to what.
Self-critiqueHe admits his own §1 "is itself constitutive of a turntablist narrative", because it is organised around the "historical turn", the marker he questions (pp. 473–474).
The four-step plan
  1. §1 Historical consciousness long pervaded the field, so the "historical turn" is better called a "historiographical turn" (474–480).
  2. §2 That turn is turntablist (480–485).
  3. §3 Turntablism is resilient, despite historians' critical mindset (485–486).
  4. §4 It is not inevitable: a radical historical critique is possible and should be promoted (486–490).
§1 · Historical → historiographical

Why "historical turn" is a misnomer

  • The common story: about 20 years ago, lawyers stopped being "complacent recipients" of linear 19th-century histories.
  • He concedes the rise in interest. The evidence is empirical (the number of publications) and institutional (new journals and book series).
  • But the Enlightenment already had an unprecedented historical consciousness. Hayden White calls its posture "Ironic".
  • 19th-century professionalisation (professorships, learned societies, RGDIP 1894, Oppenheim's "Science of IL", 1908) made a disciplinary history "very pressing". Lauterpacht wrote "The Grotian Tradition" with "acute historical self-consciousness".
  • Disciplinary history did five jobs: it confirmed the field's maturity and respectability, gave it an identity, proved its scientificity, entrenched it in a tradition (Hobsbawm) and made it a universal necessity (Chimni). The first systematic histories were Wheaton, Laurent and Nys.
  • "International lawyers did not wake up with an invigorated historical consciousness but, more simply, with a greater historiographical appetite" (p. 480). Historiography "theorises both the notion of 'the past' and the 'writing-up' of it" (Jenkins).
§2 · Three illustrations

Same heroes, same periods, even post-colonial

  • Same heroes. Gentili, Vitoria, Grotius, Vattel, Lauterpacht. Critique keeps them central: "critically engaging with those considered the 'fathers' only reinforces their fatherly place" (p. 481). The Lauterpacht paradox: critiques of his "scientific cosmopolitanism" consolidated his stature. Said's Orientalism is the analogy.
  • Same periods. The scholastic, Westphalia, the Enlightenment, the 19th century, the interwar period, decolonisation, the cold war… Same agendas too: "humanism, cosmopolitanism, modernism, state-centrism, voluntarism, hegemony, capitalism" (p. 482). Lesaffer: the debunked Westphalia myth "continues to be restated".
  • Post-colonial histories too, "albeit surprisingly". They still use "markers, periodisation, and causal sequencing all located in the West" (p. 482). fn 36: Chimni levelled this charge at Koskenniemi. It was also raised against the first generation: Alexandrowicz (1967), Elias (1972), Anand (1983).
§2 · Non-European histories stay turntablist

Three ways (p. 483)

  1. Re-centring the non-West inside a Eurocentric history. Alexandrowicz's "Grotius and India" redeems Indian Ocean practice by showing it shaped Grotius. Armitage and Pitts criticise it.
  2. Treating the non-West as "peripheral", or as a response to European IL (Becker Lorca, Shahabuddin, Meguro on "transplantation").
  3. Lamenting "the mirage of universality" while still pursuing a universal IL on "a very European idea of universality". Obregón: Bedjaoui, Anand and Elias wanted a "truly universal" IL. Pahuja: decolonisation made new states "commensurable".

Exceptions (p. 484): Eslava, Fakhri and Nesiah (eds), Bandung (2017); Shahabuddin on pre-war Japan (2019); "possibly" Kolb (2010), whose tone is still Eurocentric; Hamamoto on East Asian clichés. Half-exception: Alexandrowicz's periodisation drops Westphalia for 1815 (Congress of Vienna). Verdict: critical histories "failed to liberate disciplinary imagination" (p. 485).

§3 · Why it persists

White's restriction and Koskenniemi's resignation

  • Hayden White (Tropics of Discourse, 1978): every discipline is organised around "a set of restrictions on imagination". So historiography "cannot be the home of critique".
  • Koskenniemi's resignation (reported, not d'Aspremont's view). Lawyers owe a debt to their predecessors. Total rejection of old categories would be reductive and would lose "shared imagination" as a way to communicate. The European pedigree "cannot be unmade". So "the impossibility of avoiding some degree of Eurocentrism", even though the critique of Eurocentrism is itself European.
§4 · The cure

Radical historical critique ("historical turn 2.0")

"A conscious intervention to redraw the past and mobilise it to serve a present claim in a way that deliberately and consciously repudiates existing terms, categories" etc. (p. 487). It registers the dominant terms "with a view to consciously ignoring them in a new space of narrativisation". It is interventionist: it "purposefully turns a blind eye". Music is made through "the use of new records".

It is not: using old materials innovatively (that is Koskenniemi's plea, and it "falls short"). It is not scientific, truth-seeking or universalist. It is not a new totality (Adorno). It is a "poetry of knowledge" (Rancière). It gains new actors, turning points, ruptures, causations, genealogies, beginnings and ends (p. 488).

§4 · Four obstacles · E-L-S-U
E · Empirical rigourThe risk of "disgusting revisionism", that "anything goes". Answer: it is not literary creation. It keeps "elementary empiricism and factual evidence" and stays "plausible, intelligible, and acceptable", but uses no pre-defined "historical" method.
L · Language barriersNew places and empires mean leaving one's linguistic communities. Answer: "should not be played down" but "not insurmountable".
S · Social and culturalLawyers "may simply not know other terms". Answer: invite "new turntablists and new voices", especially those "trained outside the usual schools".
U · Unintelligibility"The risk of cacophony". Answer: it "should not be feared". Today's intelligibility is often only an "impression". Losing some is "a form of disobedience characteristic of critique" (Adorno).

Close: "load new records on their turntables… enabling the ravers in the arena to liberate their imagination" (p. 490).

03Girijashanker · Interest convergence

The thesis word is interest convergence. The pattern word is double-move. Always name the speaker.

Theory and frame · §§1–2
  • Hook. Sushma Swaraj, May 2016, after a Congolese youth was killed: "India is the land of Gandhi and Buddha… We can never have a racist mindset."
  • Derrick Bell (1980, 93 Harv L Rev 518): "the interest of blacks in achieving racial equality will be accommodated only when it converges with the interests of whites". His example is Brown v Board, which gave the US Cold War "immediate credibility". So: "racial justice—or its appearance".
  • Applied to India: "The appearance of solidarity was key". The approach "fractured" once attention turned to India's domestic record.
  • Chakrabarty (1994): in India, "racism" is "something the white people do to us". What Indians do to each other is "communalism", "regionalism", "casteism", "but never 'racism'".
  • Jayawardene, "racialized casteism": the Siddis (African-descended Indians), shaped by race, caste and colourism.
  • Varna (four ranked categories from Sanskrit texts, with Dalits outside) vs jati (thousands of local endogamous birth groups, found across religions) (Waughray and Keane). Caste affects about 260 million people. Internationally it is framed as "descent and work".
  • ICERD Art 1(1): "race, colour, descent, or national or ethnic origin". Every later fight turns on "descent".
Timeline · the record India is proud of · §3
  • 1927Cape Town Agreement. South Africa would support Indians "willing to assimilate to 'western standards of life'". This was India's legal hook.
  • 1945San Francisco. Art 2(7) (domestic jurisdiction) was put in to calm Britain, the US and Australia about their race and immigration policies.
  • 1946The "Ghetto Act" (Asiatic Land Tenure and Indian Representation Act) restricted Indians in Natal and Transvaal; 1,750 resisters were detained. India withdrew its High Commissioner and ended a trade agreement.
  • 8 Dec 1946Res 44 (I), "Treatment of people of Indian origin in the Union of South Africa", passed by two-thirds. It was diluted: the Mexico–France text dropped race and human rights and said relations were "impaired". Pandit called it an "Asian victory" and a "test case".
  • 1949Nehru: a "continuous challenge to the self-respect" of millions.
  • 1952India backed the broader item, "race conflict in South Africa", on Chari's advice.
  • 1954Res 820: apartheid is "a grave threat to the peaceful relations between ethnic groups".
  • 1955Bandung (Nehru and Sukarno): "the tragedy of Africa is greater than that of any other continent".
  • 6 Nov 1962Res 1761 condemned apartheid, set up the Special Committee and called for sanctions. The Indian item was finally merged into it.

Also: India was the first to recognise the ANC and halt trade with South Africa; Nehru set up the Indian Council of Africa; he opposed the annexation of South West Africa; and he told East African Indians to expect "no help from us" if they sought special rights. Anand: India was the "spokesperson of the newly independent countries".

The fault lines · hierarchy · §3
  • India's diplomacy was not pure altruism or "the conscience of the globe" (Anderson). It "cannot be reduced to… Nehru" (Davis and Thakur).
  • Interests: win "hearts and minds" and reshape UN membership to get support on Partition refugees and Goa. It failed: little support in the 1962 Sino-Indian war (Anand).
  • Chari (Pretoria) urged solidarity with the ANC. The MEA's response was "dismissive".
  • Bajpai (MEA Secretary General) in public said "the oppressed must unite". On his copy of a letter he wrote that the days of whites holding down "even a backward continent like Africa" are numbered.
  • Patil (1955, East Africa) said privately that segregation "from the more primitive peoples" had "some justification".
  • Gandhi's early South Africa years: Reddy says he claimed Indian superiority over Black Africans. His statue was removed at the University of Ghana in 2018.
  • "Liberal empire" (Davis): Indians were narrated as "civilised and teachable, Africans were not".
  • The separate agenda item was kept until 1962. Jantuah (Ghana): "a blow at African-Asian solidarity".
  • B.N. Rau (1949 dinner, from South Africa's archive): citizenship for "say 10, of the cultured and best type of Indians". Thakur calls it a "casteist solution to a racist problem". "Best type" meant upper-caste.
  • South Africa threw caste back. Shawcross: what if the UN enquired into caste? Louw quoted an Indian author: "come with clean hands". Pandit told Nichols this was meant to "humiliate India".
Deflection · the double-move · §4
  • India helped draft ICERD's special measures (Arts 1(4), 2(2)) and cited lower castes as beneficiaries, which implied caste was within ICERD.
  • 1996 report to CERD (CERD/C/299/Add.3): caste is a "social" and "class" distinction from an ancient functional division, and "descent" "clearly refers to 'race'". It also said race no longer "impinge[s] on the consciousness" of Indians, and para 13 cited 1946. CERD disagreed: caste is within its mandate.
  • Durban WCAR 2001: Dalit advocates used the phrase "hidden apartheid". India blocked activists (Clifford Bob) and kept "descent and work" out of the final text. Omar Abdullah said "neither legitimate nor feasible… to legislate, let alone police" and called for a "forward looking" conference. "Ironically" these were South Africa's 1946 arguments.
  • 2002 GR XXIX: descent covers "caste and analogous systems of inherited status".
  • 2007, Mr Gupta: castes share a "phenotypical resemblance", races do not, and all castes "thought poorly of the others". Rebuttals: Baber (phenotype is irrelevant to racialisation), Jayawardene (colourism), Waughray (caste is fixed and hereditary).
  • Manmohan Singh (Dec 2006): "The only parallel to the practice of 'untouchability' was Apartheid".
  • Resistance is strongest at CERD, less at CEDAW, because the racial discrimination and apartheid prohibitions have special status in IL.
Anti-Black racism · 2013–2017
  • More than 25,000 Africans study in India. Africans have been in South Asia since the 13th-century Delhi Sultanate.
  • 2013 Goa: crackdown on "unauthorized Africans"; lawmakers called them "wild animals" and a "cancer".
  • 2014 Khirki Extension: midnight raid by Somnath Bharti (AAP). Kejriwal: "We are not against any race… We are against anti-social elements."
  • African Heads of Mission called the attacks "xenophobic and racial". Swaraj called that "unfortunate, painful and surprising" and tweeted "say 'India loves you'".
  • Tarun Vijay (BJP): "If we were racist, why would we have the entire south?… We have Blacks… all around us." Like Rau, this reveals internal hierarchies.
  • 2017: calls for a UN Human Rights Council investigation faded because of trade (Ezeugo Lawrence). UPR: Haiti (2017) and Ghana (2022) each made the lone recommendation on anti-African racism; India only "noted" both.
The Hindu rashtra and the remedy · §5
  • The BJP has ruled since 2014 and is shaped by the RSS (since the 1920s). Kapur: the Hindu right frames Hindu identity partly as race, drawing on "Brahmanical racism". Savarkar said Muslims would be treated like "negroes" in America.
  • Evidence: Arts 370 and 35A revoked (2019); up to 70m Muslims and Dalits allegedly dropped from electoral rolls; the CAA; the NRC leaving 1.9m effectively stateless.
  • Salimullah (2021): the Supreme Court refused a stay on Rohingya deportations and objected to Special Rapporteur Achiume's amicus brief.
  • Possible tool: the CERD inter-state mechanism. Palestine v Israel and Qatar v Saudi Arabia & UAE were the first inter-state cases under a human rights treaty. But India has deflected CERD before.
  • Pressure works. Nupur Sharma (2022) drew protests from the OIC (57 states), and India suspended her, though it called the OIC "narrow-minded". In 2023 the MEA accused the OIC of a "communal mindset".
  • Remedy: a cross-bloc coalition of African and Muslim-majority states, perhaps led by Nigeria (an OIC member whose nationals have been targeted, and the source of the oil India "needs", per Shankar). India's reputation is "one lever".

04Vishwanath · Bodies of knowledge

The thesis words are passive recipients vs active producers of legal discourse, Dalit epistemology produced from the body, and "where is the body in international law?"

Question and thesis · §1
  • Opening line, after Venkatesh: "We cannot talk about race without addressing violence, just as we cannot talk about violent racialization without confronting caste."
  • She capitalises caste identities to "render caste visible—particularly, the lowercas(t)e". Sirleaf changed her mind; Teltumbde had used lowercase.
  • Two sides: caste falls within IHRL, or (the "purist-nationalists and sceptics") folding caste into race is "grossly reductive", a critique that could be framed as TWAIL. She uses a Dalit lens (Burra, "TWAIL's Others", 2016).
  • "International law's limited mechanisms of knowledge production have marginalized Dalits by treating them as passive recipients rather than active producers of legal discourse" (p. 380).
  • IL engages bodies only to "control, restrict, or save them". The "descriptive omission" is "deliberate" (Spain Bradley). She points to "the presence of the absence" (Derrida).
  • "Still, born": Rohith Vemula (Hyderabad, 2016): "My birth is my fatal accident." In May 2024 police closed the case, saying he had falsified his Dalit status. This is "Dalit non-existence".
Caste: history and violence · §2
  • Four varnas, from the Purushasukta in the Rigveda, codified in the Manusmriti. Verse 8.267: a Shudra who abuses a Brahmin "deserves immolation". Verse 8.272: "heated oil into his mouth and ears".
  • "Caste" comes from the Portuguese casta, "purity of blood" (Srinivas). It was solidified under colonial administration (Veena Das). There are over 1,000 caste categories; about 17% of Indians are outside them (Scheduled Castes). "Dalit" means "to split" or "rupture".
  • Resistance goes back to c. 1500–1000 BCE (Buddhism, Jainism). Phule and Periyar led the non-Brahmin movement. Colonialism both entrenched caste and "paved the way for forms of emancipation", which complicates the Dalit place in IL discourse.
  • Ambedkar sought separate electorates from 1918. The Constitution: Arts 14–16 (equality), Art 17 (abolishes "Untouchability"). Statutes: PCR Act 1955, SC/ST Act 1989. "Laws and political will alone prove insufficient."
  • NCRB: 57,582 caste hate crimes, 2018–22. Dalit women are "triply discriminated". Manual scavenging persists despite the 2013 Act.
  • Courts: Mahajan (2018) required preliminary inquiries before arrest. Khuman Singh (2019) read "only" into s 3(2)(v), a word not in the statute. A later amendment changed "on the ground" to "knowing that".
  • Beyond India: Venkatesh's "international casteist governance". US v Mozumdar (1923): a "high-caste Hindu of pure blood" was "white by law".
§3 · Dalit praxis: the Three Rs

Conversion is only a temporary escape, "because the body is permanent". So Dalits "have used their bodies as sites of resistance".

1 · Resilience: domestic lawThe law "assumes the indelibility of the caste system", against Dalits' call for abolition, yet Dalits still use it. Angela Davis's two speeches show multiple consciousness (Matsuda), a dualist tactic. "Speaking out… is a powerful form of legal dissent."
2 · Resourcefulness: international lawThe Dalit Panthers (after the Black Panthers) moved the struggle abroad. Venkatesh's Dalit Radical Tradition builds on Cedric Robinson and Ambedkar. Its two parts: law as "the ultimate act of subversion", and the strategic "use of Eurocentric liberal rights".
3 · Resistance: writingLiterature and poetry, "morally repugnant" to opponents of Dalit emancipation (§5).
§3 · The body: subject and object
  • Subject: "a site of resistance and a repository of trauma". Wing's "spirit injury". Giles's "maze of centres of perception". The body as knowledge came into IL through feminism (Charlesworth, Chinkin and Wright, 1991). Kapur: "write the body back into theory". Jeffrey: "bodies are inseparable from law".
  • Object (as printed, §3.3 repeats §3.2's heading): Vemula: "Never was a man treated as a mind." Almeida: "racism never loses sight of the body". Fanon: the white knower constructs himself through the Black man.
  • The Dalit body is "both hyper-visible and vanished": "its visibility perpetuates its invisibility". Chakrabarty: to a Brahmin it means death and waste, and is denied even touch.
  • Vantage point (not "exoticization"): a liminal standpoint "between two worlds" (Almeida). Azam: "from being 'objects of self-pity to self-owning objects'".
§4 · Why IHRL can't see Dalits
  • IL rests on the Enlightenment's "rational" individual. "Whiteness became synonymous with the mind, and Blackness the body." The rational individual is isolated from community.
  • Burra asks whether the exclusion of lower-caste experience is "endemic to… international law".
  • CERD 1996 and Durban 2001 led to little sustained action. The UPR was "superficial".
  • Mutua: the "savage-victim-saviour" metaphor. Victims are "hordes of nameless, despairing, and dispirited masses". IHRL "relies on the continual disenfranchisement of those it wishes to protect".
  • Kapur's three claims (Cruelty · Culture · Common subject). Rights mitigate cruelty more than they bring freedom. Culture is not just an obstacle. A universal subject casts victims as non-white, and "saving" Dalits reaffirms their victimhood.
  • Three more limits: reactive (rights only after violation); universalising (caste must become "sufficiently international" before IHRL acts; shouldn't it "shock the collective conscience" already?); abstract (abstract individuals over real communities).
  • Mutua: rights "must be moored in the cultures of all peoples". Soave: other epistemes are an "antidote" to lawyers' "resignation".
§5–6 · Poetry and conclusion
  • Dhasal, "Water": "How even water is taught the caste system". He was a taxi driver, a Dalit Panther, and used "raw imagery and uninhibited… expletives".
  • Sircar: "insurrectionary knowledge"; the "poetic detour". Otten/Weisberg: law is "too proud to admit" it is narrative.
  • Dalit poetry rejects Soundarya (beauty). Ganguly: Dalit pain is "not wholly translatable into the lexicon of rights". Nayar: "abject-types", "double voiced" atrocity narratives, "traumatic realism". Muthukkaruppan: caste is "a dynamic relationship continually reshaped through violence".
  • Ambedkar, "Waiting for a Visa" and Annihilation of Caste (1936). Baby Halder, A Life Less Ordinary: a domestic worker who wrote in a notebook from "Tatush" and refused the "American Dream" arc. Bama, Sangati: a woman "lifting up her sari".
  • "Unlike the resolution-driven narratives of IHRL, Dalit literature embraces incompleteness."
  • IHRL's "dark side" is its "lack of self-reflection". It "must… embrace non-liberal, non-Western, and non-Brahmanical frames". Yet the rights promise is "difficult to abandon entirely". Freedoms come "from their bodies and through their bodies".

10Self-test

Say the answer aloud, then open it. 30 questions, 10 per reading. The three full drill pages have 147 more.

d'Aspremont

Define turntablism in one line.
Making new historiographical "sounds" from the same records: keeping the same markers, periodisation and causal sequencing as the histories being critiqued.
Why is the "historical turn" a misnomer?
Lawyers were historically conscious from the Enlightenment and the 19th-century professionalisation onward. More history would only perpetuate that tradition. What was new was a historiographical appetite: theorising how the past is written.
Which book is the "watershed" of the turn?
Koskenniemi, The Gentle Civilizer of Nations (2001) (fn 4).
Give three of the five jobs disciplinary history did for the 19th-century field.
Any three of: confirm maturity and respectability (Nys); give an identity; prove scientificity; entrench a tradition (Hobsbawm); make IL a universal necessity (Chimni).
What is the Lauterpacht paradox?
Critical studies of the limits of his scientific cosmopolitanism ended up consolidating his stature. Critique of the "fathers" reinforces their fatherly place (compare Said's Orientalism).
Name the three ways non-European histories stay turntablist.
(1) Re-centring the non-West within a Eurocentric history (Alexandrowicz, "Grotius and India"). (2) Treating the non-West as peripheral or reactive. (3) Lamenting the "mirage of universality" while pursuing a European idea of universality (first-generation TWAIL: Bedjaoui, Anand, Elias).
Name two exceptions he credits.
Eslava, Fakhri and Nesiah (eds), Bandung (2017); Shahabuddin on the standard of civilisation in pre-war Japan (2019); "possibly" Kolb (2010); Hamamoto on East Asia.
Whose view is it that "some degree of Eurocentrism" can't be avoided?
Koskenniemi's, which d'Aspremont reports and then refuses.
Define radical historical critique.
A conscious intervention to redraw the past to serve a present claim that deliberately repudiates the dominant terms, categories, markers and periodisation. It registers them in order to ignore them in a new space of narrativisation. In the metaphor: new records.
The four obstacles, and his answer to the first?
E-L-S-U: empirical rigour, language, social and cultural, unintelligibility. On empirical rigour: it is not literary creation and keeps elementary empiricism and factual evidence, plus plausibility, intelligibility and acceptability, but no pre-defined "historical" method.

Girijashanker

State Bell's interest convergence and his example.
Black interests in racial equality are accommodated only when they converge with white interests. Brown v Board gave the US Cold War credibility with the third world.
What is the "double-move"?
When India is scrutinised, it (1) invokes its history of antiracism and (2) challenges whether international instruments apply in the Indian context.
Why was Res 44 (I) about Indians and not the Black majority?
Art 2(7) domestic jurisdiction made a broader resolution unlikely to pass. The Indian case had a treaty hook in the 1927 Cape Town Agreement. Pandit called it a "test case".
How was the 1946 text diluted?
The Mexico–France text dropped race and human rights. It said relations were "impaired" and that treatment should conform with the bilateral agreements and the Charter.
Res 820 and Res 1761: years and content?
820 (1954): apartheid is a "grave threat to the peaceful relations between ethnic groups". 1761 (6 Nov 1962): condemned apartheid, set up the Special Committee, called for sanctions, and absorbed India's separate item.
What did Rau propose in 1949, and what does Thakur call it?
Citizenship for "say 10, of the cultured and best type of Indians" as a token of racial equality. Thakur calls it a "casteist solution to a racist problem" ("best type" meant upper-caste).
Who said "come with clean hands", and what was the point?
Louw (South Africa), quoting an Indian author: India's caste practices undercut its moral case against South Africa.
India's two arguments to CERD in 1996?
Caste is a "social" and "class" distinction from an ancient functional division, not race; and "descent" in Art 1(1) "clearly refers to 'race'". It also cited its South African record (para 13).
What was ironic about Omar Abdullah's Durban speech?
He used the non-interference and sovereignty arguments South Africa had used against Res 44 (I), while invoking Res 44 (I) itself.
What is his proposed remedy?
A cross-bloc coalition of African and Muslim-majority states, perhaps led by Nigeria (an OIC member that sells India oil), using India's reputation as the lever. The CERD inter-state mechanism alone is not enough.

Vishwanath

State her thesis.
IL's limited mechanisms of knowledge production treat Dalits as passive recipients, not active producers of legal discourse. That erases a Dalit epistemology rooted in lived, bodily experience.
What is her driving question?
"Where is the body in international law?" She points to "the presence of the absence" (Derrida).
What does Khuman Singh (2019) show?
Courts dilute Dalit protection. The Supreme Court read "only" into s 3(2)(v) of the SC/ST Act, a word not in the statute. A later amendment used "knowing that".
The Three Rs, in order, with their sites?
Resilience (domestic law), resourcefulness (international law, the Dalit Radical Tradition), resistance (writing and poetry).
Why is conversion only a temporary escape?
"Because the body is permanent": converts still face the same problems.
Two components of Venkatesh's Dalit Radical Tradition?
Law as an emancipatory tactic, "the ultimate act of subversion"; and the strategic use of Eurocentric liberal rights to shift power to the epistemic sites of caste.
In what sense is the Dalit body "hyper-visible and vanished"?
Visible through stigma, yet erased and dehumanised: it exists only in imposed terms, so "its visibility perpetuates its invisibility". This gives a liminal vantage point (Almeida).
Kapur's three claims?
Rights mitigate cruelty more than they bring freedom; culture is not simply an obstacle to progress; a common universal subject casts victims as non-white and reaffirms victimhood.
What does the "universalising" limit demand of Dalits?
That caste first become a "sufficiently international problem" before IHRL acts. She asks whether the violence shouldn't already shock the conscience of humanity.
How does Dalit literature differ from IHRL narratives?
It "embraces incompleteness" where IHRL drives towards resolution. Dalit pain is "not wholly translatable into the lexicon of rights" (Ganguly).
