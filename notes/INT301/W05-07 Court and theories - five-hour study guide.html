EYFFINGER*

BEDJAOUI 1991

Volumes I + II

Volume I, Constitution (1870–1920): the problem of moving from episodic arbitration to a standing court; the Hague conferences and 1920 jurists’ design debates. Volume II, PCIJ (1921–1946): what that design did in practice. The PCIJ heard 29 contentious cases and gave 27 advisory opinions in 1922–40. It was linked to the League but was not an organ of it. Its statute and practice supplied substantial continuity to the ICJ.

Volume III + the exam point

Volume III, Judges (1921–2026): biography makes the supposedly impersonal institution visible through who sat on its bench. The ICJ has 15 independent judges, elected by the UN General Assembly and Security Council for nine-year terms; a party without a national judge may appoint a judge ad hoc. Exam move: connect institutional design and bench composition to Bedjaoui’s account of collective judging and the feminist question of who is absent.

Bedjaoui (1991) / the “manufacture” of judgments

Bedjaoui’s view · pp. 29–61

Judicial work is a sequence of reading, listening, deliberating and deciding, with writing as an indispensable bridge. A judgment is an institutional product: judges bring different traditions and assessments, then negotiate text that can command a majority. Read the final judgment together with separate and dissenting opinions.

01

Seisin: states file a unilateral application or a joint special agreement; authorized UN bodies request advisory opinions. The optional clause in ICJ Statute Article 36(2) has disappointed Bedjaoui; consent disputes create preliminary objections.

02

Written phase: memorial, counter-memorial, and sometimes reply and rejoinder. Annexes make files huge. Judges read them without personal law clerks; translation and the Registry matter.

03

Oral phase: parties and advocates argue, usually before a quiet bench. The point is to test and unsettle initial convictions; the same documents acquire meaning through questioning and context. In Tunisia/Libya, technical geology illustrated why judges needed to listen even when counsel’s detail seemed excessive.

04

Written notes: each judge drafts a private, anonymous mini-judgment, circulated under a Roman numeral. The President’s list of issues may guide the exercise.

05

Deliberation: judges speak in inverse seniority order, President last. This keeps senior voices from dominating the opening round. Private deliberations are protected by Statute Article 54(3).

06

Drafting: a committee elected from the majority writes a composite draft; amendments and two readings refine it in English and French. The text becomes the Court’s, not one judge’s.

07

Vote and publication: judges vote on the operative clauses, without abstention; the Court publicly reads and publishes the decision. Individual opinions may concur or dissent.

08

Interpretive lesson: obscure or cautious reasoning may reflect bargaining needed for a majority. It cannot be read like a single scholar’s essay. A slow timetable also partly reflects states’ own pleading choices.

Full course note: Bedjaoui 1991. Primary source: local PDF in the INT301 course folder.

Bedjaoui (2001) / expediency within law

Bedjaoui’s view · pp. 1–27

Law promises predictability and equal treatment, but adjudication must also end disputes. Expediency means a prudent choice among legally available paths, guided by the concrete dispute. It is discretion inside legality; arbitrary choice is outside it. A judge is never a mechanical syllogism machine.

Two terms worth holding apart: non liquet means declining to decide because the law supplies no answer; Bedjaoui resists treating legal gaps as an easy reason to abandon judgment. Equity infra legem works within a rule, while praeter legem fills around it; neither is an invitation to decide purely on personal preference. Advisory opinions add a further choice because Statute Article 65(1) says the Court may give one; Bedjaoui cautions against using that discretion simply to bypass a state’s lack of consent to contentious jurisdiction.

Why discretion arises

The court: judges are socially situated; procedural rules sometimes say “may.” The legislator: international norms contain gaps or open terms such as reasonableness, due diligence, good faith and equity. The case: political stakes and legal questions coexist; a clean political/legal split is elusive.

Five forms

  1. Rule when the norm gives no exact answer.
  2. Decline to rule despite an apparent norm.
  3. Individualize an abstract rule to concrete facts.
  4. Clarify open-textured standards.
  5. Supplement a norm to settle all aspects of a dispute.

His evaluation

Helpful: equity can prevent an absurd or destabilizing result while remaining legal. Dangerous: procedural avoidance can deny a hearing on substance. Judges must show legal grounds and acknowledge a margin of choice. The Court needs both security and peacemaking.

02 / THEORIES OF LAW

Ratner / legal realism

Ratner’s account · paras. 1–30

American legal realists challenged formalism: written rules alone do not explain what officials do. Study actual decisions, institutional behavior and social effects. The original US movement centered on domestic judges; its international descendants expanded the inquiry to policy processes, power and nonjudicial actors.

Roots and distinctions

Holmes, Pound, pragmatism and the free-law movement prepared the ground. Llewellyn’s common points include law in flux, law as a means to social ends, empirical inquiry, distrust of broad rules, narrow classifications and evaluation by effects. Rule sceptics doubt that doctrine predicts outcomes; fact sceptics (notably Jerome Frank) doubt that adjudicative facts are predictable. Realists are anti-formalist, but Ratner stresses they still treat law as a social fact and distinguish existing law from desired law.

Three international strands

New Haven: Myres McDougal and Harold Lasswell model law as decisions that are both authoritative and controlling, made by many participants across arenas; they seek a minimum order of nonviolent dispute settlement and an optimum order of human dignity. Scandinavian realism: Alf Ross studies sources as what motivates judges; Lundstedt doubts international law’s reality. Political realism: Carr and Morgenthau emphasize power, interest and weak enforcement; this is a related IR argument, not the same school.

New Haven memory cue: map participants, perspectives, arenas, power bases, strategies, outcomes and effects. Then ask what policy goals, past trends, conditioning factors, future projections and alternatives the decision engages. Its seven decision phases are intelligence, promotion, prescription, invocation, application, termination and appraisal; its eight values are security, wealth, respect, skill, enlightenment, well-being, rectitude and affection. Critics say this can dissolve law into politics and too easily defend US policy, including Vietnam and nuclear testing. Ratner’s response is that formal doctrine also misses how international law works.

Full course note: Ratner. The local Max Planck PDF cites numbered paragraphs.

Kammerhofer / legal positivism

Kammerhofer’s account · paras. 1–36

“Positivism” names several theories, and using it as a casual accusation obscures their differences. At its core, positive law is humanly posited law: identify validity by a rule’s source or place in a legal system rather than by whether its content is morally good. The separability of legal validity and moral merit does not forbid moral criticism.

Classical state focus

Oppenheim, Triepel and Anzilotti often foreground sovereign states and consent. The Lotus (1927) presumption is that a restriction on a state’s freedom should not simply be assumed. In dubio mitius reads doubt narrowly against extra obligations. Kammerhofer warns against caricaturing these writers as simple “state will makes everything” theorists.

Kelsen

Law is a hierarchy of ought norms. An act of will creates a norm only when a higher norm empowers it. The Grundnorm is a presupposed basic norm that lets legal scholarship see acts as legally valid; it is not a treaty or a psychological fact. “Pure” describes the theory’s method, which separates legal validity from politics, sociology and natural-law morality.

Hart

Law joins primary duty rules with secondary rules for recognition, change and adjudication. A rule of recognition exists through officials’ social practice. Hart described international law as a set of rules without the same developed secondary-rule system as a state. Later Hartians try to explain a more systemic international law.

Current practice: Kammerhofer calls much supposedly positivist scholarship a culture of orthodoxy—following familiar ICJ pronouncements, consensus and pragmatic accommodations—rather than a rigorous philosophy. His example is judicial disagreement between Judges Kooijmans and Simma about self-defence against non-state groups in Armed Activities (DRC v Uganda), 2005. His verdict: the label is muddled but unavoidable, so name the specific theory and its criterion of validity.

03 / WHOSE WORLD?

Charlesworth, Chinkin & Wright (1991) / feminist approaches

The authors’ view · pp. 613–645

International law presents its rules as neutral while its institutions and categories largely reflect male experience. Feminist inquiry asks who speaks, which injuries become visible, and whether legal remedies alter the social and economic structures that make discrimination possible. There is no single feminist voice or method.

I–II. Introduction and different voices · 613–621

The authors draw on critical legal theory and Carol Gilligan’s “ethic of care” debate to question whether abstract rights reasoning captures relationships and dependence. They refuse to treat women as one uniform group. Third World challenges to European dominance share an interest in exclusion but often overlook gender; Western feminism can misunderstand Third World women. Reproductive choice, anti-colonial nationalism, poverty and repression make agendas vary by place. Their common concern is domination by men, not a fixed “female nature.”

III. Masculine world · 621–634

Organization: states, diplomats, judges and experts are predominantly male. The article’s historical 1991 examples include women leading only 4 of 159 UN member governments and 4 of 149 permanent missions (March 1990). What counts as a public issue is therefore skewed. Norms: international law reproduces public/private divisions: relations among states versus “domestic” matters, and public violence versus injury in the home. Torture rules emphasizing official conduct can miss domestic violence; slavery and trafficking show how law can see some exploitation while obscuring its gendered structure.

CEDAW as an ambiguous advance · 631–634

CEDAW (1979; in force 1981) names sex discrimination and reaches political, economic, social and cultural fields. Yet its equal-treatment frame can leave male life as the implicit standard; “special” women’s issues are marginalized. Extensive reservations and a weak institutional position limit the treaty. The authors argue that education and attitude change alone cannot remedy structural inequality. Exam trap: they do not dismiss CEDAW; they show its promise and its limits.

IV–V. Rights, development and reconstruction · 634–645

Formal rights can mask unequal resources, pit a woman against family power, privilege civil/political over economic/social needs, or protect practices harmful to women. But rights can also give excluded groups recognition and organizing power. The 1986 Right to Development Declaration sounds universal yet may ignore women’s unpaid work and unequal access to land, aid and jobs. The authors propose gender-aware interpretation, attention to structural abuse, broader state responsibility for private acts, and access for women to international complaint mechanisms. They end cautiously: law constructs what the world treats as public, so changing it can open political possibilities without guaranteeing equality.

Primary publication: AJIL 85(4), 1991; a full scan/transcription was consulted. Do not substitute the course folder’s Charlesworth (1999) “Feminist Methods” note for this 1991 three-author reading.

Makau Mutua (2000) / What is TWAIL?

Mutua’s view · pp. 31–38

The international legal order acquired worldwide reach through empire, but geographic universality does not make it just. TWAIL is an intellectual and political response rooted in anti-colonial struggle and Bandung. It diagnoses the order, designs alternatives and seeks to end underdevelopment.

Three objectives

  1. Expose the racial hierarchy embedded in norms and institutions.
  2. Construct a different legal framework for international governance.
  3. Transform material conditions of underdevelopment through scholarship, policy and politics.

Mutua values earlier reformers as well as radicals (“affirmative reconstructionists”) and rejects elites who simply assimilate into an unjust order (“minimalist assimilationists”).

Four defining commitments

  1. Anti-hierarchical: reject civilizational rankings and “othering.”
  2. Counterhegemonic: challenge Security Council privilege, debt regimes, Bretton Woods institutions and Western control of trade.
  3. Sceptical of imposed universals: judge rights and market rules by whose experience made them and whose interests they serve.
  4. Coalitionary: work with critical race theory, other critical scholars and marginalized people everywhere.

Historical chain: colonial conquest → formal decolonization and sovereign equality → continued dependence through the Security Council, IMF/World Bank and GATT/WTO → Group of 77 and the New International Economic Order as attempted reconstruction. Mutua also criticizes Third World states that silence their own poor and non-state constituencies. Exam trap: TWAIL challenges an existing legal order; it does not say international law is unnecessary or that all norms from outside the Third World are automatically invalid.

Primary record: University at Buffalo · ASIL Proceedings. Full text consulted via mirrored scan.

Anghie, Chimni, Fakhri, Mickelson & Nesiah (2025) / Introduction

The editors’ view · pp. 1–8

The Research Handbook on Third World Approaches to International Law presents TWAIL as a many-voiced scholarly project with practical stakes. Its critiques explain how rules distribute benefits and reproduce colonial afterlives; its constructive work imagines solidarity, resistance and new legal futures.

§1. Introduction · 1–3

Ideas and institutions have material consequences. TWAIL recovers anti-colonial archives and marginalized voices, interrogates sovereignty, borders and human rights, and asks how legal regimes allocate power. Earlier “TWAIL I” lawyers worked within institutions: New International Economic Order proposals, ICJ judging and national liberation. The editors use Georges Abi-Saab’s “behind enemy lines” image to capture tactical engagement with a legal system shaped by power. They also mention current human-rights work concerning Palestine and Gaza.

§2. The Third World? · 3–5

The term points to states emerging from colonialism and to Bandung (1955), non-alignment, self-determination and NIEO struggles. It has problems if read as a stage on a ladder toward a “First World.” The editors defend its oppositional, historical and solidaristic meaning: the Third World is a project, not merely a location. “Global South” does not fully preserve that history.

§3. Politics and geographies · 5–7

Where scholarship is produced, published and cited matters. Global North institutions often validate or amplify TWAIL work while Global South scholars become less audible. The editors call for alternative genealogies, syllabi, conferences, journals and networks, including on debt and feminist TWAIL. They reject any single scholar’s claim to speak for a homogeneous Third World.

§4 + conclusion · 7–8

The 50-chapter Handbook aims to make TWAIL usable across classic topics (treaties, self-determination, rights, criminal law), linked fields (race, history, feminism, Marxism), and current problems (climate, debt). The conclusion uses the Chagos Archipelago to show that formal transfer of sovereignty leaves hard questions about military power and Chagossian interests. Decolonization remains unfinished, and TWAIL’s analysis applies to inequality beyond a geographic Third World.

05 / WRITE A SUMMATIVE ANSWER

Prompt A

“The ICJ merely applies pre-existing rules.” Discuss.

  1. Start with Statute Art. 38 and consent to jurisdiction: legal constraints are real.
  2. Use Bedjaoui 1991 to show collective production and interpretive negotiation.
  3. Use Bedjaoui 2001: rules often leave legally bounded choices; Frontier Dispute helps, South West Africa warns.
  4. Contrast positivist validity with realist law in action.
  5. Conclude that judgment requires reasons and accountability for discretion.

Prompt B

“International law is universal.” Assess through Week 7.

  1. Separate worldwide application from equal voice in rule making.
  2. 1991 feminists: male personnel, public/private split, CEDAW’s limits.
  3. Mutua: colonial origins, UNSC/economic hierarchy and reconstruction.
  4. 2025 editors: Third World as project; knowledge production and Chagos.
  5. Conclude with concrete reforms: access, representation, material redistribution, plural histories.

Prompt C

“What does a theory of international law reveal and conceal?”

  1. Define positivism precisely: source and validity; distinguish Kelsen from Hart.
  2. Realism adds decisions, effects and policy, but may lose legal specificity.
  3. Bedjaoui gives a court-level test of both theories.
  4. Feminism and TWAIL expose social and historical exclusions in their starting assumptions.
  5. Argue for a layered account: validity, practice and distribution.
Five traps: PCIJ ≠ ICJ; realism ≠ political realism; legal discretion ≠ arbitrariness; formal equality ≠ lived equality; TWAIL critique ≠ rejection of every international legal norm.

W05 / COURT AND BEDJAOUI
01. Why is the PCIJ a turning point after the PCA?

It was a standing court with a statute and fixed procedure, rather than a tribunal assembled for each arbitration.

02. Was the PCIJ itself a League organ?

No. It was closely linked to the League but institutionally separate.

03. What does Article 36(2) do?

Allows states to accept compulsory jurisdiction in advance toward other states accepting the same obligation, subject to their declarations.

04. What are Bedjaoui’s four judicial verbs?

Read, listen, deliberate, decide; writing is the key intermediate activity.

05. What is a judge’s written note?

An anonymous personal mini-judgment circulated to colleagues before deliberation.

06. Why does the President speak last?

Deliberation proceeds in inverse seniority, limiting senior influence at the outset.

07. Why can a judgment’s reasoning be cautious?

It is a composite text capable of holding a majority; separate/dissenting opinions reveal disagreement.

08. Define expediency as Bedjaoui uses it.

Prudent selection among lawful options to suit a concrete dispute; it is neither arbitrary nor simply political convenience.

09. Name the three sources of limits on mechanical judging.

The human/legal court; the incomplete or open-textured legislator; and the mixed legal/political nature of cases.

10. Which case is Bedjaoui’s warning about declining to rule?

South West Africa (1966), where a standing objection kept the Court from the merits.

11. Which case shows equity helping an inherited boundary rule?

Frontier Dispute (Burkina Faso/Mali), 1986.

12. Why is ex aequo et bono different from ordinary equity?

Article 38(2) permits decision outside ordinary legal rules only with party agreement; Bedjaoui’s legally bounded equity remains within adjudication.

W06 / RATNER AND KAMMERHOFER
13. What does “law in action” mean?

Study officials’ actual decisions and effects, not just the text of legal rules.

14. Rule scepticism versus fact scepticism?

The first doubts rules predict decisions; the second doubts facts found by courts are predictable.

15. Does Ratner call legal realists anti-positivist?

No. They reject formalism but often still see law as a social fact and separate law as is from law as desired.

16. Who are the two New Haven names?

Myres McDougal and Harold Lasswell.

17. What two qualities make a New Haven decision law?

Authority (legitimacy) and control (effectiveness).

18. What are New Haven’s minimum and optimum orders?

Minimum: nonviolent dispute settlement. Optimum: human dignity through shared values.

19. How does political realism differ from legal realism?

IR political realism starts with state interest and power; legal realism studies how law operates in decisions and behavior.

20. State the source/separability claims of positivism.

Validity rests on humanly identified sources or criteria; legal validity and moral merit are analytically distinct.

21. What is the Lotus principle in this debate?

Do not presume a restriction on state freedom without a legal basis.

22. What is Kelsen’s Grundnorm?

A presupposed basic norm enabling interpretation of a hierarchy of norms as legally valid.

23. What is Hart’s rule of recognition?

An official social practice identifying valid legal sources.

24. Why does Kammerhofer dislike the loose word “positivist”?

It conflates classical state consent, ordinary doctrinal orthodoxy, Kelsenian normativism and Hartian social-rule theories.

W07 / FEMINISM AND TWAIL
25. What are the feminist article’s two structural targets?

The organizational composition of international law and its normative categories.

26. How does the public/private line hide harm?

It treats violence in homes or “domestic” jurisdictions as outside international concern despite state and social power shaping it.

27. Why is the 1991 authors’ view of CEDAW mixed?

It legally recognizes discrimination, but male-standard equality, reservations and weak structural remedies limit change.

28. Do they reject rights altogether?

No. Rights can mobilize and make people visible, although formal rights alone do not remove unequal power.

29. Give one gendered problem with the right to development.

Neutral language can hide unpaid labor, unequal aid, land access or low-paid work.

30. Why must feminism attend to Third World women’s differences?

Colonial history, nationalism, poverty, population policies and repression change what equal freedom requires.

31. Name Mutua’s three TWAIL objectives.

Expose hierarchy, build an alternative legal order, and end underdevelopment.

32. What does Mutua mean by “Third World”?

A historically produced and oppositional political experience, not simply a development rank or geographic zone.

33. Name Mutua’s four commitments.

Anti-hierarchical, counterhegemonic, suspicious of imposed universals, and coalitionary.

34. Does Mutua excuse Third World state elites?

No. He criticizes collaborators and calls for internal democratization and subaltern voices.

35. How do the Handbook editors define the Third World?

As a political and historical project, not merely a place.

36. What is the editors’ knowledge-production critique?

Global North universities, publishers and citation systems disproportionately validate TWAIL work and can silence Global South scholars.

37. Why do they cite Abi-Saab’s “behind enemy lines” image?

To describe tactical engagement with institutions whose power structures TWAIL also criticizes.

38. What does the Chagos example show?

Formal sovereignty change can leave military power and displaced people’s claims unresolved.

CROSS-WEEK / SYNTHESIS
39. What could a feminist ask of Bedjaoui’s judicial “factory”?

Whose voices enter the file, who sits on the bench, and which injuries the Court’s categories can hear.

40. Give one three-layer account of international law.

Positivism explains validity; realism and Bedjaoui show practice; feminism and TWAIL expose whose interests the law distributes and whose voices it excludes.
