One World or None

Nuclear order, 1945–1957, and the idea behind it

Three readings about who gets to order the nuclear world. Bensaude-Vincent, Boudia and Sato redefine “nuclear order” as everything nuclear that shapes how we live, not just deterrence and treaties. Roehrlich tells how the first try at international control, the UN Atomic Energy Commission of 1946–52, failed and still set the template for the IAEA. Kraft follows British scientists who defied their government over H-bomb fallout between 1954 and 1957, and ended up founding Pugwash.

The week in one breath · one block per reading

1 · Bensaude-Vincent, Boudia & Sato (2022)

Fukushima has become “ordinary”, just as every nuclear disaster since Hiroshima has been trivialised. How did such a destructive technology become mundane? Their answer is that the nuclear order is not only deterrence and nonproliferation among states. It is “the dimensions of the nuclear domain that constitute and mediate our experience of the world”. It was built through four entangled dynamics: managing violence by splitting bombs from energy; pacifying atoms through promotion, expertise and control; normalising risk through secrecy, denial and exceptionalism; and timescaping, reordering past and future. There is “no end of the nuclear age in sight”, so adaptation is the only option.

2 · Roehrlich (2022), ch. 1

From H. G. Wells's The World Set Free (1914) to the scientists' slogan “One World or None” (1945), the bomb made international control look like the only way to survive. The UN Atomic Energy Commission (1946–52) deadlocked. The US Baruch Plan wanted a supranational authority with no veto and with sanctions; the Soviet plan wanted a ban on the bomb first, with inspection later. The Commission died after the Soviet test of 1949. But the talks were not just Cold War theatre. They produced the one idea nobody questioned, that every state should have access to peaceful nuclear energy, along with thinking on safeguards and a dual mandate. “The IAEA would be born from such thinking.”

3 · Kraft (2018)

Castle Bravo (1954) made fallout a public danger. Britain, racing to build its own H-bomb, managed it in secret. It struck a quiet deal with the BBC, buried the grim Strath Report, and in 1956 published an MRC report calling fallout a “negligible hazard”. Dissenting scientists outside the “nuclear nexus”, above all Joseph Rotblat and Bertrand Russell, broke the silence: Rotblat's 1955 Bravo analysis, the Russell–Einstein Manifesto, and the 1957 Sr-90 statement. They were vilified for it. Blocked at home, they turned transnational. Fallout was “a necessary, and perhaps a sufficient, condition” for Pugwash, which first met in Nova Scotia in July 1957.

How the three readings connect

Read this before the summaries

1 · Two meanings of “nuclear order”

In international relations, “nuclear order” means states and international organisations preventing war through deterrence and nonproliferation. The introduction names Scheinman, Walker and Ritchie as examples Intro p. 3. Roehrlich's UNAEC story is that kind of order in the making: a commission, vetoes, safeguards. Bensaude-Vincent, Boudia and Sato widen the term to take in “survivor bodies, representation, expertise and worldviews”. Kraft sits in between. His order is made by governments, but also by scientists who either lend it authority (the MRC) or contest it (Rotblat).

2 · The bomb/energy line holds everything up

The introduction's key mechanism is the “clear-cut demarcation between bombs and energy” p. 4. Roehrlich shows where that line came from. There was “no precedent for the prohibition of an entire form of energy generation”, so the only uncontroversial idea at the UNAEC was that every state should have access to peaceful uses; hence a dual mandate to promote and to control pp. 22, 26. Kraft shows the line was “artificial and born of political expediency”. Calder Hall was celebrated for its electricity, while its plutonium went quietly into weapons. The MRC report was “a carefully crafted manifesto for nuclear energy” pp. 76, 84–85.

3 · Scientists: insiders, outsiders, internationalists

Roehrlich's physicists (Bohr, Oppenheimer) pushed for international control and were sidelined after “the ‘hierarchy’ took over” p. 27. Kraft's Britain split its scientists into government insiders, the “atomic knights” Cockcroft, Penney and Hinton, and outsiders like Rotblat. Blocked at home, the outsiders chose a transnational route: Pugwash. The introduction adds that expertise both produced and hid knowledge. It built a pacified atom that “paradoxically also produced ignorance and blind spots” p. 8, and Weinberg called the resulting system a “military priesthood” p. 10.

4 · Secrecy as a tool of order

The introduction calls secrecy and dissimulation “distinctive features of the nuclear milieu”, not exceptions p. 10. Kraft gives the British mechanics. Policy was made by a handful of people. The BBC agreed on 15 February 1955 to restrict H-bomb coverage. The Strath Report stayed secret until about 2000. Rotblat was branded “rather wild” and a “fellow traveler”. In Roehrlich's story, secrecy and espionage are what sink sharing: the US public would not accept sharing with a state that had spied on it p. 29.

5 · One world, or one network

“One World or None” asked for world government. The UNAEC tried an intergovernmental commission and deadlocked. Kraft's scientists invented a third form, “a network of scientists” doing “second-track” diplomacy, which was “a response to the failure of traditional diplomacy”. Fallout justified all of it. In Rabinowitch's words it was “universal and compulsory”, and the introduction treats it as the first planet-wide environmental issue Kraft p. 61; Intro p. 3.

6 · Links to other weeks

Week 3 (read in full): Kraft's story begins where Higuchi's ends, with Castle Bravo cracking the powers' “definitional power”. Kraft also cites Higuchi's earlier work, names Gabriel and Sunshine, and makes Libby an AEC commissioner defending tests. The introduction cites Lindee (1994) on secrecy about radiation effects, and its bomb/energy demarcation is the line Lindee says Fukushima dissolved. Week 4: Kraft reads Atoms for Peace as winning “hearts and minds” during a thermonuclear build-up. Roehrlich's chapter 2 continues her story, and the introduction cites Krige (2006) and Hecht's “nuclear exceptionalism”. Week 1: the UNAEC's fight over veto and sanctions is order against justice in practice, under Bull's stated thesis. Weeks 5–6: the introduction cites Creager on isotopes as foreign policy and Masco on bombs and planetary crisis. Links to weeks not read in full use only those readings' stated theses.

The introduction in one breath

Fukushima, like Hiroshima, Chernobyl and every nuclear “event” before it, has been trivialised. Its effects linger, but its “eventness” fades. Some 30 nations still rely on nuclear power. The editors ask “How did it become mundane?” They treat nuclear technology as “a world-making technology par excellence” and redefine the nuclear order: not the IR sense of states preventing war, but “the dimensions of the nuclear domain that constitute and mediate our experience of the world”, meaning bodies, expertise, infrastructures, categories and time. The volume's four sections name the dynamics that built it. Managing violence: violence was reserved for bombs, and reactors were cast as benign. Pacifying atoms: promotion, expertise and control produced knowledge and ignorance together. Normalising risk: secrecy, denial and “nuclear exceptionalism” did the work. Timescaping: nuclear technology reordered past and future. Since the nuclear age will outlast our anticipation, “adaptation becomes the only possibility” in an “adaptive Anthropocene”.

Opening: the Olympic flame at J-Village Unheaded opening

In March 2020 the Olympic flame came from Greece to Japan. For the authorities, the Torch Relay symbolised recovery from earthquake, tsunami and nuclear accident. It was to start from J-Village, a luxurious national soccer centre about 20 km from the defunct Fukushima Daiichi plant, funded by TEPCO and restored with a donation from Kuwait n. 1. Governor Masao Uchibori: “We are happy to send out a message, at home and abroad, that any difficulty can be overcome.” But COVID-19 was spreading, and officially 41,000 residents were still displaced nine years on, “a number many consider an undercount”.

The “Reconstruction Games” were postponed to 2021, the disaster's tenth anniversary. The meltdown had turned “a vast swath of land … into a semi-wasteland”, and watching it happen in an advanced industrial nation, “we were caught off-guard” by the damage and the uncertainty p. 1.

pp. 1–4

One mantra: never again

“Today, the Fukushima disaster has become ordinary.” Its repercussions go on (evacuation, uncertainty about radiation, lawsuits over accountability and compensation), and experts updated standards and stress tests. Yet it faded into memory, trivialised like the disasters before it, starting with Hiroshima and Nagasaki. “These events have become so commonplace that their eventness is questionable. Their lingering effects are not.” pp. 1–2

Several generations have lived in a “nuclearised world”: the Cuban missile crisis, Three Mile Island, Chernobyl. (“We” n. 2 means humans generally, not the abstract “Anthropos” of the Anthropocene. A lesson of Fukushima is that humans are “bound to Earth”.) Each accident prompts the same questions (what can we learn, how do we ensure it never happens again?), yet daily life is barely shaken. Few noticed when the Doomsday Clock (invented by atomic scientists in 1947, long between 5 and 12 minutes to midnight) moved to two minutes in 2018 p. 2.

About 30 nations, Japan included, rely on nuclear energy, with dozens of reactors being built in the Global South and North. Their questions: how did such a destructive technology become so central over 75 years, so widely adopted despite disasters and “hollow assertions” that they won't recur? “How did it become mundane?” The volume treats this as a case of how “adaptation to disasters and the forgetting of crises can be manufactured” p. 2.

The editors' framework

They build on technoscience: science and technology are “constitutive of our world”, and artifacts carrying economic, social and political values “have lives of their own … shaping our senses, desires and actions”. “Nuclear technology is a world-making technology par excellence.” It has been hailed as security, innovation and abundant energy (Hecht 1998; Jasanoff and Kim 2013), and it has also threatened health, poisoned water and food, and degraded the environment. Fallout from atmospheric tests was “the first planet-wide environmental issue recognised in the 1950s” (Higuchi, 2020), and radioactive traces are now “the quintessential indicators of humanity's impact on Earth” (Masco, 2010) pp. 2–3.

Fukushima is used “as a prism”, looking back to Hiroshima to reexamine the “balance” of good and evil in a dual-use technology, using new and declassified documents, fieldwork, Anthropocene studies and international scholarship (n. 3: two workshops of historians, anthropologists, STS scholars and philosophers), focused on the US, France and Japan. The aim is not fine-grained history but “an interdisciplinary perspective on the construction of the nuclear order”. It overlaps with Gordin and Ikenberry (2020), but instead of centring Hiroshima it stresses violence, expertise and infrastructure p. 3.

Key definition

“Nuclear order refers to the dimensions of the nuclear domain that constitute and mediate our experience of the world.” This is “more encompassing” than the IR usage, a global order preventing war through “deterrence and nonproliferation” (Scheinman 1987; Walker 2000; Ritchie 2019). Those analyses “focus on nation-states and international organisations instead of survivor bodies, representation, expertise and worldviews”. This volume takes in material and institutional infrastructures, the cultural categories that structure space and time, and symbolic and physical traces. The nuclear order is “both … a product of history and … a constitutive element of the future world” p. 3.

Four sections name “four entangled dynamics” answering two questions: How has nuclear technology shaped the world we live in? How have we come to live with this technology? Nuclear things are world-objects (Michel Serres): global in reach but “ungovernable and indomitable” however much technical and political work goes into controlling them pp. 3–4.

Managing violence: categories and demarcation

Why the disregard for evidence of destruction? On 6 August 1945 Hiroshima saw a blast whose heat melted metal, then radioactive “black rain”. About 70,000–140,000 died in Hiroshima and 40,000–80,000 in Nagasaki within months n. 4 (Wellerstein 2020), and tens of thousands more suffered radiation and burns. The US justified the bombings as ending the war, “deftly evading responsibility for the humanitarian consequences”. The arms race that followed bred a “well-founded fear” of human extinction p. 4.

The editors' argument

“A key mechanism for establishing this order was the imposition of a clear-cut demarcation between bombs and energy.” Bombs were destructive, to be feared and restricted; energy would improve modern life. “Violence became exclusively associated with weapons and war.” Crucial to this was the persistent downplaying of radiation's “slow afflictions”. The US Occupation called the bombs' destruction instant, a brief hot blast, and through censorship “purposefully concealed the long-term harm they already had reason to expect” pp. 4–5.

“Downplaying this intrinsic violence has been necessary for expanding nuclear energy programs.” From Atoms for Peace (1953), civilian programmes “flourished without causing similar alarm”. But Three Mile Island, Chernobyl and Fukushima show that “nuclear violence is not bound to formal hostilities. The potential for devastation is embedded within the reactor” p. 5.

Chapters previewed

  • Kyoko Sato: much of what we know about radiation's health effects comes from the hibakusha, yet the standards built on it decide who is “worthy” of support as a certified hibakusha. Survivors pushed back with testimony and counter-expertise, and status slowly expanded, but exclusions added “symbolic violence”. Thresholds based on distance from ground zero devalued “each survivor's lived, bodily experience” p. 5.
  • Nuclear geography (Davies; Alexis-Martin and Davies): because radiation is invisible, zoning of contaminated and habitable land comes from “tangled compromises” between standard measurements and negotiation p. 5.
  • Joseph Masco: tests, unlike slow radiation, are “spectacular and swift”. Trinity's witnesses felt the “nuclear sublime” (Wilson 1994). In the 1950s Kodak and other contractors filmed Pacific tests and put film badges on workers. Masco links menacing images to slow violence on bodies and global contamination: exposure records “duly recorded and dully considered within safety thresholds, quietly allowed those tests to continue”. The records form “an archive of US nuclear nationalism” with implications for accountability pp. 5–6.
  • Iconic images and Cold War propaganda framed nuclear issues as survival. After Sputnik (1957), Apollo's “Blue Marble” helped create a planetary view. Jasanoff's “sociotechnical imaginaries” are visions of desirable futures achievable through technoscience p. 6.
  • Boundaries between military and civilian, worthy and unworthy: “destructive pursuits come from ‘bad guys’ while ‘good guys’—identified as Western, White and male—work with non-destructive atoms. Only the ‘other’ needs policing.” John Krige's chapter shows boundary work using tropes of gender, race and pathology to build a “nuclear apartheid”. Dual use rests on “externalised malice” (Rabinow and Bennet) set against “internalised good intentions”, so that “we” are entitled to split atoms. US leaders framed nuclear weapons as existential and American leadership as key to a stable order. Colonial and imperial worldviews are “virtually indistinguishable from the entire endeavor” pp. 6–7.
  • Mary X. Mitchell: the US changed Micronesia's legal status and built a flexible testing network in the Marshall Islands. This spared the homeland but doomed islanders and others (the Japanese crew of the Lucky Dragon No. 5 at Bravo), reinforcing a “neo-colonial world order of White and non-White countries”. The same “externalisation of malice” fuels fears of “Islamic bombs”, “rogue states” and “nuclear orientalism” (Gusterson 1999) p. 7.

The result is a tentative order of demarcations: “good and bad, safe and dangerous, contaminated and habitable”. Yet fallout has “lasting and possibly immeasurable effects” p. 7.

pp. 7–10

Pacifying atoms: control and containment

The violence of splitting atoms “had to be domesticated to secure the technology's acceptance”. The arms race made containment and deterrence foundational, and a world peace movement arose at once (Wittner), with scientists and intellectuals calling for international nuclear governance p. 7.

A complementary pacification promoted medicine and energy, shoring up the military/civilian boundary: national greatness (Hecht 1998), Atoms for Peace (Krige 2006), reactors promising abundance in the 1970s oil crisis, and today a rebrand as “green” technology that emits no warming gases n. 5 (Bill Gates backing new plants; TerraPower's reactor in Wyoming) pp. 7–8.

Expertise made the pacified atom. The Manhattan Project concentrated unprecedented expertise and money. Cold War efforts to understand where bombs could be used and what radiation does created or transformed whole fields, including environmental science and climate research (Doel; Turchetti and Roberts). In the late 1940s and 1950s the US used nuclear knowledge and isotopes as foreign policy (Creager 2015; Krige 2016) p. 8.

The editors' paradox

The advance of knowledge and control “paradoxically also produced ignorance and blind spots”. Born of fear, the postwar order was rebuilt on “rational foundations” by experts who framed “a regime of global surveillance, oversight and regulation that legitimised nuclear activities in the public eye”. Some rose in global networks while serving national interests and mediating with political authorities over fallout p. 8.

Chapters previewed

  • Angela Creager and Maria Rentetzi: the IAEA (1957), promoting “peaceful” uses while preventing diversion, has “twin divergent goals”, a contradiction “never … reconciled or even officially acknowledged”. It steers the spread of technologies, materials, lab designs and safety practices (though it once lacked authority to enforce them). Its promotion resembles development aid to the Global South, but dual use needs “a different regime of geopolitical control” pp. 8–9.
  • Nestor Herran: radiation monitoring networks began as military intelligence (did the enemy have the bomb?), merged with the 1950s fallout controversy, spread with 1960s power stations, and sped up after Chernobyl, which also produced citizens' counter-expertise platforms (Topçu) p. 9.
  • Containment: the IAEA and nuclear-funded experts produce risk concepts and doctrines (Boudia 2014), with serious accidents as a central theme p. 9.
  • Maël Goumri: US and French engineers (1950s–1980s) moved from denying that accidents could happen to the concept of “hypothetical accidents”, framing severe accidents as improbable and “theoretical”, a “residual” domain, instead of learning from real experience p. 9.
  • Tania Navarro: French experts saw a partnership of nature and technology as the answer to waste. The global shift from disposal to storage and from dilution to containment grew with concern about the volume of waste p. 9.

Surveillance, regulation and monitoring are “indispensable companions in the nuclearised world”. Alvin Weinberg (1972): the “military priesthood” set up to control weapons had to be extended to other radioactive materials. The “Faustian bargain” gave cheap, clean energy in exchange for society keeping expert institutions alive for ever and accepting “a defanged acknowledgement of risk” p. 10.

pp. 10–12

Normalising risk: denial and trivialisation

Accidents and counter-expertise undermine the “containment doctrine”. After 75 years the cumulative damage is considerable, and this section studies how institutions and governments minimise hazards and govern “living amid toxic ruins” p. 10.

Secrecy surrounded weapons and reactor details (Galison 2010; Wellerstein 2021) and the effects of radiation at Hiroshima and Nagasaki, not fully disclosed for decades (Lindee 1994). “Far from exceptions, these patterns of retention and dissimulation of information are distinctive features of the nuclear milieu.” p. 10

  • Hiroko Takahashi: in 1954, after a Japanese fishing boat was hit by fallout from a US test, Japan briefly inspected tuna and destroyed contaminated fish. The “so-called full settlement” was crucial for continued US testing, backed by Japanese and American scientists who downplayed health effects at a Tokyo conference. Politics and diplomacy ended the diagnostic testing and data collection p. 10.
  • Censorship and press codes restricted early discussion (Braw; Takahashi). For decades scholars have interviewed Bikini and Marshall Islanders, test veterans, African uranium miners (Hecht) and plant workers (Jobin) to “backfill crucial data” pp. 10–11.
  • Disqualification and denial are maintained by psychological and physical violence (Chernobyl: Kuchinskaya; Brown): refuting victims' suffering, denigrating opponents, threats, surveillance, imprisonment, even death sentences, “perhaps its own sort of nuclear waste containment and disposal—simply applied to the human evidence of ongoing risk” p. 11.
  • Kate Brown: Chernobyl parents failed to win foreign attention for their children's illnesses. Western experts displaced Soviet medicine's focus on environmental causes with individualist explanations (behaviour, psychology, genes). UN and national agency “experts” dismissed fallout effects and even blamed Soviet citizens' “addiction” to state welfare p. 11.
  • Harry Bernas: Fukushima was a “normal accident” (Perrow 1984), not an “unforeseeable” natural event. Utilities, ministries and regulators ignored a seismologically “overdue” earthquake and tsunami; profit, power and inertia allowed “uncertainty and criminal malpractice” in siting reactors on fault-ridden coasts p. 11.
Exceptionalism and its flip side

Minimisation often takes the form of Hecht's nuclear exceptionalism (2012): singularising each case, justifying it by local circumstance, and using culture, “often tainted by stereotypes”, to explain it. So Chernobyl becomes an old Soviet reactor inferior to Europe's, and Fukushima becomes extraordinary circumstances plus “a culture of obedience” in which people did not feel empowered to improvise p. 11.

The flip side is normalisation and trivialisation. Making nuclear technology unproblematic “requires political maintenance work”: techno-political initiatives, through knowledge production, safety and security, public expertise, categories and governance technologies, that make institutions “resistant to challenge and criticism” and convince the world nuclear technology is “indispensable” p. 12.

Soraya Boudia's chapter: global nuclear governance has been “a political proving ground” for managing hazards, with three intermingled paradigms, containment, risk assessment and adaptation, forged by the IAEA, ICRP and UNSCEAR. Chernobyl and Fukushima prompted social experiments in continuing daily life in “sustainably contaminated” areas, now applied elsewhere. The accumulated tools carry “intrinsic contradictions”, creating new risks and eroding legitimacy without stopping nuclear development, “despite the exorbitant economic and environmental costs” p. 12.

pp. 12–15

Timescaping: memory and future visions

Space. Fear of destruction led bodies like the IAEA to build global surveillance that collapses borders. The Blue Marble gave “a view from nowhere” (Grevsmühl), what Hannah Arendt called “earth alienation”, the hallmark of modern science n. 6 (the Anthropocene reverses this into a view “from the inside”; Latour's “earthlings” versus “Modern Humans”) pp. 12–13.

Time. Jeremy Rifkin: “Time is our window onto the world. With time we create order.” Historians' categories: Koselleck's “horizon of expectation”; François Hartog's “regime of historicity”, the future-oriented modern regime being coercive enough to be an “order of time” p. 13.

“… The term ‘order’ implies at once succession and command: the times (in the plural) dictate or defy, time avenges wrongs, it restores order following a disruption, or sees justice done.”

Hartog 2015, p. 1 · quoted p. 13

Most useful for the volume is Barbara Adam's timescape: many forms of time coexisting, physical and cultural temporalities entangled, time impossible to grasp without space and matter. Jasanoff's sociotechnical imaginaries are “collectively held, institutionally stabilised and publicly performed visions of desirable futures”, and media spread scientists' visions (Nieto-Galàn). So far, visions of the future have been “the only expression of the technological footprint on the cultural frameworks of time” pp. 13–14.

These chapters tie visions of the future to past and present, and space to time, and ask whether Hiroshima and Nagasaki reconfigured the modern regime of historicity. They focus on the US, France and Japan to avoid reifying the nuclear order (Hughes). As mass bombing of cities, atomic bombing raised no moral issue in 1945, and the shift from German to Japanese targets drew no serious objection (Bernstein 1995). Were the bombs local operations, or “a deep, unsettling fear of the future”? Witnesses spoke at once of the “dawn of a new era”, the “nuclear age” p. 14.

  • Bernadette Bensaude-Vincent: catastrophic visions did not erode the promise of progress; nuclear technology nurtured both. “A strikingly ambivalent order of time emerged to allow the promise of a ‘bright future’ atop the ruins of atomic bombings.” p. 14
  • Ran Zwigenberg (spelled “Zwingenberg” once in the text): why did Japan, a victim of atomic bombing, embrace nuclear power? Atoms for Peace combined memory culture with modernisation. Many in Hiroshima and the anti-weapons movement wanted modern comforts, and Hiroshima became “a symbolic sanctuary dedicated to world peace” pp. 14–15.
  • Scott Gabriel Knowles: memorials centre on “events”, but nuclear disasters span many timescales. His concept is the “slow disaster”, in which risks and fears are known long before and after any single event. Museums, filmmakers, artists and citizens work “in a fog of uncertainty” p. 15.
pp. 15–16

What now? Open questions for further research

Can we predict the end of the nuclear age? Apart from medical radionuclides, nuclear technologies have always been controversial. Arms protesters have never softened. But environmental movements have tempered their opposition to nuclear power since the 1990s as zero-carbon policy favoured it: “an uneasy moral balance when the slow disaster of nuclear fallout and the slow disaster of climate change compete” p. 15.

The technology is aging. First-generation reactors are being decommissioned while EPRs and “advanced” reactors arrive. Even “atom-free” countries face decades of decommissioning and still need radionuclides, and waste outlives political regimes. “Nuclear technologies are here—not forever, but for a duration that exceeds our power of anticipation.” The world “cannot be denuclearised by political decisions alone” p. 15.

The editors' conclusion

The question is how to fit social and political timeframes to the “inexorable lifetimes of radioactive materials”. Storing or burying waste is complicated by toxicity. “Isolating the technosphere from the biosphere is utopian thinking”; life adapts to radionuclides, as Chernobyl's wildlife shows. “Separation is not an option, and so adaptation becomes the only possibility.” pp. 15–16

Abolition divides the world. The Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons passed the UN in July 2017 with 122 votes in favour and entered into force in January 2021. ICAN, working with Hiroshima and Nagasaki survivors, won the 2017 Nobel Peace Prize. Yet no nuclear-weapon state supports it, “Not even Japan” (n. 7: 54 ratifications by June 2021), and it ignores civilian technology p. 16.

Nuclear technology is also the best marker for the start of the Anthropocene: geologists chose the plutonium from mid-century tests because it is man-made, planetary and long-lasting enough for the geological timescale. The threat is “Not only a nuclear apocalypse … but also the slow disaster of the technology's ongoing, mundane uses”, part of an emerging “adaptive Anthropocene” linking warming, global health, ecological crisis and the nuclear order p. 16.

Reading 2 · Institutional history · Johns Hopkins University Press, 2022 · Chapter 1 · pp. 16–30

One World or None Elisabeth Roehrlich, Inspectors for Peace: A History of the International Atomic Energy Agency

Chicago citationRoehrlich, Elisabeth. “One World or None.” In Inspectors for Peace: A History of the International Atomic Energy Agency, 16–30. Baltimore: Johns Hopkins University Press, 2022.

The chapter in one breath

This is the IAEA's prehistory. Wells's 1914 novel imagined atomic war followed by world government. After Hiroshima, scientists took up the slogan “One World or None”, and control of nuclear energy and a peaceful world order became “twin goals”. Disarmament had a long record of failure: victor-imposed (Carthage, Versailles) or voluntary but never verified (the Hague, the League, Washington, Kellogg–Briand). The bomb forced states to think about verified disarmament. But since there was “no precedent for the prohibition of an entire form of energy generation”, every plan tried to share peaceful uses while denying military ones. At the UN Atomic Energy Commission (1946–52), the Baruch Plan (a supranational IADA, no veto, sanctions) met the Gromyko plan (ban and destroy bombs first, inspect later, keep the veto). It deadlocked and died after the Soviet test of 1949. Roehrlich rejects reading it as mere propaganda. The talks fixed a dual mandate, the consensus that all states should have peaceful access, and early safeguards thinking. “The IAEA would be born from such thinking.”

Opening: Wells, Bohr and “One World or None” Unheaded opening · epigraph

“… From the first they had to see the round globe as one problem; it was impossible any longer to deal with it piece by piece. They had to secure it universally from any fresh outbreak of atomic destruction … On this capacity to grasp and wield the whole round globe their existence depended.”

H. G. Wells, The World Set Free, 1914 (epigraph) · p. 16

Wells published The World Set Free in 1914, the year the First World War began. Set in the 1950s, it describes a global atomic war that kills millions and destroys cities. Wells was the first to coin the notion of atomic bombs, three decades before the first explosion (16 July 1945), and imagined them thrown from airplanes. More significant, after the war the world's leaders create a global authority to secure peace, and civilian nuclear energy becomes “a blessing” pp. 16–17.

Thirty years later real physicists reached similar conclusions. In 1944 Niels Bohr tried to persuade Roosevelt and Churchill to tell Stalin about the bomb and open talks on postwar arms control. He failed. After Hiroshima (6 August 1945) and Nagasaki (9 August) scientists' calls for international control “found powerful expression in the slogan ‘One World or None’”. Intellectuals and journalists joined in, making world government “an enormously important cultural leitmotif” of the postwar years p. 17.

Roehrlich's aim

The chapter recounts the IAEA's prehistory: how control of nuclear energy and a peaceful world order became “twin goals” of postwar reconstruction and how this played out in the young UN. Early plans had all states benefiting from peaceful uses while weapons were banned. The General Assembly's first resolution created a commission “to deal with the problems raised by the discovery of atomic energy”. The UN Atomic Energy Commission (UNAEC) was dissolved “in 1951” (p. 17; p. 28 gives 11 January 1952) after fruitless talks, but it showed that intergovernmental organisations, not just treaties or summits, “could be institutions of choice” against the nuclear threat. She asks why the weapons brought states together, how these talks differed from earlier disarmament, and what they had to do with the start of the Cold War pp. 17–18.

pp. 18–20

Building the Bomb

  • Discovery. Wilhelm Conrad Röntgen discovered X-rays (November 1895). Weeks later Henri Becquerel, working with uranium salts, observed what Marie Słodowska Curie and Pierre Curie named “radioactivity”: the spontaneous decay of unstable nuclei, releasing radiation. “A new form of energy—nuclear energy—had been discovered.” Medical uses set off a worldwide uranium hunt compared to the gold rush, and journals created an international community of nuclear physicists p. 18.
  • Fission. In late 1938 Lise Meitner, Vienna-born, Stockholm-based, who had fled the Nazis in Berlin, and her nephew Otto Frisch were the first to explain fission conclusively (Fermi, Hahn and Strassmann had observed it). They borrowed “fission” from cell biology: a neutron splits a nucleus, releasing two or three neutrons that split others in a chain reaction. This needs a critical mass and, to sustain it, a moderator such as graphite or heavy water p. 18.
  • Power. A 1-gigawatt coal plant needs 80 train cars of coal a day; a nuclear plant needs fuel once a year, filling half a train car. Uncontrolled, the same reaction makes “incredibly powerful explosives” p. 19.
  • Secrecy. In early 1939, with war imminent, states restricted publication to disrupt international exchange and protect militarily valuable knowledge p. 19.

On 16 July 1945 near Alamogordo the US tested a plutonium implosion bomb, the Nagasaki type. Three days before Nagasaki it dropped an untested uranium bomb on Hiroshima. The two bombs killed over 200,000 people p. 20.

pp. 20–28

International Inspection and Control

A history of unverified disarmament · pp. 20–21

Voluntary cooperation on arms is “a relatively recent phenomenon”. Historically, disarmament was imposed on the defeated by the victors. After the Second Punic War (218–201 BC) Carthage had to dissolve its army and give Rome all but ten ships and its war elephants. The Treaty of Versailles (1919) is a recent example p. 20.

  • The Crimean War (1853–56), the first “industrial war”, reported to mass audiences by telegraph and photography, prompted the first international peace movement. Pacifists including Bertha von Suttner (Nobel Peace Prize 1905) attended the Hague conferences of 1899 and 1907, called by Tsar Nicholas II mainly for his own ideological, economic and strategic ends. The only result was a ban on poison gas and “projectiles the sole object of which was asphyxiation”, with no control regime; the US never ratified it p. 20.
  • The League of Nations (1919): disarmament was its “greatest crusade and its greatest disappointment”, because states saw it as limiting sovereignty and withheld information on their weapons p. 21.
  • The Washington Conference (1921 and 1922, at US prompting): France, Britain, Italy, Japan and the US limited battleships, tonnage and submarines, again without verification or enforcement p. 21.
  • The Kellogg–Briand Pact (1928, US Secretary of State Frank B. Kellogg and French Foreign Minister Aristide Briand) outlawed war as “an instrument of national policy”. It had over 60 signatories by 1939, no enforcement, and did not prevent war p. 21.
Roehrlich's pattern

Across modern disarmament, “controversy over the sovereign rights of states and the rejection of verification and enforcement measures always hampered implementation.” Unlike in Wells, the new world organisation came before the bomb: the UN Charter was signed at San Francisco in June 1945, two months before Hiroshima p. 21.

Why the atom was different · pp. 21–22

The bomb “prompted states to think more seriously about verified disarmament and enforcement”, but optimism about peaceful uses complicated the task. Radioactivity and fission resembled the chemical industry and dynamite, with civil and military uses. Alfred Nobel imagined a weapon so powerful it would end war, “an early expression of the logic of military deterrence”. Influenced by von Suttner, he founded the Nobel Prizes, first awarded in 1901 pp. 21–22.

Roehrlich's key point

“There was no precedent for the prohibition of an entire form of energy generation, so the international attempts to confront the challenges created by the nuclear age tried to balance the sharing of and access to nuclear technologies and materials on one hand, while denying and restricting it on the other.” This held even in 1945, when no peaceful reactor yet operated p. 22.

Declarations, 1945 · pp. 22–23

  • 15 November 1945, Three-Nation Declaration on Atomic Energy (Canada, UK, US): prevent “the use of atomic energy for destructive purposes”, promote peaceful ends, create a UN commission to propose “the elimination from national armaments of atomic weapons” and “effective safeguards by way of inspection and other means”, and share no certain knowledge until “effective, reciprocal, and enforceable safeguards” exist. So the UN was imagined as “the custodian of international nuclear safeguards”. The word safeguards came from the 1919 Paris Peace Conference: a League “to provide safeguards against war” p. 22.
  • 27 December 1945, a US–UK–Soviet communiqué (Byrnes, Bevin, Molotov): the UNAEC would be a commission of the General Assembly but report to the Security Council, with the Council's members plus Canada. The Soviets insisted the Council, with its veto for the permanent five (France, China, USSR, UK, US), have the final say. Already at Dumbarton Oaks (1944) they had wanted a security body relying on great-power unanimity pp. 22–23.

The Acheson–Lilienthal Report, March 1946 · pp. 23–24

Only after committing publicly to the UNAEC did the US set up a committee under Under Secretary of State Dean Acheson, with a board of consultants led by David E. Lilienthal (chairman of the Tennessee Valley Authority). Oppenheimer drafted most of the Report on the International Control of Atomic Energy. A mere pledge would not stop states, as Kellogg–Briand showed. Civil and military uses rely on the same materials and technology, so an Atomic Development Authority should own and control all nuclear materials, including uranium and thorium mines, and all “dangerous” technology, a list expected to change. Inspection of what stayed national was proposed, but inspections alone were not enough p. 23:

“The presence of a large number of ‘foreigners’ necessarily having special privileges and immunities inquiring intimately and generally into industrial and mining operations would be attended by serious social frictions.”

Acheson–Lilienthal Report, one of the “human factors” of control · p. 23

Roehrlich adds, looking ahead, that IAEA inspectors “were not neutral, interchangeable agents of an international bureaucracy, but individuals” with their own expertise and politics, facing equally diverse technicians and officials p. 23.

The authors thought some civil uses were “within close reach of actuality” but showed “little exuberance”. They neither banned nor celebrated nuclear energy, and valued the peaceful atom as an asset for a security regime: banning all research would go against human curiosity and attract the wrong inspectors. “The difficulty of recruiting enforcement officers having only a negative and policing function … is obvious. Such a job lacks any dynamic qualities. It does not appeal to imagination.” So disarmament needed “not only a stick, but also a carrot—civilian nuclear science and technology”, an idea central to the IAEA p. 24.

The Baruch Plan against the Gromyko plan · pp. 24–26

The UNAEC opened on 14 June 1946 at the UN's temporary headquarters, a former Sperry Corporation building at Lake Success, New York (photograph, p. 25). The US was represented by Bernard Baruch, Truman's personal choice, a “Wall Street speculator and Democratic Party financier”, not a nuclear expert, 75 years old and a veteran of the War Industries Board. Britain's Sir Alexander Cadogan found him “a dreadful type of talking American … quite impossible to work with” p. 24.

The Baruch Plan followed Acheson–Lilienthal (“no system of safeguards that can be devised will of itself provide an effective guarantee”) but added a “sharper political edge”: no veto, and sanctions. Implemented, it would have put nuclear energy under a supranational body able to override member states pp. 24–25. Gromyko, speaking in English, said humanity “stands at the threshold of a wide application of atomic energy for peaceful purposes” pp. 25–26.

The 1946–48 sessions were dominated by the veto and by which came first, control or prohibition. For the Soviets, inspections on their territory were “anathema”. Secretly building a bomb since 1943, they argued publicly that the US bombs must go first p. 26.

What both sides agreed

Despite the differences, the US and USSR agreed that any future nuclear body would have a dual mandate of promoting civilian uses while regulating military uses, the foundation of all later negotiations. Attlee had considered barring Germany and Japan from all nuclear technology in late 1945, but that never became mainstream. “Thus the only uncontroversial proposition of the UNAEC negotiations was that all states should have access to the peaceful applications of nuclear technologies.” p. 26

Mexico's Luis Padilla Nervo proposed a Scientific and Technical Committee on safeguards, seen as technical. With the Soviets taking part, it unanimously adopted a report on detecting and preventing diversion at declared and undeclared facilities and on detecting clandestine activity. In the Security Council the Soviets vetoed it p. 26.

In 1947 the Soviets proposed keeping nuclear activities national, with an “International Control Commission” tied to the Council making periodic inspections and “special investigations” of suspected violations. Bertrand Goldschmidt, the French physicist and later an IAEA veteran, called this in hindsight a “forerunner to the NPT”. The US found it unable to detect clandestine programmes, rejecting in 1947 safeguards it would back thirty years later in the NPT. Goldschmidt: it then seemed “completely unrealistic” to expect any nation to renounce the bomb “without any assurance that all nations will be prevented from using them”. Behind the scenes the British had a similar proposal leaving activities in national hands; the US rejected it before it reached the UNAEC p. 27.

Oppenheimer, looking back

He reportedly lost all hope the day Baruch was appointed. In June 1950, talking to a UN Security Council Affairs official, he said scientists had been relegated “to a secondary place” after “the ‘hierarchy’ took over” in 1945–46. The shock had faded and the momentum was lost. He dismissed peaceful-atom optimism as “loose talk” with “too many vague hopes of a paradise round a corner”, yet saw the UN as one of “the instruments that can bridge the gulf”. Inspection and control were “much more a political than a scientific problem” pp. 27–28.

The Soviet veto of the committee's report proved him right. After the Soviet test in August 1949 the UNAEC's premises no longer existed. On 11 January 1952 it was dissolved and merged with the Commission for Conventional Armaments into the UN Disarmament Commission. “Disarmament” was then an umbrella term; from the late 1950s it split into disarmament (abolition), arms control (reduction) and nonproliferation (limiting spread). In October 1952 Britain became the third nuclear state; a month later the US tested the first thermonuclear bomb, several hundred times more powerful. Since then there have been over 2,000 nuclear test explosions p. 28.

pp. 28–30

Cold War at the United Nations

From 1945 to 1949 the US was the sole nuclear power. The Soviet test set the superpower dichotomy, and the arms race became central to East–West rivalry. “The very notion of a ‘cold war’ stems from the entanglement of superpower dynamics and nuclear development”: before Walter Lippmann popularised the term in 1947, Baruch's adviser Herbert B. Swope had spoken of a “cold war” in the UNAEC talks. The UN quickly became a Cold War battleground p. 28.

Nuclear weapons were thought exceptional, but decisions about them were tied to other conflicts: Berlin and the division of Germany, and the Korean War as the first Cold War proxy war in Asia. US testing continued, and the US even invited all UNAEC members to watch tests at Bikini Atoll p. 29.

Was the UNAEC just propaganda and failure? Ten years on, legal scholar David F. Cavers imagined historians seeing its “ritualistic character”, proposals made to be rejected, like “medieval morality plays or even Punch and Judy”. Most scholarship focuses on US–Soviet rivalry. Campbell Craig and Sergey Radchenko argue the Baruch Plan was “one of America's first acts of the Cold War”, not a serious attempt at order. With Soviet espionage coming to light, the US public would never have accepted sharing with a state that spied on it. They compare it to the Marshall Plan (1948): both put the Soviets in a no-win position (accept and the US wins; reject and be blamed for rejecting peace). The Soviets in turn demanded the US give up its bombs, to look like the true peace-lover, also expecting rejection p. 29.

Roehrlich's verdict

“The propagandist aspects of the UNAEC negotiations are evident, but it would be too simplistic to dismiss the negotiations as little more than rote exchanges among Cold War rivals.” Some officials, scientists and experts found the talks useful for thinking through instruments of a future order, such as safeguards pp. 29–30.

UN staff also studied models for an atomic organisation. Bodies “only technical and administrative” (the Universal Postal Union, the International Telecommunication Union) and those “neither political nor operational” (WHO, ILO) seemed unsuitable. They looked at those with “some political or operational aspects”: the International Civil Aviation Organization and the IMF p. 30.

The Cold War narrative also misses North–South and producer interests: Belgium (colonial power in the Congo) and Brazil backed the Baruch Plan only if uranium and thorium still in the ground would not become IADA property p. 30.

Delegates went on to careers in disarmament, arms control, nonproliferation, science and activism, and to the IAEA. They learned the obstacles and met foreign colleagues. “By default, they decided that nuclear energy should be further explored. What was theoretically thinkable—banning not only the military but also the civilian development of nuclear technologies—seemed neither realistic nor desirable. The IAEA would be born from such thinking.” p. 30

The article in one breath

Introduction

In March 1954 the 15-megaton Castle Bravo test at Bikini Atoll was far larger than planned fn 1 (the first of six Castle shots, on 1 March, and the first US test of a weaponised H-bomb). It contaminated more than 200 Marshall Islanders and more than 20 US personnel, including on the Navy tanker Patapsco. On 11 March the AEC called it a “routine” test with all affected now well. Then the Japanese fishing boat Daigo Fukuryū Maru (Lucky Dragon), about 70 km outside the exclusion zone, reached Yaizu on 14 March: all 23 crew had radiation sickness and one later died fn 2 (Aikichi Kuboyama). The “tuna panic” ruined a vital industry fn 3. Two years after the occupation ended, Japan felt itself a victim of US nuclear weapons for a second time, and Bravo became a diplomatic flashpoint fn 4 (the US paid $2 million in January 1955 to settle the crew and fishing claims) pp. 58–59.

At a press conference at the end of March, AEC chairman Lewis Strauss disputed where the Fukuryū Maru had been, hinting at espionage, which angered Japan further. His off-the-cuff remark that the bomb could devastate a city the size of New York alarmed Americans already uneasy about raised radiation levels fn 5 p. 59.

Kraft's thesis

Bravo was called the “shot that made the world fallout-conscious” fn 6 (Daniel Lang of The New Yorker). “In reality, the emergence of fallout as an issue of national and international concern in the mid-to-late 1950s was a slower and more complex process, one in which scientists played a leading role.” Governments wanted fallout data for their own purposes but above all to manage public information and protect their programmes. Dissident scientists fostered debate and “redefined weapons tests as a radiological threat to public safety”. Heeding Einstein's call to take science to the “village square” fn 7, British (and US) critics “faced considerable obstacles”. The issue offers insight into “the possibility of dissident knowledge in the context of Cold War science”, echoing Jessica Wang's “uneasy, ambivalent cultural space” fn 8 pp. 59–60.

Taylor, Wittner, Grant and Nehring explain British anti-nuclear feeling and protest fn 9; Greta Jones and Christoph Laucht study scientists in British nuclear politics fn 10. What remains is how dissenting scientists brought fallout into the spotlight in the thermonuclear age p. 60. Why Britain? It was the third nuclear power but not a superpower. The controversy struck just as it was striving to become the third thermonuclear power. Its political culture gave scientists freedom to speak but, as Jones notes, also limited criticism and curtailed collective opposition fn 11. And Britain is key to the origins of Pugwash (the Pugwash Conferences on Science and World Affairs, PCSWA, 1957) p. 61.

Fallout hit nuclear and non-nuclear states alike. Eugene Rabinowitch, founder and editor of the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, called it “universal and compulsory” fn 12. It linked the local and the global and demanded a transnational response fn 13, and for critical scientists it became “a rallying point” for reaching across Cold War divides via Pugwash. Building on US fallout history, British nuclear-state history and Pugwash histories, Kraft offers a “more systematic analysis” of scientists' debates and of the government and senior scientists' efforts to manage knowledge and rebut dissent, and of the strategies of Rotblat and Russell that led to Pugwash p. 61.

pp. 62–66

Fallout from Thermonuclear Tests: An Unclear but Present Danger

In the US, Bravo “did not disrupt” testing: Castle's other five shots went ahead in April–May 1954, and Eisenhower approved fifteen more tests (Teapot in Nevada and Wigwam off San Diego) fn 14. The AEC's report of 15 February 1955 fn 15 found the “small amount” of extra exposure not of “serious concern”. It used what became the standard defence, that fallout was less of a hazard than natural background or medical radiation, and it stressed security: “fallout is much less dangerous than falling behind the Russians”; “the degree of risk must be balanced against the great importance of the test program” fn 16 p. 62.

Senior scientists legitimated this fn 17: Willard Libby (AEC commissioner 1954–59, involved in Projects Gabriel and Sunshine, begun 1949 and 1953) fn 18 and Edward Teller, both staunch H-bomb supporters fn 19. Caroline Kopp showed US positions were often rooted not in science but in politics, worldview, institution or career; Teller's and Libby's reflected AEC ties and their politics fn 20 pp. 62–63.

Critics: the Federation of American Scientists called for a UN-coordinated test moratorium fn 21. Sharper critics came from outside AEC circles: Linus Pauling, Ralph Lapp and Alfred Sturtevant fn 22 (Sturtevant's 1954 “spat” with the AEC's John Bugher over genetic danger and the treatment of Hermann Muller). Pauling, a Nobel chemist convinced fallout caused leukaemia, publicly challenged Teller and Libby fn 23 (TV debate with Teller, 1958; Nobel Peace Prize 1962). Vilified at home, he found collegiality abroad. He signed the Russell–Einstein Manifesto (1955, led by Russell and Frédéric Joliot-Curie) and in 1956, with Barry Commoner and Edward Condon, organised test-ban petitions fn 24 (2,000 US signatures in June 1957; 9,235 at the UN in January 1958). Their effect is hard to gauge, but they symbolised resistance and scientists' “instinct for international mobilization” pp. 63–64.

The AEC's rhetoric fit Eisenhower's “massive retaliation” fn 25 (NSC 162/2, 1953). Fallout slowed neither US nor Soviet testing fn 26, nor British, French or Chinese ambitions. The risk was “deemed overwhelmingly worth taking.” From March 1954 to the end of 1957 the three powers ran 115 tests fn 27, from the Pacific, Kazakhstan, the Barents Sea and the Australian desert fn 28 p. 64.

Concern spread worldwide, “not least among the nonaligned”. As early as April 1954, India's Nehru, whose country lay in the path of all three powers' fallout, led calls to suspend atmospheric tests. The pope called for an end to testing at Easter 1954 and Christmas 1955 fn 29. Responses varied with national polity and culture fn 30 (the pitfalls of “nuclear culture” as a term), but common fears ran across borders. Kai Erikson: radiation is “invisible to the senses at low doses, insidious in its latent effects”, with a “special place in the human sense of terror” fn 31. From the mid-1950s fears focused on leukaemia fn 32, especially ABCC data on the hibakusha fn 33, and on a nuclear genre from mutant aliens to Godzilla to Nevil Shute's On the Beach fn 34. Ralph Lutts: “the fall of radiation had become the modern equivalent of the fall of darkness and the stroke of midnight” fn 35 pp. 64–65.

Scientists were central on both sides, and dissenters' ability to force debate was “powerfully shaped by the specific national context” p. 66.

pp. 66–70

Bravo in Britain

Bravo came as Churchill's government weighed a British H-bomb fn 36 (Ivy Mike, November 1952, had “stunned Whitehall”). The decision, taken in July 1954, was disclosed in the 1955 Defence White Paper fn 37. Two years later Duncan Sandys's White Paper stressed thermonuclear deterrence fn 38. A 1954 amendment to the US Atomic Energy Act eased cooperation with NATO but not with Britain fn 39, so Britain had to “go it alone” to stay “at the top table” fn 40. Churchill found the H-bomb “laden with doom” fn 41 but financially and strategically attractive, “more bang for the buck”. Under Churchill, Eden and Macmillan, fallout was “a problem to be managed”. Britain built its H-bomb from 1954 to 1957 pp. 66–67.

Policy was made in secret by a few senior politicians and handpicked scientists fn 42, and the public knew little. Seeing fallout as a challenge to this secrecy, the government downplayed it. Peter Goodwin shows pressure on the BBC; Adrian Bingham shows some popular-press journalists pushing past Fleet Street's caution fn 43. Dissent was suppressed formally and informally fn 44, and Rotblat especially risked a backlash. But Bravo, a highly publicised drama, framed fallout as an international problem p. 67.

British papers covered Bravo briefly, through US responses fn 45, noting US experts' “astonishment” fn 46. In April 1954 BBC Panorama featured Russell and Rotblat on Bravo. The BBC broadcast Russell's Man's Peril in December 1954, but the government was already restricting coverage fn 47: Churchill sought to stop further H-bomb programmes and hinted at a ban by ministerial order fn 48. Officials feared that fallout from peacetime tests would blur into a generalised fear of radiation fn 49 (“radiophobia”, a Soviet coinage after Chernobyl) and turn people against nuclear weapons as such. So the government concealed both, to shield the weapons programme, protect testing, and preserve the “peaceful atom”: on 15 February 1955 it announced ten new reactors costing £300 million p. 68.

The Cabinet knew the radiological reality from briefings by William Penney and John Cockcroft; insiders had known since the Manhattan Project fn 50 (Malloy). A late-1954 briefing prompted a review fn 51 (convened by Cabinet Secretary Sir Norman Brook after Penney outlined thermonuclear war). Overseen by civil servant William Strath and completed in March 1955, the Strath Report painted a grim, post-apocalyptic Britain fn 52 (it stayed secret until about 2000). Via Cockcroft it drew on US data from Project Gabriel and the AEC's Bravo work fn 53, evidence of transatlantic networks among senior scientists and cooperation at the top fn 54. From 1955 the MRC and the US NAS also coordinated radiation studies (Cockcroft, Harold Himsworth, the AEC's Detlev Bronk) fn 55 pp. 68–69.

Kraft on Strath

Strath's message, that there could be “no defense” against the hydrogen bomb, was “considered too disturbing for the public” and kept secret, showing how much the government valued controlling public knowledge. As Hughes notes, civil defence debate was suppressed “both to promote the credibility of the nuclear deterrent and to maintain the social and political order” fn 56. Civil defence posed a dilemma: not alarming the public while preparing it for war fn 57. In private the Cabinet faced “a radioactive wasteland”; in public it downplayed fallout pp. 69–70.

pp. 70–77

Beyond Bravo: The Fallout Issue Takes Shape

Strath reached the Cabinet on 8 March 1955, in a turbulent period: the February Defence White Paper, the nuclear energy expansion (with Calder Hall under construction in Cumbria), and the AEC report of 15 February. All of this came amid heavy publicity for Atoms for Peace and the coming Geneva conference, which would showcase civic energy and the medical isotope fn 58. “Remaining silent about nuclear weapons, the British government showcased its development of the peaceful atom”, and Cockcroft painted bright atomic futures fn 59 (he won the IAEA's Atoms for Peace Prize in 1961) p. 70.

The White Paper confirmed the H-bomb fn 60 (it also nearly tripled civil defence spending to over £70 million). The Cabinet made the decision knowing the fallout problem; deterrence and the “top table” made it imperative fn 61. Some left Labour MPs (Frank Allaun, Fenner Brockway, Philip Noel-Baker, Edith Summerskill) opposed it, but the party executive endorsed the policy. Trade unions called for an end to testing fn 62 (the Allied Engineering Union in May 1954). Jones: Labour and the unions were “simply ‘holding the line’” fn 63. Later calls for unilateral disarmament spawned splinter groups fn 64: the Committee for the Abolition of Nuclear Weapons became, in November 1956, the National Council for the Abolition of Nuclear Weapons Tests (NCANWT), later merged into CND fn 65. Pacifist groups (Fellowship of Reconciliation, Women's International League for Peace and Freedom, Quakers, National Peace Council) condemned testing fn 66 p. 71.

British media received the AEC report quietly fn 67 (its publication delayed by US wrangling) fn 68 (the Guardian reprinted it and noted the absence of British data). But it became “a turning point” because scientists were incensed. Newsweek called it the “terrible truth”, the FAS called for a moratorium and Pauling organised a petition fn 69. In Britain the government stayed silent until 23 March 1955, when Rotblat “took the unprecedented step of openly challenging the AEC's findings”, calling their portrayal of danger as “insignificant” “misleading” fn 70 p. 72.

Kraft on the BBC agreement

From late 1954 the government sought closer control of BBC coverage of thermonuclear “effects” fn 71, above all “fallout”, a new term (fn 72: replacing “death dust”, “ash” and “debris”; an umbrella for many isotopes and for local-to-global spread; the BBC avoided the word). On 15 February 1955, the very day the AEC report appeared, a meeting of Whitehall and ministers (Macmillan, Norman Brook) with the BBC's Ian Jacob (director general) and Sir Alexander Cadogan (chair of governors) led the BBC to restrict H-bomb coverage and consult the government informally fn 73: “a subtle but effective means of controlling what the British public knew”. Whether it anticipated the AEC report is unclear. For the rest of 1955 BBC coverage was “minimal and uncritical and did not feature fallout” fn 74 pp. 72–73.

The press was muted (Bingham finds some Mirror and Express writers more critical) fn 75. The Anglican Church treated the H-bomb as political, not moral: Kirk Willis's “caution and acquiescence over boldness and challenge” fn 76. Dissenting clergy (the dean of Canterbury, the bishops of Exeter and Chichester, Methodist Donald Soper) were, as Dianne Kirby argues, suppressed from within and by a reluctant press fn 77. Control of public information was “absolutely central to [their] thinking about the hydrogen bomb”; the “last thing” wanted was a “consistent policy of education”, exactly Rotblat's aim fn 78 pp. 73–74.

With no effective political opposition and a quiet church, scientists became “the vanguard of opposition”. But they were divided. The Atomic Scientists' Association (ASA) had disappointed since the late 1940s fn 79; political differences undermined collective action fn 80; opponents lacked an effective organisation fn 81 (the US movement at least left the BAS) and had become onlookers fn 82 p. 75.

Postwar choices split physicists into “different worlds”. Insiders on the weapons project rose to prominence: Cockcroft, Christopher Hinton, Penney fn 83 (knighted, the “atomic knights”), a new “scientist-entrepreneur” in what Ronald Doel calls “science in black”, the secret links between scientists and officials serving the national security state fn 84. Cockcroft (Cavendish, Nobel 1951, wartime Chalk River) became director of AERE Harwell in 1946 and the public face of the peaceful programme fn 85. Outsiders like Rotblat, working on medical applications, were privy neither to decisions nor to fallout data fn 86 (Galison on classification creating unequal access) pp. 75–76.

Kraft on the peaceful/military line

“The line drawn between the peaceful and military ‘atom’ was, in one sense, artificial and born of political expediency.” Rotblat could frame “peaceful” work as ethical but could never fully separate from military uses fn 87 (British radioactive waste dumping at sea and in the Thames, per Hamblin, would be a revealing comparison). The duality of the reactor, making energy, medical isotopes and fissile material, made them inextricable. Postwar reactor investment was primarily military; peaceful uses were a “spin-off”. After December 1953 the distinction gained political weight through Atoms for Peace, “fundamentally about winning the hearts and minds of the U.S. public” during a thermonuclear build-up fn 88 pp. 76–77.

Bravo brought back the divides of 1945. Insiders rallied behind government; long-time opponents turned critical. The debate had “a striking asymmetry, of center and periphery”, pitting pro-nuclear insiders against scientists removed from Whitehall, and it “rested on scientific uncertainty” that kept it charged and, to the government's chagrin, public p. 77.

Russell and Rotblat: “We Cannot Be Silent Bystanders” Title quotes Rotblat · fn 89

Nobel laureate philosopher Bertrand Russell, alarmed by Bravo, broadcast Man's Peril on BBC radio (23 December 1954) to about six million fn 90. He believed scientists, by expertise and internationalism fn 91, had a key role, and he worked with Joliot-Curie (president of the Communist-led World Federation of Scientific Workers) fn 92 toward the summer 1955 Russell–Einstein Manifesto pp. 77–78.

Joseph Rotblat, Polish-born, met Russell on Panorama in April 1954. He had left the Manhattan Project in 1944 on learning the bomb would likely be used on Japan, not Germany, and supposedly to intimidate the USSR fn 93. He moved to Liverpool University fn 94 and by 1954 was professor of medical physics at St Bartholomew's Hospital, having turned from weapons work. His conception of social responsibility p. 78:

“Scientists cannot live in isolation from other groups that together form the world community … The ivory towers in which scientists once pretended to live have been crumbling for many years, and were finally demolished by the pressure and heat waves of the Hiroshima bomb.”

Rotblat, “A Social Conscience for the Nuclear Age” · fn 95 · p. 78

Galvanised by the AEC report, which claimed “certainty where there was none”, Rotblat analysed Bravo himself using data from Japanese biophysicist Yasushi Nishiwaki [spelled “Yashushi” in the source] fn 96. Thermonuclear weapons with a fission-fusion-fission design posed serious danger, especially genetic damage, and the cumulative effects of low-level exposure made ending tests imperative. It was also a protest against the “manipulation and suppression” of data: he called the AEC's presentation “misleading” and demanded publication p. 79.

Published in the Atomic Scientists' Journal (March 1955) and the BAS fn 97, it was his first major public intervention. The press reported it guardedly: the Manchester Guardian ran “Genetic damage from H-bomb experiments: Scientist demands publication of data” fn 98. The US press stressed his criticism of the AEC fn 99. As a radiation biology expert he “could not readily be dismissed”: “we are sailing much closer to the wind than many of us thought”; “until we know better we must accept the most pessimistic estimate. There is too much at stake to risk any other approach” fn 100 pp. 79–80.

Kraft on Rotblat's impact

He was “lambasted in the House of Lords, especially by Churchill's scientific adviser, Lord Cherwell” fn 101 (Frederick Lindemann, seen as arrogant and too influential). The vehemence reflected his effect: fallout and testing became a major political issue, and bomb tests were recast “as a sinister threat not only to people's own health but to the health of their children”. He questioned the government's veracity and undermined trust in radiation-safety bodies, which embarrassed Anglo-American relations and discomfited an AEC already under fire over Nevada downwind contamination fn 102 p. 80.

He spoke alone because working through the ASA was impossible. Like P. M. S. Blackett before him, he was criticised by colleagues (W. G. Marley, Penney) and became suspect in Whitehall fn 103 (inside dissent came only in the 1960s, with Solly Zuckerman protected by his friendship with Lord Mountbatten, the “Zuckbatten axis”). In summer 1955 Whitehall called him “rather wild” and steered the BBC away from him toward someone with the government's “full confidence” fn 104. He later admitted that “in defying the government (he) had considerably narrowed the possibilities of my influencing public opinion again”, called his report “whistle-blowing” from “public duty”, and having “blotted (his) copybook with the Establishment” was watched and censured fn 105 p. 81.

Russell, a staunch anti-Communist, mobilised scientists. The Russell–Einstein Manifesto, signed by Einstein, Joliot-Curie, Rotblat, Pauling and others, warned of the H-bomb's “universal peril” and radiological poisoning, including the “deadly dust” of peacetime tests. It urged a new East–West dialogue and “a new way of thinking”: “remember your humanity and forget the rest” fn 106 (a week later the German-speaking Mainau declaration) pp. 81–82.

At home they used public lectures, for example through cooperative societies and extramural departments fn 107 (C. F. Powell, Central Hall, February 1955; Rotblat, 1957). Letters pages and MPs' mailbags showed growing opposition. A mother wrote to the Guardian (March 1957): “no longer can we read the scientists' warnings and turn away” fn 108. Fallout entered Parliament through Labour and Liberal MPs fn 109 (Allaun, Barbara Castle, Joe Grimond). A spring 1955 exchange fn 110 (debate opened by Summerskill, 29 March 1955) led the prime minister to ask the MRC to review radiation hazards pp. 82–83.

Two reports frame the rest: the MRC's The Hazards to Man of Nuclear and Allied Radiations (June 1956) fn 111 and Rotblat's group's “Radiostrontium Statement” (April 1957) on Sr-90, suspected of causing leukaemia and bone sarcoma fn 112, amid growing international pressure against Britain's first H-bomb test p. 83.

pp. 83–88

The MRC Report, June 1956: The Government Position on Fallout—and Testing

Commissioning it answered parliamentary and public concern and let the government be seen “taking control”, with the appearance of openness. The committee fn 113 (chaired by MRC Secretary Sir Harold Himsworth, with Cockcroft, John F. Loutit, C. H. Waddington, Stanley Mitchell, Lionel Penrose, Austin Bradford Hill) mixed nuclear and medical establishments; its “conservative composition” made an unwelcome outcome unlikely. Fallout was a “negligible hazard”, and “at present” tests gave “no cause for alarm” pp. 83–84.

Kraft's reading of the MRC report

“The MRC report was a carefully crafted manifesto for nuclear energy.” It opened: “the future development of our civilization is closely bound up with the exploitation of nuclear energy.” It noted “widespread public concern” but set testing within exciting new industries. It admitted uncertainty about Sr-90 while reassuring that research was under way p. 84.

Military and non-military atoms were “two sides of the same coin”. Calder Hall, the first nuclear power station, went online in October 1956 and was celebrated, while its role in producing plutonium for weapons was “less well publicized”. Britain also led a new medical isotope industry for cancer fn 114. By presenting weapons testing's risks as “essentially the same” as the peaceful atom's, the report made the dangers “minimal” and the programme a “balanced portfolio”: risks “controllable within acceptable limits” fn 115 (both Doel's “science in black” and Chilton's “nukespeak”) pp. 84–85.

The report also “functioned to define the boundary between science and politics”. Critics were cast as crossing from the lab into politics, “an argument not made with regard to those who invoked the report in defense of the nuclear agenda” p. 85.

A parallel US study under the NAS, funded by the Rockefeller Foundation, was “heavily influenced by the AEC” fn 116 (contentious, with confrontations among scientists). The MRC and NAS reports were published simultaneously on 12 June 1956 with similar conclusions fn 117, a “remarkable show of transatlantic cooperation”, timed amid the US Redwing and British Mosaic tests fn 118 (17 and 2 shots) pp. 85–86.

  • Genetic and leukaemia dangers negligible, but both were more circumspect on Sr-90 fn 119 (geneticists more worried, stressing cumulative low doses).
  • Behind the scenes the MRC and AERE ran a nationwide Sr-90 study of children's teeth and infant and adult bones, the bones taken postmortem via doctors and pathologists fn 120 (the Agricultural Research Council studied soil, cattle and sheep bones in the rainy, low-calcium uplands of Wales and Scotland).
  • Both favoured the “threshold” concept, so that some exposure counted as safe, important for industry fn 121 (yet they led the ICRP to lower occupational limits), and compared risks to background and a chest X-ray.
  • Hamblin: both bodies were “keenly aware of the crisis in confidence that would occur if the independent reports arrived at significantly different conclusions” fn 122. Divergence could fuel fear, calls to end testing, and erode support for civil energy, given investment and competition with the USSR fn 123 pp. 86–87.

Not everyone was reassured. The Manchester Guardian ran a David Low cartoon of umbrella-wielding nannies clutching the MRC report to shield infants from Sr-90 falling like rain, among mushroom clouds and a sinister nursery rhyme. Rotblat, Russell and Pauling were dismayed: low-level radiation's dangers could not yet be adjudicated fn 124 (threshold versus linearity unresolved). Sr-90 was the most feared fn 125, a “bone-seeker” irradiating marrow and raising leukaemia risk, dreaded because it struck children and was fatal fn 126. Letters and mailbags linked fallout and leukaemia fn 127. Physician Ronald Bodley Scott: leukaemia was “a pestilence of the atomic age” fn 128 p. 87.

Sr-90 tracking also helped conceive the planet as an interconnected ecosystem fn 129 (Higuchi). Sr-90 was the first pollutant Rachel Carson discussed in Silent Spring (1962) fn 130. In 1956 its danger was contested, and Rotblat, “perhaps sensing an opportunity”, turned to it, his second decisive intervention pp. 87–88.

pp. 88–91

April 1957: A Statement about Sr-90

The ASA's Radiation Hazards Committee was one of four study groups formed in autumn 1956 fn 131 (with Disarmament, Scientific Social Responsibility, and Formation of an International Body of Scientists). Members were radiobiologists Jack Boag and Patricia Lindop and radiologist Sidney Osborn; Rotblat was the driving force. Its “Statement on Radiostrontium” fn 132 challenged the MRC, forecast leukaemia cases at current test rates and stressed children's sensitivity. “If technical details might be lost on the lay public, the threat of cancer, especially in children, would not.” Cumulative exposure made stopping tests imperative pp. 88–89.

Timed to coincide with preparations for Britain's first thermonuclear test, it was circulated within the ASA per protocol, exposing tensions. Osborn urged letting the government see it first fn 133. Alexander Haddow (leukaemia specialist, Chester Beatty Research Institute) thought fallout no major hazard and ASA comment inappropriate fn 134. Sharper opposition came from the “nuclear establishment”: Marley (Harwell, on the Strath Committee) and Nobel physicist George P. Thomson (a MAUD Committee veteran) wanted ASA policy based on the MRC report and saw the H-bomb as vital fn 135 p. 89.

Released on 16 April 1957, it appeared under the Radiation Hazard Committee, not the ASA, apparently at Cockcroft's suggestion fn 136. It got scant press coverage; Rotblat hoped the New Scientist, BMJ and Lancet would reach “the intelligent layman” fn 137 (the BAS reprint carried a Vicky cartoon on “tit for tat” testing). In Whitehall it proved “incendiary”, bringing Cherwell's wrath again fn 138 and criticism from Himsworth and Penney fn 139 pp. 89–90.

Rotblat was “unrepentant” as evidence mounted: ABCC epidemiology and studies of irradiated patients linked radiation and leukaemia fn 140 (birth-defect evidence too), though Libby downplayed Sr-90's link fn 141 (caesium-137 and carbon-14 also worried). In Science (May 1957) Edward B. Lewis supported a linear dose–leukaemia relationship, no safe dose fn 142, challenging the threshold fn 143. MRC circles dismissed it as “armchair speculation”, but it emboldened dissenters, and calls for a test ban grew fn 144 (in September 1957 the US ran its first underground test, Plumbbob Rainier, 1.7 kt, while the superpowers pursued “clean bombs") p. 90.

This hit just before Grapple X, Britain's first thermonuclear test, criticised worldwide for fallout and proliferation. Wayland Young (NCANWT) in the Guardian (March 1957): next “it will be France, then perhaps half a dozen countries”; “is it not our special duty as the first second-rate Power on the scene to take an initiative in stopping the whole thing … as terrifying as all worthwhile initiatives always are?” fn 145. Unlike the superpowers, which became thermonuclear before fallout was recognised fn 146 (though Redwing in 1956 drew opposition from India and Japan), Britain developed the weapon knowing its radiological dangers pp. 90–91.

pp. 91–94

From Spring to Autumn, 1957: Countdown to Grapple X

In early 1957 the new Macmillan government announced spring and autumn tests, with Grapple X set for November on Christmas Island fn 147 (Grapple: three shots in May–June; Antler: three in September–October). Rotblat's push for an ASA call to cancel failed fn 148. Labour backed him, calling on 3 and 17 April 1957 for a ban on H-bomb tests and postponement of the British test. Unlike 1954–56, testing and fallout now got wide, critical coverage; polls showed growing opposition fn 149, and foreign opposition to the UK tests rose fn 150 (Japanese scientists' appeal to British physicists, February 1957) pp. 91–92.

The climate turned confrontational. In 1957 Lord Cherwell and Foreign Secretary Selwyn Lloyd campaigned against activists. Lloyd called Rotblat a “fellow traveler” on BBC radio's Woman's Hour (May) fn 151. Rotblat was undeterred; J. B. Priestley urged moral leadership and unilateral disarmament fn 152 (a spur to CND) p. 92.

Macmillan was unmoved, stressing deterrence fn 153 (a test ban as a first step to slow the arms race, argued by Philip Inglis in 1954 and Waddington; Britain would consider it only after becoming thermonuclear). Britain would change testing only within a three-power moratorium fn 154. Abandoning the test would “put Britain in a position of inferiority, even for the purposes of negotiation” fn 155. H-bomb status also served Macmillan's “great prize”: rapprochement with the United States. So news of the Windscale fire (8 October 1957) was withheld for a time in both countries fn 156. Sputnik 1, the same week, added justification. Grapple X was detonated on 8 November 1957 fn 157 pp. 92–93.

Afterwards public anti-nuclear sentiment became organised protest, above all CND, a distinctive moment when middle-class activists and women mobilised fn 158. CND foregrounded fallout, a test ban and an end to the arms race fn 159. Russell and Rotblat helped found it; Rotblat left its committee in late 1958 for Bart's and, by then, Pugwash p. 93.

Kraft on the limits of British dissent

British dissenters faced nothing like Truman's “Loyalty Order” or HUAC, but they did face colleagues' criticism and Whitehall's personal attacks. Rotblat: “even in a democracy like Britain, the Establishment has powerful means to restrict dissident views” fn 160. The politically impotent ASA left them no channel at home or abroad. Even the conservative Haddow lamented in June 1957 the lack of an “international medium” for “our scientific responsibility” fn 161. From this mix of constraint and freedom came “a novel transnational initiative organized by scientists, for scientists”: Pugwash pp. 93–94.

pp. 94–98

Pugwash, Nova Scotia, July 1957: Scientists Launch a Transnational Initiative

Russell, with Joliot-Curie and Rotblat, planned a meeting of senior scientists for January 1957 in Delhi, inviting across the political spectrum. Haddow, who had declined to sign the Manifesto in 1955, declined again in June 1956, partly because he saw the UN as the right vehicle fn 162 (he had backed a UN “Scientific Concilium”; the UN's 1955 UNSCEAR had a different remit) p. 94.

The invitation shows the strategy: fallout dominated, and independence was essential because “the work of a commission of official delegates may sometimes be restricted by political considerations” fn 163 (signed by Russell, Born, Joliot-Curie, Infeld, Pauling, Powell, Rotblat and Yukawa), perhaps a veiled reference to the MRC fn 164 (or UNSCEAR, whose 1958 report did deem fallout a hazard). Scientists would not represent governments but follow “their own consciences”, gaining “a wider and more ready acceptance by public opinion”. A key theme: tests “contributing to a competition in armaments” p. 95.

Finances and the Suez crisis moved it to Pugwash, Nova Scotia, July 1957, funded by industrialist Cyrus Eaton fn 165. It gathered 22 senior scientists from ten countries fn 166: invitation only, elite, under Chatham House rules. Though no Soviet or Chinese scientist had signed the Manifesto, both countries were represented fn 167. It began the practice of a summary statement sent to governments. The first (1957) came from three committees: radiation hazards; controls and safeguards; social responsibility fn 168 pp. 95–96.

Kraft on fallout and Pugwash

“Fallout served as a rallying point for a new form of transnational activism.” It dominated the Manifesto, drove Pugwash's inception and topped its first statement, because it crossed borders and blocs. The New Republic: “an era of radiation without representation”. Japanese physicist Mitsuo Taketani: the “whole population of the world is being used as guinea pigs” fn 169. Fallout “hung as much over Moscow as it did over London and Washington”, yet came from tests states called vital, setting nuclear states against the international community. Pugwash claimed that terrain for the Manifesto's “new way of thinking” p. 96.

Rotblat: “Dissidence can be said to be part of our ethical code” fn 170. Fallout forged that code, an ethical case against testing through which scientists showed social responsibility to present and future publics pp. 96–97.

Fallout alarmed governments and citizens everywhere and “was arguably decisive in rendering possible and legitimating” a meeting across Cold War divides. “Fallout was a necessary, and perhaps a sufficient, condition for its emergence.” It was national and transnational at once, and scientists, as “the foremost experts”, were “uniquely placed” to reach across borders p. 97.

Rotblat “found his métier”, presiding over Pugwash for life. British hostility followed: officials long saw it as “verging on Communist Front gatherings” fn 171, and other governments were suspicious too fn 172. Led by Rotblat, C. F. Powell, Rudolf Peierls, Rabinowitch and Aleksandr Topchiev, it became a channel for “second-track” diplomacy, its influence hard to gauge fn 173. It was recognised by the 1995 Nobel Peace Prize to Pugwash and Rotblat pp. 97–98.

pp. 98–100

Conclusion

Kraft's conclusions

Until 1954 the powers downplayed radiological danger through denial of contamination, test-site measures and secret data, while funding radiation research, a tension reconciled as “national security”. Bravo, “the first serious accident”, “laid bare the long-concealed potential for radiological catastrophe”. That it became a sustained controversy owed much to scientists: Rotblat and Russell in Britain, Pauling and Commoner in the US. “Dissident scientists ensured that fallout became a part of public life in Cold War Britain”, bringing the arms race into “the everyday lived experience of the ordinary citizen” p. 98.

The government met them with attacks on Rotblat, muted discussion, and mobilised government-aligned scientists to call fallout harmless, “rein in” dissidents and suppress collective action in bodies like the ASA. The government believed science should assist, not hinder, its Cold War priorities, and MRC panellists were “at ease with this role”. The coordinated MRC and NAS reports were “transnational cooperation between governments, senior scientists, and scientific committees driven by the imperatives of the arms race” pp. 98–99.

Fallout became part of British nuclear culture. Labour did not challenge the Conservatives, but by 1957 a wider lobby (left intellectuals, Quakers, Anglicans, pacifists) emerged, and from it came CND. Whether Russell and Rotblat consciously exploited fallout for a broader agenda is unresolved, but it served them. It lifted the “veil of secrecy”, re-engaged the public, and gave scientists a rallying point pp. 99–100.

Transnational initiatives may have seemed “the only viable path left open”. Pugwash was “a product of the Cold War context” and a pragmatic response to failure at home; “Neither a movement nor an organization in the conventional sense, it is perhaps best understood as a network of scientists”, distinctive in its network form and transnational agenda, and “a response to the failure of traditional diplomacy” p. 100.

The week on one screen

The argument in ten lines

  1. The bomb made international control look like survival itself: Wells's world authority (1914), Bohr's plea (1944), “One World or None” (1945).
  2. Past disarmament was either imposed on the defeated or never verified; nuclear weapons forced the question of verification and enforcement.
  3. Nobody could ban a whole energy source, so every plan tried to share peaceful uses and deny military ones. That is the dual mandate.
  4. At the UNAEC (1946–52) the Baruch Plan (supranational IADA, no veto, sanctions) met the Gromyko plan (ban first, veto kept); only Poland backed the Soviets.
  5. The Commission died after the 1949 Soviet test, but its safeguards thinking and its one consensus (peaceful access for all) fed straight into the IAEA.
  6. Castle Bravo (1954) exposed H-bomb fallout; the AEC answered that fallout was “much less dangerous than falling behind the Russians”.
  7. Britain, racing for its own H-bomb, managed fallout in secret: the Strath Report hidden, BBC coverage restricted from 15 February 1955, the MRC report (June 1956) calling it “negligible”.
  8. Outsider scientists broke the silence: Rotblat's Bravo analysis (1955), the Russell–Einstein Manifesto (1955), the Sr-90 statement (1957). Whitehall attacked them.
  9. Blocked at home, they turned transnational. Fallout was “a necessary, and perhaps a sufficient, condition” for Pugwash (July 1957).
  10. Seen more broadly, the nuclear order is everything nuclear that mediates experience. It was built by splitting bombs from energy, pacifying, normalising and reordering time, and it will need adaptation, not an exit.

Bensaude-Vincent, Boudia & Sato · Introduction

1How do the editors define “nuclear order”, and how does it differ from the IR usage?

“The dimensions of the nuclear domain that constitute and mediate our experience of the world”: infrastructures, cultural categories of space and time, traces, bodies, expertise. The IR usage (Scheinman, Walker, Ritchie) means a global order preventing war through deterrence and nonproliferation, centred on states and IOs. p. 3

2What puzzle drives the volume?

How such a destructive technology became central and “mundane” despite disasters and “hollow assertions” of “never again”. Fukushima has become “ordinary”, and adaptation and forgetting “can be manufactured”. p. 2

3Why do they open with the Olympic flame?

J-Village, 20 km from Daiichi, TEPCO-funded and restored with Kuwaiti money, was to launch the “Reconstruction Games”. Officials said “any difficulty can be overcome”, while 41,000 remained displaced. Recovery staged over lingering harm. p. 1

4What is the “key mechanism” of the order in “Managing violence”?

“The imposition of a clear-cut demarcation between bombs and energy.” Violence was reserved for weapons, reactors were cast as benign, and slow radiation harm was downplayed (Occupation censorship). But “nuclear violence is not bound to formal hostilities”. pp. 4–5

5What does Kyoko Sato's chapter show about hibakusha?

Standards built on hibakusha data decide who is “worthy” of certified status. Survivors used testimony and counter-expertise; thresholds based on distance from ground zero devalued lived experience, adding “symbolic violence”. p. 5

6What is the paradox of “pacifying atoms”?

The advance of knowledge and control “paradoxically also produced ignorance and blind spots”. Experts rebuilt a fear-based order on “rational foundations” and a regime of surveillance and regulation that legitimised nuclear activity. p. 8

7What are the IAEA's “twin divergent goals”?

Promoting “peaceful” uses while preventing diversion to military uses, a contradiction “never … reconciled or even officially acknowledged” (Creager and Rentetzi chapter). p. 8

8What did Weinberg mean by the “military priesthood” and “Faustian bargain”?

Weapons-control institutions had to extend to all radioactive materials. Society got cheap, clean energy in exchange for keeping expert institutions alive for ever and accepting “a defanged acknowledgement of risk”. p. 10

9What is “nuclear exceptionalism”, with examples?

Hecht's term: singularise each disaster and explain it by local, often stereotyped culture. Chernobyl = an old Soviet reactor; Fukushima = extraordinary circumstances plus “a culture of obedience”. p. 11

10Name Boudia's three governance paradigms.

Containment, risk assessment and adaptation, forged by the IAEA, ICRP and UNSCEAR. Adaptation means continuing life in “sustainably contaminated” areas after Chernobyl and Fukushima. p. 12

11What is a “timescape”, and what are Hartog's and Koselleck's terms?

Adam: many forms of time coexisting and entangled with space and matter. Hartog: “regime of historicity”, an “order of time”. Koselleck: “horizon of expectation”. pp. 13–14

12Why do the editors say “adaptation becomes the only possibility”?

Nuclear technologies last “for a duration that exceeds our power of anticipation”; waste outlives regimes; isolating the technosphere from the biosphere is “utopian”. So “Separation is not an option”. pp. 15–16

13What does the introduction say about the TPNW and the Anthropocene?

The TPNW (July 2017, 122 in favour, in force January 2021; ICAN's Nobel) is backed by no nuclear-weapon state, not even Japan, and ignores civilian technology. Geologists chose test plutonium as the Anthropocene marker: man-made, planetary, long-lasting. p. 16

Roehrlich · One World or None

14Why does the chapter begin with H. G. Wells?

The World Set Free (1914) first coined atomic bombs and imagined a global authority securing peace after atomic war, with civil nuclear energy as a blessing. That is the vision later expressed as “One World or None”. pp. 16–17

15What were the “twin goals” of postwar reconstruction?

Control over nuclear energy and the creation of an international peaceful world order. Early plans had all states benefiting from peaceful uses while weapons were banned. p. 17

16What did Bohr try in 1944?

To persuade Roosevelt and Churchill to tell Stalin about the bomb and open postwar arms-control talks. He failed. p. 17

17Explain the dual-use problem as Roehrlich presents it.

Plutonium, first produced in 1940, is reprocessed from the spent fuel of power reactors. That by-product of civil energy can make bombs, so some technologies have both civil and military uses. p. 19

18What pattern runs through pre-nuclear disarmament?

It was imposed on the defeated (Carthage, Versailles) or voluntary but unverified and unenforced (the Hague 1899/1907, the League, Washington 1921–22, Kellogg–Briand 1928). “Controversy over the sovereign rights of states and the rejection of verification and enforcement measures always hampered implementation.” pp. 20–21

19Why was the atom different from earlier disarmament problems?

“There was no precedent for the prohibition of an entire form of energy generation”, so plans balanced sharing peaceful technology against denying military use. p. 22

20Where does the term “safeguards” come from, and what did the Three-Nation Declaration propose?

From the 1919 Paris Peace Conference (a League “to provide safeguards against war”). On 15 November 1945 Canada, the UK and the US called for a UN commission on eliminating atomic weapons and “effective safeguards by way of inspection”, and would not share knowledge until safeguards existed. p. 22

21What did the Acheson–Lilienthal Report propose, and why the “carrot”?

An Atomic Development Authority owning all nuclear materials and dangerous technology, because inspections alone were insufficient (foreign inspectors would cause “serious social frictions”). Peaceful research was a carrot: a purely “negative and policing function … does not appeal to imagination”. pp. 23–24

22Compare the Baruch Plan and the Gromyko plan.

Baruch (14 June 1946): an IADA with ownership and control of dangerous activities, no veto, sanctions, “an international law with teeth in it”. Gromyko (19 June): ban the use, production and storage of bombs, destroy them within three months, then control, keeping the Security Council veto. pp. 24–26

23What was the “only uncontroversial proposition” of the UNAEC?

That all states should have access to the peaceful applications of nuclear technology. The US and USSR also agreed on a dual mandate: promote civilian uses, regulate military ones. p. 26

24Why did Goldschmidt call the 1947 Soviet proposal a “forerunner to the NPT”?

It kept nuclear activities national under periodic inspections and “special investigations” by an International Control Commission. The US rejected in 1947 safeguards it would back thirty years later in the NPT. p. 27

25What did Oppenheimer say in June 1950?

Scientists were relegated “to a secondary place” after “the ‘hierarchy’ took over”; peaceful-atom talk was “loose talk”, “too many vague hopes of a paradise round a corner”; control was “much more a political than a scientific problem”; the UN could still “bridge the gulf”. pp. 27–28

26How does Roehrlich answer Craig and Radchenko?

They call the Baruch Plan “one of America's first acts of the Cold War”, a no-win trap like the Marshall Plan. She grants the propaganda but calls dismissal “too simplistic”: the talks advanced safeguards thinking, UN staff studied models (ICAO, IMF), producer interests (Belgium, Brazil) surfaced, and delegates carried lessons into the IAEA. pp. 29–30

Kraft · Dissenting Scientists

27What does Kraft revise about Castle Bravo?

It was called the “shot that made the world fallout-conscious”, but fallout's emergence as an issue “was a slower and more complex process, one in which scientists played a leading role”. p. 59

28Why is Britain a key case?

The third nuclear power but not a superpower, striving to become the third thermonuclear power as the controversy broke. Its political culture both freed and limited scientists, and it is central to Pugwash's origins. p. 61

29How did the AEC's February 1955 report justify testing?

The extra exposure was a “small amount”, not of “serious concern”, less than natural background or medical radiation, and “fallout is much less dangerous than falling behind the Russians”. p. 62

30What was the Strath Report, and what happened to it?

A 1955 review, overseen by William Strath, of thermonuclear attack on Britain, drawing on US data via Cockcroft. It found there could be “no defense”, was judged too disturbing, and was kept secret (until about 2000). pp. 68–69

31What was agreed with the BBC on 15 February 1955?

At a meeting with ministers and officials, the BBC's Ian Jacob and Sir Alexander Cadogan agreed to restrict coverage of the H-bomb and its effects and to consult the government informally. For the rest of 1955 coverage was minimal and did not feature fallout. p. 73

32Who were the “atomic knights”, and what is “science in black”?

Cockcroft, Hinton and Penney, knighted nuclear insiders. Doel's “science in black” names the secret links between scientists and officials serving the national security state. p. 75

33How does Kraft see the line between the peaceful and military atom?

“Artificial and born of political expediency”: the reactor makes energy, isotopes and fissile material. Calder Hall (1956) was celebrated for power while its plutonium role went unpublicised. pp. 76, 84–85

34What did Rotblat's 1955 Bravo analysis argue, and how was he treated?

Using Nishiwaki's data: fission-fusion-fission bombs posed serious genetic danger through cumulative low-level exposure, the AEC's presentation was “misleading”, and data should be published (“accept the most pessimistic estimate”). Cherwell attacked him in the Lords; Whitehall called him “rather wild”; he had “blotted (his) copybook with the Establishment”. pp. 79–81

35Why does Kraft call the MRC report “a carefully crafted manifesto for nuclear energy”?

Its conservative committee found fallout a “negligible hazard” and framed radiation within Britain's nuclear future (“our civilization is closely bound up with … nuclear energy”). It treated weapons risks as “essentially the same” as peaceful ones, and cast critics as political. pp. 84–85

36What was unusual about the MRC and NAS reports' publication?

Both appeared on 12 June 1956 with similar conclusions: a coordinated transatlantic effort to avoid a “crisis in confidence” if they diverged. Both favoured the threshold concept. pp. 85–86

37What did the April 1957 Sr-90 statement claim, and how was it received?

Sr-90, a bone-seeker, would cause leukaemia, especially in children, at current test rates, so tests should stop. It got scant press but was “incendiary” in Whitehall; Marley and Thomson opposed it within the ASA, and it came out under the Radiation Hazard Committee. pp. 88–90

38Why did Britain go ahead with Grapple X?

Deterrence, standing (abandoning it would mean “inferiority, even for the purposes of negotiation”) and Macmillan's “great prize” of US rapprochement. The Windscale fire was withheld and Sputnik added pressure. It was detonated on 8 November 1957 at Christmas Island. pp. 92–93

39What was Pugwash, and why was fallout key to it?

The first meeting was in July 1957 at Nova Scotia, funded by Cyrus Eaton: 22 scientists from 10 countries, East and West included, invitation-only, Chatham House rules, with post-meeting statements to governments. Fallout crossed borders and blocs, so it was “a necessary, and perhaps a sufficient, condition”. Pugwash is best understood as “a network of scientists”. pp. 95–100
