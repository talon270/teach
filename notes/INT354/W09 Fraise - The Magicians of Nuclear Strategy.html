The magicians of nuclear strategy

Thomas Fraise on how RAND thinkers made deterrence feel knowable, manageable, and authoritative.

Fraise, Thomas. “The Magicians of Nuclear Strategy: Ritualized Knowledge Production and the Origins of Strategic Nuclear Thought.” Global Studies Quarterly 6, no. 1 (2026): ksag015. doi:10.1093/isagsq/ksag015.

The reading in one breath

Early Cold War strategists at and around RAND did more than offer specific war plans. They gave US elites a script of deterrence: a vocabulary and set of methods that linked nuclear actions to supposedly predictable effects, despite the impossibility of directly checking whether an avoided war had been deterred. Their position between academia and the secret state, their specialized craft, and classified access made them unusually credible. Fraise calls this magic because ritualized expertise turned radical uncertainty into a sense of control; he does not claim their work was fraudulent or without scientific merit.

01 / Reading key

What is Fraise claiming?

pp. 1–4

The article shifts the question from whether a strategist's proposal became policy to how strategists made nuclear policy intelligible in the first place.

Deterrence
Trying to prevent another actor's action by making the threatened costs credible. Its success is hard to observe: the attack that did not happen offers no direct causal proof.
Script
A shared set of meanings that tells officials what an action is for, what counts as doing it properly, and which effect it is expected to produce.
Ontological security
A sense that the surrounding world and one's actions in it are reliable and manageable. Epistemic security is confidence in one's knowledge.
Fraise's position

RAND thinkers' greatest contribution was an action-enabling account of deterrence. The script connected practices to effects that could not be verified independently, making high-risk choices seem tractable pp. 2–3.

02 / Source-order summary

The article, section by section

pp. 1–9

Opening: an influence beyond war plans

Untitled introduction · pp. 1–3

In the 1950s, civilian thinkers around the RAND Corporation—Bernard Brodie, Albert Wohlstetter, Herman Kahn, Thomas Schelling, and others—formed a community of nuclear strategists. They had academic credentials, security clearances, and government access. Their mission, as Daniel Ellsberg later recalled, felt like saving the world. Their concepts remained central long after the 1950s. A footnote cites Licklider’s view that, with one exception, the basic elements of strategic thought were set out by 1956. Yet historians disagree over how much direct influence they had on actual US force levels and war plans; some strategists themselves complained that policymakers ignored proposals p. 2.

Fraise accepts that limitation and changes the measure of influence. Drawing on Jutta Weldes, he says state action needs shared meanings before it can be performed. RAND strategists supplied such meanings for deterrence. Earlier authors had already treated deterrence as discourse; his addition is that its script works like magic where danger is extreme and causal effects cannot be checked. The article also asks how a small circle acquired the authority to act as nuclear-policy “guardians,” echoing Robert Dahl's concern with rule by experts pp. 2–3.

Fraise's historiographical move

The apparent gap between declaratory influence and limited impact on operational plans does not settle the question. The strategists also shaped the terms in which officials interpreted action itself p. 2.

Deterrence, magic, and ritualized knowledge production

First titled section · pp. 3–4

Weldes' work gives Fraise the premise that shared meanings make state action possible; J. A. Vuori supplies “deterrentification,” the process by which language makes an object or act count as deterrence. Fraise calls deterrence theory a script because it puts practices into recognizable slots and tells actors how those practices supposedly connect to goals. Wohlstetter's choice of bomber basing scheme is his example: the scheme's effect on Soviet decisions is not self-evident, so a strategic account must assign it deterrent meaning p. 3.

Following Malinowski, Fraise argues that magic becomes socially useful where chance, danger, and incomplete knowledge converge. Nuclear strategy had all three: war would be catastrophic, prior experience was minimal, and real-world testing was intolerably risky. Anthony Giddens' idea of ontological security describes the confidence people seek in such conditions. Xymena Kurowska lets Fraise connect it to epistemic security—confidence in what one claims to know. A script can then provide perceived causal certainty even if an adversary's deterrence cannot be independently observed pp. 3–4.

His “magician” is an ideal type with three linked features. Liminality means standing between established worlds; esoteric craft means specialized methods known to an initiated few; secrecy guards knowledge and separates insiders from outsiders. His comparisons range from astrologers to ritual practitioners. He cites Luhrmann on secrecy's ability to generate deference and McGoey on “oracular power,” the authority to pronounce on what can and cannot be known. He explicitly says magicians need not be frauds p. 4.

Footnotes 4–5

Fraise notes that occult practice cannot be cleanly separated from science and technology, and that Roman diviners were judged differently when serving public institutions or private clients p. 4.

The Birth of a Liminal Community: Nuclear Strategists at the RAND

Second titled section · pp. 4–5

Fraise locates nuclear strategists in a crisis of strategic knowledge. World War II experience seemed inadequate after the hydrogen bomb arrived in 1952. War could no longer be planned apart from preventing war, and nobody had experience of an H-bomb conflict. Civilian operations research, developed during World War II, further challenged officers' monopoly on strategy. Alan Enthoven's reported reply to a general captures the new equality of ignorance: “I have fought just as many nuclear wars as you have” pp. 4–5.

RAND began as an Air Force project in 1946, then added economics and social science departments in 1950. It became the center of strategic thought by the late 1950s. The researchers were neither conventional academics nor soldiers. RAND valued independence from its sponsor, though its leading strategists also clashed with its own leadership. Brodie, Wohlstetter, and Kahn were seen as autonomous; restructuring curtailed them, and Wohlstetter was fired in 1963. Ellsberg later described a brotherhood with a quasi-religious sense of mission p. 5.

This marginal status also let them cross worlds. RAND personnel served on more than 200 federal advisory bodies in 1959–60; Brodie advised the Air Force chief in 1951; and several joined the Kennedy administration. During the Cuban Missile Crisis, Kaufmann, Schelling, and Ellsberg were drawn into government work. Public culture recognized the strategist as a distinct figure—admired in some profiles, condemned by critics such as Robin Blackburn, and caricatured through Dr. Strangelove, often linked to Kahn. Fraise adds literary scholar Peter Goodrich’s observation that Western stories place “mad scientists” near wizard figures; the cultural analogy is suggestive, though it does not demonstrate official reliance on RAND. Fraise treats these portrayals as evidence of the figure's unusual status, rather than evidence that a particular policy was adopted p. 5.

Secrecy and esoterism in the strategists' craft

Third titled section · pp. 5–7

Carol Cohn's “techno-strategic language” explains how jargon and altered meanings identify insiders. Fraise then examines two methods associated with RAND. Systems analysis sought to turn strategic choices into rigorous quantitative comparisons. Wohlstetter's analysis of Strategic Air Command base vulnerability, later publicized in “The Delicate Balance of Terror” (1958), became its best-known example. Wohlstetter claimed RAND analysts had operational knowledge outsiders lacked, criticized outside nuclear-policy writers including Pugwash scientists, and worried that a simple bomb-damage calculator could make untrained people sound expert pp. 5–6.

When McNamara brought RAND thinkers into the Defense Department in 1961, systems analysis gained policy prestige. Journalists and observers simultaneously admired its apparently superhuman rationality and criticized its limits. Game theory had a similarly powerful public association with RAND and nuclear strategy. Yet Brodie, Schelling, and Wohlstetter all qualified its practical role: Schelling argued that the influence was modest and that strategists borrowed useful concepts without doing formal game theory. Fraise reads their qualified embrace as part of the craft's aura; this interpretive move is open to challenge p. 6.

The difference between reputation and practice matters. Fraise reports that systems analysis sometimes used simple arithmetic, had no stable public method, and was hidden by classification. Game theory's use also drew criticism for simplification. Following Alfred Gell, he calls these methods techniques of enchantment: practices that may be useful while also inspiring awe. He suggests that systems analysis lost some mystique when exported to more publicly assessable fields such as urban policy p. 7.

Secrecy supplied the final boundary. RAND staff needed Top Secret clearance; most work remained classified until public nuclear-strategy writing expanded in the late 1950s. Clearance created an internal prestige hierarchy and, Ellsberg recalled, a sense that outsiders were uninformed. Wohlstetter insisted that serious judgments required actual classified operational data, while Schelling said losing government access reduced both his information and his audience. Fraise's point is not simply that secrecy hid work: it also helped define who could credibly speak p. 7.

Substantive footnotes 8–12

Fraise links this “domestication” of nuclear mystery to Kinsella; traces Wohlstetter's bomb-wheel remark to an interview; cites work on opacity and classification; and quotes a 1959 Nation account of RAND's clearance requirement pp. 6–7.

Making Deterrence Possible: The Legacy of Nuclear Strategists

Fourth titled section · pp. 7–8

Fraise argues that the strategists' language gave officials cognitive mastery; their “science of warfare” made contingent arguments appear like timeless truths; and their probabilistic vocabulary recast chance as calculable risk. In his reading, even Schelling’s “threats that leave something to chance” domesticate chance by placing it inside an actionable framework. That interpretation is debatable: explicitly leaving something to chance may also show that strategists acknowledged uncertainty. He cites Schelling's emphasis on decisions over accidents, Kaufmann's and Brodie's belief that nuclear use was unlikely, and Kahn's ordered ladder of escalation as different ways of making nuclear outcomes appear manageable p. 8.

The article adds that this expert identity was masculine coded: rational control, technical hardness, brotherhood, and capacity for violence became marks of strategic authority. Fraise draws on Catherine Eschle and connects this to the normalization of deterrence. A footnote says a reviewer prompted this dimension, which helps explain why the gender discussion appears late and briefly p. 8; fn 16.

Fraise's claim about evidence of demand

The prevalence of briefings and advisory roles, including McNamara's request that Kaufmann teach strategic theory before SALT talks, suggests elites sought a usable account of deterrence. Fraise does not claim that they read every strategist carefully or let strategists make all policy choices pp. 7–8; fn 13.

Conclusion

Fifth titled section · pp. 8–9

Fraise restates that liminality, specialized craft, and secrecy made strategists authoritative in a field where verification was exceptionally difficult. He expressly recognizes the scientific value of their work. His target is the idea that expert status followed only from independently demonstrated accuracy or ordinary professional prestige. Against a Bourdieusian account of objectivation and accumulated social capital, he emphasizes that nuclear strategy remained partly mystified even while experts categorized it. Their script also concealed or sidelined gaps in knowledge. As RAND's early strategists left in the 1960s, their texts became a nuclear-strategy canon. Later obituaries and popular histories sometimes credited Brodie or Schelling with helping avert nuclear war; public debate after Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine returned to classic deterrence language. Fraise argues that such narratives are politically consequential even if strategists did not personally direct every decision pp. 8–9.

Fraise's final verdict

Rationalizing deterrence after the fact is itself an important form of power: the story can make practices seem possible, proper, and sustainable p. 9.

The argument against

A narrow history that asks only whether RAND advice became an operational plan. Fraise also extends earlier accounts of deterrence as discourse by specifying a magical, ritualized form of authority pp. 2–3.

Evidence base

Published strategic texts and histories, memoirs, journalism, oral-history interviews, and RAND-related institutional records. The paper offers a conceptual historical interpretation rather than a systematic measurement of elite beliefs.

Three features
  • Between academia and government
  • Specialized methods and language
  • Classified access and secrecy
Source caution

“Magic” names an authority-producing response to uncertainty. Fraise says technical content can still be valuable p. 8.

WHAT HE DOES WELL / 01

He changes the test of influence.

Distinguishing a strategist's adopted proposals from the categories officials used to understand deterrence resolves an important historiographical puzzle. It explains why an expert can matter even when a particular recommendation is rejected.

WHAT HE DOES WELL / 02

He examines who may speak.

Clearances, jargon, and access are concrete mechanisms of expert authority. The paper helps connect knowledge production to the course's question of whose nuclear order becomes authoritative.

PRESS THE EVIDENCE / 01

Authority is clearer than reassurance.

Advisory appointments and seminars show demand for advice. They do not by themselves prove that officials felt ontologically secure, or that reassurance changed decisions. Ask what internal records, interviews, or comparisons could test that link.

PRESS THE EVIDENCE / 02

Secrecy may have another explanation.

Operational data were classified for strategic reasons, and some technical methods can expose real vulnerabilities. Fraise acknowledges their value. What evidence separates useful expertise under uncertainty from status-producing ritual?

PRESS THE READING / 03

Game theory may be overread.

Brodie and Schelling said they used relatively little formal game theory. Fraise treats this as deference to a powerful craft they could not yet fully use. Their statements may instead indicate that public fascination exceeded its actual role pp. 6–7.

PRESS THE SCOPE / 04

Whose sense of safety?

The article centers US elites. A script that steadied officials could also authorize arms racing or shift danger onto civilians and non-nuclear states. The gender paragraph opens this issue but arrives too late to reshape the main story.

A 45-second contribution you can adapt

“Fraise's strongest move is changing what counts as influence. RAND strategists may not have written every war plan, but they taught officials how to interpret actions as deterrence. I find the proof problem persuasive: a war that never happened cannot tell us which action prevented it. But I want stronger evidence that technical rituals made policymakers feel secure, and a clearer way to separate an aura of expertise from analysis that genuinely identified vulnerabilities.”

Four questions for the room
  1. If no nuclear war occurred, what evidence could support—or falsify—a claim that a policy deterred it?
  2. Does Fraise's “magic” metaphor explain a causal process, or mainly rename the difficulty of verification?
  3. Was classified access a source of mystique, a necessary source of operational data, or both?
  4. Does a more intelligible nuclear order for US elites also make that order more just? Compare Bull's order/justice frame, Hecht's contested nuclearity, and Masco's account of managed nuclear fear.
