01 / Backing Fraise

Influence goes beyond adopted war plans

PDF pp. 2–3, 8–9

Opening argument · about 1 minute

What I find most convincing is Fraise’s change in the question of influence. Historians can ask whether RAND strategists actually determined force levels or wrote war plans, and the answer may often be no. But a policy can still depend on the concepts people use to describe it. Calling a bomber basing choice “deterrence” connects that choice to an expected effect on an adversary, even though the effect cannot be seen directly. Fraise argues that RAND thinkers gave officials a script for making such connections. That makes their influence more basic than getting one recommendation accepted. They helped define what a sensible nuclear action looked like. So I would judge their impact partly by whether officials adopted their language and assumptions, not only by whether they followed specific advice.

Rebuttal · about 1 minute

Reveal opposing response

That wider definition of influence is interesting, but it risks becoming impossible to test. If an official uses the language of deterrence, we cannot automatically credit RAND with causing the decision. Military officers, politicians, and earlier strategic traditions also talked about threats and retaliation. Even if RAND supplied a familiar vocabulary, officials might have used it to justify choices they already preferred for other reasons. The harder question is whether this script changed a choice that would otherwise have been different. Fraise gives us persuasive evidence that strategists gained an audience, but less direct evidence about that counterfactual. I would want drafts, meeting records, or a case where officials explicitly chose an option because the RAND framing made it appear credible. Without that, interpretive influence remains plausible but difficult to establish.

Ask the room: What evidence would show that a strategic concept changed a decision rather than merely described it?
02 / Backing Fraise

The proof problem in deterrence

PDF pp. 3–4, 7–8

Opening argument · about 1 minute

Fraise’s account starts with an unusually severe evidence problem. We cannot run a controlled nuclear crisis twice, once with a deterrent policy and once without it. If no attack occurs, we do not know whether our threat prevented it, whether the adversary never intended to attack, or whether something else mattered more. Yet governments must still make decisions. Fraise’s point is that strategic theories supply a script linking actions to the invisible outcome of prevention. That script makes officials able to act and to explain their actions as rational. I think this is his strongest insight because it explains why expertise can become so powerful precisely where its key causal claim is hardest to verify. It also makes me cautious when anyone says a particular nuclear posture “worked.”

Rebuttal · about 1 minute

Reveal opposing response

We should be careful about moving from “hard to prove” to “basically unknowable.” Many important claims in international politics lack controlled experiments. We can still compare crises, examine what adversaries said they feared, study force vulnerabilities, and ask whether leaders altered their plans when a threat changed. None of that delivers certainty, but it can make some deterrence claims more credible than others. For example, an analysis showing that exposed bombers could be destroyed before retaliation identifies a real vulnerability even if it cannot prove that fixing it prevented war. Fraise recognizes that strategic work may have scientific value. His argument is strongest when it asks us to state the limits of evidence, not when the “magic” metaphor seems to flatten differences between strong and weak analysis.

Ask the room: What would count as good evidence for deterrence when a direct test is impossible?
03 / Backing Fraise

Secrecy makes experts authoritative

PDF pp. 5–7

Opening argument · about 1 minute

Fraise shows that expertise is not only a matter of having a better argument. RAND strategists stood between academic research and the classified security state. They used specialized methods, had access to operational information, and could tell outsiders that serious judgment required knowledge outsiders were not allowed to see. This arrangement made their claims difficult to challenge publicly. A security clearance became a marker of credibility as well as access. The example matters because public debate about nuclear policy may look open while the standards for being heard are set behind closed doors. Fraise is not saying that classified information is useless. He is asking us to notice how secrecy helps decide who gets treated as an expert and whose objections can be dismissed before they are heard.

Rebuttal · about 1 minute

Reveal opposing response

There is a fair reason why much nuclear planning was classified: useful analysis of bomber bases, capabilities, and vulnerabilities required operational details that an adversary could exploit. Without those details, public commentators might simply be wrong about the options available. So it is too easy to treat every appeal to classified knowledge as a move to silence critics. Sometimes an analyst really does know something relevant that cannot be disclosed. Fraise’s question about authority remains valuable, but we need a more precise test. Did insiders use secrecy to avoid scrutiny when a claim could have been evaluated openly? Were dissenting cleared analysts heard? Could conclusions be released without revealing sensitive inputs? Those questions would let us assess the cost of secrecy without assuming that secrecy itself makes the analysis suspect.

Ask the room: How can people scrutinize nuclear policy when some of the relevant evidence must remain classified?
04 / Pressing Fraise

Does the “magic” metaphor overreach?

PDF pp. 3–4, 6–8

Opening argument · about 1 minute

I think Fraise’s “magicians” metaphor is memorable, but it can blur an important distinction. A strategist may use models and jargon that impress outsiders, yet still discover a genuine vulnerability or clarify a difficult choice. Wohlstetter’s work on the vulnerability of Strategic Air Command bases is a good example: whether a policy ultimately deterred an adversary is uncertain, but the exposure of aircraft to attack is a real question. If we call the whole practice magical, listeners may hear “fraud” or “empty ritual,” even though Fraise explicitly rejects that reading. I would like the article to specify when expertise is doing two things at once: producing useful knowledge and producing confidence beyond what that knowledge warrants. The excess confidence, rather than technical analysis itself, seems to be the target.

Rebuttal · about 1 minute

Reveal opposing response

The metaphor works precisely because it does not require the strategist to be a fraud. Fraise says the methods can have scientific merit. His point is that in a domain defined by catastrophic danger and limited verification, even useful techniques can acquire a second social role: they reassure an audience that the world is intelligible and manageable. Systems analysis may expose a vulnerability and also make a much larger claim about controlling nuclear escalation feel credible. Those effects should be studied together, not pulled apart too quickly. The metaphor directs attention to the authority produced by institutions, technical language, and secrecy. If we replace “magic” with a milder phrase like “uncertain expertise,” we may lose what is distinctive about the public and political power of this expert community.

Ask the room: What separates a useful model from a model that creates more confidence than its evidence supports?
05 / Pressing Fraise

Game theory: practice or reputation?

PDF pp. 6–7

Opening argument · about 1 minute

One place where I would push Fraise is his treatment of game theory. He notes that figures such as Brodie and Schelling described formal game theory as having a modest role in their actual work. Fraise reads this alongside the powerful aura of RAND’s specialized craft. But a simpler possibility is that the public image of nuclear strategy was more mathematical than the practice itself. If that is true, the article needs to distinguish the influence of a technique from the prestige attached to its name. This does not destroy Fraise’s broader point about expert authority; in fact, the gap between reputation and practice might support it. But the evidence seems to warrant a narrower claim: game theory helped create an image of rigorous control, while its direct practical contribution varied across strategists and problems.

Rebuttal · about 1 minute

Reveal opposing response

That gap may be exactly why Fraise includes game theory. He is interested in how nuclear strategy gained authority, not in proving that every strategist solved formal equations. A method can shape a field by setting its vocabulary and ideal of rationality even when practitioners use it selectively. Schelling’s work, for example, can draw on strategic interaction without looking like a textbook proof. The fact that practitioners downplayed formal game theory while outsiders still saw nuclear strategy through its mathematical image is historically revealing. It shows that the field’s public identity was not a transparent reflection of daily practice. Fraise’s claim would be overstated if he said equations directly determined policy. But as an account of how specialized craft gained prestige, the mismatch is evidence worth taking seriously.

Ask the room: Can a method influence policy through its reputation even when officials rarely use it formally?
06 / Course connection

Whose sense of security?

PDF pp. 7–9 · INT354 lens

Opening argument · about 1 minute

Fraise helps explain how US decision makers came to feel that nuclear danger could be discussed and managed. But I want to ask whose security this script served. If a strategic vocabulary reassures officials, that does not tell us whether the policies are just or safe for everyone affected by them. A credible threat depends on the possibility of violence against populations who have little voice in the discussion. This takes us back to the course’s order-versus-justice question: a stable nuclear order for powerful states may distribute risk very unevenly. Fraise briefly notes the masculine coding of the expert strategist, but the people outside the strategic community remain mostly background. I would extend his argument by asking who gets excluded when technical language defines what counts as a rational nuclear choice.

Rebuttal · about 1 minute

Reveal opposing response

That is an important question, but it may ask this article to do a different job. Fraise sets out to explain how a small group of US strategists acquired authority and how deterrence became an actionable script. He does not claim to give a full moral assessment of nuclear order or a history of everyone exposed to it. His narrower focus lets him identify mechanisms—classified access, esoteric craft, and institutional position—that a broader ethical account could later use. We can connect those mechanisms to unequal risk without treating the absence of every affected group as a flaw in the causal argument. The fair criticism is more specific: when Fraise says strategists made the world feel manageable, he should specify whose world became manageable and avoid letting an elite perspective stand in for everyone’s experience.

Ask the room: Can an account of expert authority explain nuclear order without also examining who bears its risks?

A closing line if discussion is ending

“Fraise makes me less willing to treat nuclear strategy as simply a contest of correct and incorrect models. His article asks who gets to turn uncertainty into authoritative advice. The question I would leave with is how we can demand useful expertise without letting its prestige hide what remains unknown—or whose lives are placed at risk.”
