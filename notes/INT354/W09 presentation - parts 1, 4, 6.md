## Part 1: The emergence of nuclear strategists and the crisis of military knowledge

Our article is Thomas Fraise's "The Magicians of Nuclear Strategy." His question: how do experts gain authority over something that can never be tested?

Before nuclear weapons, strategists learned from past wars. After 1945, no state could fight a nuclear war to check whether deterrence worked. You can test what a bomb destroys. You cannot test whether a threat deters. As **Connelly and colleagues** put it in this week's reading, struggles over the fate of the earth were settled "in a land of make-believe."

Into that gap stepped civilian strategists: Brodie, Wohlstetter, Kahn and Schelling, mostly at RAND, the Air Force think tank set up in 1946. They stood between academia and government, with classified access and the ear of policymakers.

Why is authority the puzzle, and not accuracy? **Connelly** shows that the Air Force forecast a thousand Soviet ICBMs by 1961. The Soviets built eleven. The analysts were promoted, not held to account. As late as 1983, **McNamara** still finds the people "most familiar" with these weapons split into three "mutually exclusive" views of their role.

So Fraise's magician comparison is not an insult. **Connelly** reaches the same point: Cold War leaders used "modern forms of divination," because "power must cloak itself in the mystique of clairvoyance." Fraise asks how that cloak was made.

## Part 4: Esoteric craft, system analysis and game theory

Fraise's second feature of the magician is esoteric craft: techniques that only insiders can use. For strategists, that meant technical vocabulary, system analysis and game theory. Carol Cohn shows that this language makes mass death sound manageable.

The craft had real value. Wohlstetter's "Delicate Balance of Terror" showed that deterrence needs forces that survive a first strike. **McNamara** recalls that Kennedy-era analysis found Soviet conventional power overestimated and NATO's underestimated. That finding drove the shift to flexible response.

But this week's readings also show the enchantment. **Connelly and colleagues** describe a 1962 Pentagon study that calculated "average deliverability per weapon" to three decimal places, with almost no reliable data on Soviet forces. They call it "smoke and mirrors." System analysis "depended entirely on the criteria used to pick winners," so it worked as a self-fulfilling prophecy.

Game theory had the same double edge. **Powell** examines Schelling's famous analogy: chess with a die, where one bad roll means disaster for both players. Powell says Schelling "claims too much." The simple mechanism "obscures" how narrow the real path to war is. The model looks clear because it hides its assumptions.

This is Alfred Gell's "technique of enchantment." The method clarifies a problem and also makes its user look like a seer. Precision became a performance of authority.

## Part 6: The deterrence script, uncertainty and political decision-making

The deterrence script runs: keep forces that survive an attack, threaten credible retaliation, and the adversary holds back. But if no attack comes, no one can prove the threat was the reason.

Fraise separates risk from uncertainty. Risk can be estimated. Uncertainty cannot, because outcomes depend on how leaders perceive threats. **McNamara** admits this in 1983: to know what deters Moscow, "we must put ourselves in the minds" of a few Soviet leaders.

Strategists still turned uncertainty into models. **Connelly and colleagues** describe a 1962 Pentagon forecast worked out to three decimal places with almost no real data. They call it "smoke and mirrors." A model can organise a problem without making it predictable.

Schelling's "threats that leave something to chance" admit that no one fully controls escalation. **Powell** shows the hidden cost: once both sides' forces could survive, these threats "actually presupposed irrationality." **McNamara** puts it plainly: the threat deters only because "it in fact increases the risk of nuclear war."

Strategists also disagreed. Kaufmann and Brodie doubted any president would go nuclear, and **McNamara** privately advised Kennedy and Johnson never to initiate nuclear use. Kahn, by contrast, mapped escalation in stages.

They shaped policy through concepts, briefings and shared language. **Connelly** shows Berlin war games in which officials "virtually 'lived' the game."

So they made deterrence possible as policy, without proving it would always work.
