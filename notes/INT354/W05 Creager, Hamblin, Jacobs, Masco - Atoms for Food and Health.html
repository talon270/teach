Atoms for Food & Health

Summaries of this week's four readings. Two follow the peaceful atom into medicine and agriculture. Two follow the bomb's effects into classrooms and into New Mexico's land, animals and people. Every section, figure and note is covered, then a revision kit, an evidence ledger, a self-test and a coverage audit.

The week in one breath

Each reading finds the promise of the atom more political than it looked. Creager shows how the US Atomic Energy Commission, from 1946, turned the Oak Ridge reactor into a national supplier of radioisotopes. It was a deliberate programme that let the AEC prove it was “truly a civilian agency”. It changed biology through tracers and medicine through diagnostics, but never delivered the cancer cure it advertised. Hamblin follows atomic agriculture into the United Nations in the 1960s. The IAEA, whose purpose was to promote the atom, outmanoeuvred the FAO and silenced the FAO geneticist Ronald Silow. It sold irradiated food, sterile insects and mutation breeding to the poorest countries, and manufactured a consensus that “modern” meant “atomic”. Jacobs reads the 1951 school films Duck and Cover and Atomic Alert. They were meant to teach survival and trust in authority. Instead they taught children that adults could not protect them, and the generation raised on “no future” became the New Left. Masco argues that the bomb turned the biosphere itself into an experiment. His examples run from pigs blasted in Nevada to radioactive wildlife preserves, tritium-sniffing honeybees and the Area G waste dump. The Manhattan Project, he argues, produced “mutant ecologies” that will outlast the state that made them.

Nuclear Energy in the Service of Biomedicine

The U.S. Atomic Energy Commission's Radioisotope Program, 1946–1950 · Angela N. H. Creager, Princeton University

The article in one breath

After 1945 the US government became “the manufacturer of a basic laboratory and clinical tool” p. 653. Manhattan Project scientists, worried about the future of Oak Ridge's Clinton Laboratories, proposed using the wartime graphite reactor to make radioisotopes for outside researchers. The Isotopes Branch made its first sale, a millicurie of carbon-14 for cancer research, the day after the Atomic Energy Act became law in August 1946. Priced below cost and heavily promoted, reactor isotopes went mostly to biology and medicine: phosphorus-32 and iodine-131 first, then carbon-14. As tracers they transformed biochemistry and ecology, from Calvin's photosynthesis cycle to Hutchinson's pond. In medicine they transformed diagnostics, while the advertised cancer cure stayed a “mirage”. Creager argues the program was a deliberate political commitment. It let an AEC with “little else to hold out” show it was “truly a civilian agency”, at home and abroad. It also turned the cyclotron physicists' gift economy into a subsidised, supply-driven trade in commodities. Historians who read the AEC only through military patronage miss initiatives “driven by political concerns rather than military objectives”. For Creager the isotope program was “the fundamental dream of the AEC … that atoms could cure as well as kill” p. 679.

Introduction

Why isotopes mattered politically and materially · the program as deliberate commitment · tracers vs radiation sources

“The radioactive isotope puts nuclear energy at the service of the investigator and thus tremendously increases his power of perception.”U.S. Atomic Energy Commission, 1948 · epigraph, p. 650 · fn 1

Claim
Radioisotopes mattered politically, for the AEC's civilian image and for foreign relations, and materially, because they changed postwar biomedicine. Their distribution was a deliberate commitment that made the government a manufacturer.
Evidence
Smyth's 1950 remarks; Evans's 1946 claim; shipment and publication counts; the AEC's own tracer/source distinction.
Against
Bomb historiography centred on physics; accounts of the AEC in biology that stress research funding and overlook production.

Smyth: “Isotopes”

On 23 March 1950 Henry DeWolf Smyth, an AEC Commissioner, addressed the agency's Advisory Committee on Isotope Distribution. The minutes record:

He said that the Atomic Energy Commission is especially interested in isotope distribution because when it is asked, “What are the peacetime uses of atomic energy?” it can reply “Isotopes.” Not that they will be useful sometime but that they are already useful. The isotope distribution program is enormously valuable because it reveals the Atomic Energy Commission as more than just a weapons organization. This is true not only in this country, but abroad where the foreign distribution of isotopes has had a very good effect on our foreign relations.… He concluded by saying that the Commission considers the importance of the isotope program quite out of proportion to the financial questions involved.

Minutes, 23–24 March 1950 · p. 650 · fn 2 (emphasis in original)

Smyth is remembered as the physicist who wrote the official account of the Manhattan Project. The “Smyth Report” is “the starting place for a vast historiography of the bomb oriented around its origins and consequences in the physical sciences” p. 650. Until recently that literature “seldom addressed” what the bomb project, or its “nominally civilian successor” the AEC, meant for biology, medicine and agriculture. Yet the isotopes Smyth mentioned “were disproportionately used in biomedical research and therapy as compared with the physical sciences and engineering” p. 650. (The phrase “physicists' war” for World War II is Kevles's fn 3.)

Proof of a civilian agency

Creager's view

The Commissioners viewed the provision of radioisotopes “as a crucial demonstration that the AEC was truly a civilian agency, i.e., that their contributions went beyond constructing atomic weapons” pp. 650–651.

Scientists joined in. The health physicist Robley Evans claimed in 1946: “The sober truth is that through medical advances alone, atomic energy has already saved more lives than were snuffed out at Hiroshima and Nagasaki” p. 651. fn 4 Other publications repeated Evans's three medical advances: radiolabelled blood cells to test whole-blood preservatives, radioiodine therapy for thyroid disorders, and metabolic tracing.

By 1948 the AEC “had little else to hold out as evidence of the atom's peaceful benefits”, because hopes for a quick nuclear power industry, with energy “too cheap to meter”, had faded. So “the biomedical benefits associated with radiation and radioisotopes became crucial to the politics of atomic energy” p. 651. fn 5 The AEC's General Advisory Committee issued a statement to correct “unwarranted optimism” about atomic power. “Too cheap to meter” itself comes from Lewis Strauss, AEC chairman, speaking to the National Association of Science Writers on 16 September 1954.

Isotopes and foreign relations

Radioisotope exports “at first permitted shipments only for biological and medical uses”. The US government saw them as “a key means of using science to aid diplomacy”. At first exports were justified as part of the Marshall Plan fn 6. By the mid-1950s Eisenhower's “Atoms for Peace”, which focused on foreign development of atomic energy, gave the program's international reach special attention. “Shipments of radioisotopes were intended to signify the beneficence of the U.S. to the free world” p. 651.

fn 7 “Here my study supports John Krige's contention that science served as a crucial instrument of foreign policy, particularly once Eisenhower focused attention on the non-military fronts of the Cold War” (Krige 2006, a Week 4 reading). The language of atomic “dividends” comes from Maisel 1947 fn 8.

Material consequences, in numbers

Political significance alone would make the program “a valuable case for understanding the civilian ‘dividends’ of atomic energy”. But it also “altered the repertoire of reagents and therapies commonly employed in postwar biomedicine” p. 651.

  • 1946–1955: nearly 64,000 shipments of radioactive materials to laboratories, companies and hospitals pp. 651–652.
  • From fall 1947 a foreign export program made up about one-sixth of shipments. It was politically controversial fn 9.
  • Domestic and foreign recipients together published more than 10,000 scientific papers in the program's first 8 years p. 652.

fn 10 The count understates use. Many shipments were bulk amounts to companies that made labelled molecules, so “the total number of isotope shipments received by ultimate users is several times greater than the quoted number of shipments [63,990] from ORNL” (AEC 1955).

What the historiography stresses, and what it misses

Existing work on the AEC in life science stresses its own research and its grants. John Beatty and Susan Lindee showed that its interest in radiation damage funded genetics and the Atomic Bomb Casualty Commission, “with its own complex political and scientific agendas” fn 11. In the 1950s, worry about low-grade radioactive waste led Oak Ridge National Laboratory to build a large ecology group, “instrumental in the development of radioecology” (Bocking) fn 12. Biology and medicine were also done at the national laboratories and at AEC-sponsored universities fn 13. The isotope program stands out for its early origin, since the Manhattan Project set it up before the Atomic Energy Act of 1946, and for “the sheer number of research sites that it affected” p. 652. (For Britain's program, 1945–65, see Kraft 2006 fn 14.)

Creager's view

“The governmental distribution of radioisotopes was a deliberate commitment, not a mere side result of the postwar continuation of atomic weapons production.” Most AEC isotopes “were not, strictly speaking, fission by-products, but rather isotopes generated specifically by using the bombardment capabilities of the nuclear ‘pile’” pp. 652–653.

The government as manufacturer

“The program put the federal government in a peculiar role, as the manufacturer of a basic laboratory and clinical tool” p. 653. The historiography “barely” notes this; Lenoir and Hays (2000) are “an important exception” fn 16. Grant programs shaped research “by footing the bill”. The isotope program instead involved the government “directly in the production and distribution of perishable laboratory goods”. Congress argued over the state's relation to university science and delayed the National Science Foundation until 1950. Meanwhile AEC isotopes showed the shift towards supporting basic research “in strikingly tangible terms” p. 653.

fn 15 The first AEC chairman, David Lilienthal, had run the Tennessee Valley Authority. The state as supplier of isotopes, and even more of nuclear power, continued the New Deal's controversial involvement in energy utilities. Thomas Hughes compared building the Manhattan Project's plants to building the TVA. fn 17 Some agencies did fund basic research before 1950: the Office of Naval Research and the National Institutes of Health.

Two classes: tracers and radiation sources

From the start the AEC sorted shipments into two classes: tracers, and sources of radiation, notably for therapy fn 18.

  • Research relied mostly on tracer methods, such as tagging biological molecules with radioactive atoms to work out metabolic pathways.
  • Early clinical uses mostly aimed to replace X-rays and radium with specific isotopes, “usually in hopes of better localizing the radiation exposure”.
  • So tracer vs source implied research vs therapy. The quantities differed hugely: therapy “might require more than a thousand times higher dosage” than tracer use of the same isotope pp. 653–654 fn 19.
  • “At the biological level, it was the observation that very low-level amounts of radiation did not disturb fundamental living processes that legitimated the use of radioisotopic tracers as probes” p. 654.

fn 20 Biologists used isotopes either as tracers or as function-disturbing radiation, “rarely both”. One exception: phosphorus-32 “suicide” experiments on bacteriophage, where the phage DNA carried so much label that the phosphorus atoms' decay inactivated the phage.

Where the distinction broke down: diagnostics

Diagnostics was a medical use closer to tracer work in both quantity and approach. “The diagnostic uses of radioisotopes proved even more revolutionary than the employment of radioisotopes to treat cancer”, the goal behind most early medical experiments, which continued after the war “under the aegis of the AEC” p. 654. Contemporary observers saw clinical diagnostics as “essentially an application of tracer methodology to medicine”: small amounts of isotope used to detect particular organs, functions or tissue types fn 21.

Early postwar publicity about “potential breakthroughs in medicine” focused on therapy, especially cancer fn 22. (John Lawrence and others had raised the same hopes in the late 1930s and early 1940s.) The expectations that isotopes would revolutionize cancer therapy “were not substantiated”, whereas by the 1950s they had become important tools in diagnostics p. 654 fn 23. Alison Kraft made the same point from British sources. Diagnostics, like tracers, drew on “the power of radioactive materials to make visible processes that were previously imperceptible”. The AEC told Congress in 1948:

[U]ntil recently man's vision has not penetrated into the details of growth and change in the living cell or the intricate chemistry of the molecule. Now, using tracer isotopes the scientist and engineer can observe atoms as they take part in basic organic and inorganic reactions, much as though atoms were visibly tagged or labeled. For the first time it becomes possible to follow in intimate detail nature's fundamental processes.

AEC report to Congress, 1948 · p. 655 · fn 24
Creager's view

The “epistemological similarity” between tracer and diagnostic uses shows “the important interchanges between laboratory research and clinical application, biology and medicine”. This continued a precedent set by researchers around cyclotrons, “even as the scale and means of disseminating radioisotopes were altered radically by the government's program” p. 655.

Redeploying the Oak Ridge Reactor

Claim

Evidence
The Manhattan District History; committee reports and letters; Cohn's oral history; the photographs of the first sale; request and sales figures for 1946.
Against
The assumption that the program was an automatic spin-off of weapons production.

Scientists plan for peace (1944–45)

The initiative to use the Oak Ridge graphite reactor as a production facility for outside users came from Manhattan Project scientists, reacting to postwar uncertainty p. 655 fn 25. From 1944 scientists at the Metallurgical (Met) Lab in Chicago began to think about what would happen to the Manhattan Engineer District (MED) after the war. They were less involved than Los Alamos in the final bomb work. Besides the Met Lab and Berkeley's Rad Lab, the MED had three large reservations:

  • Site X, Tennessee: two plants to purify uranium-235 and a graphite reactor for producing plutonium, called Clinton Laboratories;
  • Site W, Hanford, Washington, on the Columbia River: large-scale plutonium production;
  • Site Y, Los Alamos: bomb design and construction under J. Robert Oppenheimer pp. 655–656.

Zay Jeffries, a Met Lab metallurgist, suggested a prospectus on postwar “nucleonics” (nuclear research and development). Lab head Arthur Compton appointed a committee. Its November 1944 report proposed that the federal government set up national laboratories after the war. Which sites would qualify was “highly contested”. General Leslie Groves first left the choice to the future atomic energy agency. But the legislation “stalled in Congress amid a lengthy debate over civilian versus military control of atomic energy” p. 656. Staff drifted away, especially from Los Alamos and Clinton. Late in 1945 Groves and Oppenheimer authorised continued research at Berkeley, Los Alamos and Clinton with no overall postwar plan.

fn 27 Michael Gordin pointed out that the same people were active in the scientists' movement against the bomb. Jeffries wrote an early version of the Franck Report. “Nucleonics” never caught on the way “atomic energy” did. McGraw-Hill's magazine Nucleonics ran from September 1947 to 1967 and explained that “to the proton and neutron has been given the common name of nucleon”, defining nucleonics as “the technology for utilizing” the processes that rearrange nucleons. It carried articles on labelling and detecting isotopes in biomedical research.

“Dogpatch”: why Clinton needed a mission

  • Clinton's rural setting earned it the wartime nickname “Dogpatch” p. 656.
  • Its graphite reactor, built in 1943, was a pilot plant for Hanford's bigger plutonium reactors. Once Hanford ran, the Tennessee reactor was no longer needed for bomb material pp. 656–657.
  • Oak Ridge had an industrial orientation, with the gaseous diffusion and electromagnetic separation plants alongside.
  • Clinton lost its academic contractor, the University of Chicago, in July 1945. Monsanto took over, “the first industrial firm to manage one of the Manhattan District's large research laboratories”.
  • New national laboratories depended on academic backing (as with Argonne and Brookhaven), which left Oak Ridge at a disadvantage. The official AEC history: “For everyone at the Clinton Laboratories the future was uncertain and for many of them it seemed hopeless” p. 657.

Cancer as the first justification (late 1945)

Late in 1945 a Committee on Growth of the Clinton Laboratories appointed a Radioisotope Committee to “study the possibilities of distributing radioisotopes for general and cancer research” p. 657. The word “cancer” drew on the long use of radioactivity in cancer therapy and on “strong public sentiment in favor of mobilizing science” in a “war against cancer”. (The phrase dates from the 1930s; President Nixon revived it in the early 1970s fn 33.)

During the war the American Cancer Society raised large sums and asked the National Academy of Sciences' National Research Council to hand out grants through a new Committee on Growth. That committee pointed out that cyclotron-made radioactive materials were inadequate for cancer research. Through its efforts Frank Jewett, president of the National Academy of Sciences, wrote to Secretary of War Robert Patterson in October 1945. He urged that the isotope by-products of the “chain-reacting piles of the Manhattan District” go immediately to medical researchers pp. 657–658. fn 35 The letter was dated 18 October 1945; Jewett offered the Committee on Growth for “controlled and intelligent distribution”.

From plan to Isotopes Branch (Dec 1945 – Feb 1946)

  • The MED's own Radioisotope Committee, Waldo Cohn, J. R. Coe, C. D. Coryell and Arthur H. Snell, proposed in December 1945 that Oak Ridge supply research institutions with reactor isotopes, with a step-by-step plan p. 658. fn 36 That month Paul Aebersold drafted the forms researchers would use to request “a radioelement”.
  • 3 January 1946: the plan reached Stafford Warren, director of the MED's Medical Division. The director of the Research Division set up a separate operating unit.
  • February 1946: the “Isotopes Branch” at Oak Ridge was running, with Paul C. Aebersold as chief. Aebersold had earned his PhD with E. O. Lawrence and handled isotope applications around Berkeley's cyclotrons before the war. During the war he consulted at Oak Ridge fn 37. His 13 June 1946 job application survives in his papers at Texas A&M. In the war's last months he worked at Los Alamos on health protection for the 1945 test, which the note spells “Almagordo”.
  • According to biochemist Waldo E. Cohn, he and Aebersold were the driving force behind the committee and its plan fn 38.

Pricing: “at cost”, then “out-of-pocket”

The Oak Ridge plan “met with enthusiasm from scientists who had been prominent in the war effort” pp. 658–659. On 8–9 March 1946 the Advisory Committee on Research and Development for the Manhattan District “endorsed the distribution of radioisotopes at cost”, as part of its vision for national laboratories p. 659. fn 39 It had seven wartime leaders: Robert F. Bacher (made an AEC Commissioner seven months later), Arthur H. Compton, Warren K. Lewis, John R. Runoff (so printed), Charles A. Thomas, Richard C. Tolman and John A. Wheeler.

Groves then formed the Interim Advisory Committee on Isotope Distribution Policy, headed by Lee DuBridge:

  • physicists Lee DuBridge and Merle Tuve; chemists Linus Pauling and Vincent du Vigneaud;
  • medical researchers Cornelius Rhoades and Cecil J. Watson; biologists Raymond Zirkle and A. Baird Hastings;
  • Zay Jeffries and L. F. Curtiss for the applied sciences; Aebersold as non-voting secretary p. 659.

Its report of 20 April 1946 strongly backed a major program and recommended charging users no more than “out-of-pocket” costs. Groves asked Monsanto to “estimate costs of production and distribution, exclusive of pile costs, plant rentals, or costs of existing laboratories” p. 659. According to one Oak Ridge scientist, Groves first wanted the reactor's construction cost built into prices. Scientists objected: “you put that price on carbon-14 and you won't sell a microcurie of it. You can't [put] it on the back of biological researchers” p. 660. The scientists won. Buyers would not pay for the wartime infrastructure that made reactor isotopes possible.

fn 40 The Interim Committee met only once and worked mostly by correspondence. It served until 31 December 1947, when the AEC named a permanent Committee on Isotope Distribution. Its invited members were Gioacchino Failla (chair), Hymer L. Friedell, A. H. Holland, J. G. Hamilton, Joseph W. Kennedy, Robley D. Evans, Robert F. Mehl, Austin M. Brues, Leslie N. Nims, H. A. Barker, Henry Borsook and Paul Aebersold.

Allocation, human use and transport

  • A Subcommittee on Allocation and Distribution would judge each request and requester to set priorities p. 660.
  • A Subcommittee on Human Application could veto any use of radioactive materials involving humans. It first met on 28 June 1946. It covered medical experiments and also industrial exposure, such as sulfur-35 in underground coal mines and yttrium-90 as a tracer in gasoline for simulated plane crashes.
  • Expecting scarcity, the committee treated ethics “principally in terms of allocation”: who would get isotopes, among human uses and between human uses and other research. In the end the reactor “not only kept up with demand but soon exceeded it, rendering this allocation scheme moot” p. 660.
  • Transport was “a new problem for the carriers”. The Railway Express Company's rules for shipping radium were extended to isotopes. Through the Civil Aeronautics Board, airlines drew up rules for flying radioactive materials out of Oak Ridge, “although not all the airlines were willing to accept shipments” p. 660.

The catalog and its rules (14 June 1946)

The first catalog of Oak Ridge isotopes appeared in Science on 14 June 1946 as “Availability of Radioactive Isotopes: Announcement from Headquarters, Manhattan Project”. Cohn credited it to himself, R. T. Overmann and Aebersold p. 661. fn 46 Aebersold wrote the administrative part and Cohn the technical part.

  • About one hundred isotopes were available; each request went to the Isotopes Branch for review.
  • Domestic buyers for non-Commission work had to file an “Acceptance of Terms and Conditions for Order and Receipt of Byproduct Materials (Radioisotopes)” fn 47.
  • Only individuals at “qualified institutions” could receive them; no secondary distribution.
  • Fundamental and publishable research had top priority. “Routine commercial application” and non-publishable research fell outside the guidelines, a policy the AEC later reversed to promote industrial use.

Putting fundamental and unclassified research first showed the scientists' commitment to civilian benefits. This was when many physical scientists were lobbying against military control of atomic energy fn 48. The announcement also ranked research above therapy, partly because therapy needed more material. In fact, once the new agency ran production, medical uses got more attention and priority p. 661.

The first sale (1–2 August 1946)

On 1 August 1946 President Harry S. Truman signed the Atomic Energy Act, giving the Manhattan Project a legal basis to distribute reactor “byproduct materials”. The first “shipment” came the next day, “with appropriate formalities in front of the Clinton pile” pp. 661–662. It was “a carefully orchestrated event with full press coverage”. As photographers worked and Colonel E. E. Kirkpatrick looked on, Eugene Wigner handed a millicurie of carbon-14, sold for $367, to E. V. Cowdry and William L. Simpson, research director and associate research director of the Barnard Free Skin and Cancer Hospital, St. Louis (Figure 1). The first shipment went to cancer research, and its buyer came from St. Louis, home of Clinton's contractor, Monsanto p. 662.

Weak demand, then promotion

By the end of 1946 the Isotopes Branch had 306 requests, had made 125 shipments, and had sold just under $30,000. Planners had expected more. When they polled institutions, three reasons came back: “(a) lack of trained personnel, (b) lack of electronic detection devices, and (c) the speed with which radioisotopes were made available found research with plans incompleted [sic] for their utilization” p. 662. The Branch set out to overcome them.

Claim
Reactor isotopes changed scale and cost, but early demand followed the cyclotron era's uses and users. Public hope, raised by the bomb, looked for cures.
Evidence
The War Department's 1946 press release; Aebersold's 1948 chart (Figure 2); shipment estimates; Maisel in Collier's.
Against
The press release's framing of isotopes as the “first peacetime application” of wartime research, as if they were new.

The War Department's June 1946 press release, issued with the catalog, called the “availability of radioisotopes for biological and medical research” the “first peacetime application of the results of wartime atomic research” p. 662. It admitted that artificial isotopes were not new, since cyclotrons had made small amounts before the war. But the reactor “changed drastically the scale and cost of production”. For carbon-14 it could “be made equivalent in production to hundreds of cyclotrons” p. 662.

The cyclotrons' legacy

Even so, the cyclotron legacy shaped early demand. The isotopes most wanted in the program's first two years were those already in use in research or therapy. Institutions tied to a large cyclotron also bought more from the government (Figure 2) p. 663. fn 55 These were the University of California, MIT, the Carnegie Institution of Washington, Columbia University, the Bio-chemical Research Foundation (Newark, Delaware), Washington University and Harvard.

The two isotopes shipped most were phosphorus-32 and iodine-131: about two-thirds of domestic shipments, and a higher share of foreign ones pp. 663–664. fn 56 The estimates run to 30 June 1949. Iodine-131's short half-life (8 days) is one reason there were so many shipments of it: it had a short shelf life.

Radium, neutrons and false starts

Both isotopes had been part of medical physics since the 1930s, made with neutron sources and cyclotrons, and in both cases early metabolic studies led quickly to clinical use. “The desire to supplant expensive radium (upwards of $20,000 per gram) with relatively less expensive forms of radiation, namely neutron rays and artificial radioisotopes, provided the motivation for much of the medical activity around cyclotrons”, and justified funds for costly cyclotrons p. 664. “Not all the new radiation sources proved beneficial”:

  • John Lawrence gave up neutron therapy after disappointing results with 250 patients treated in cyclotrons pp. 664–665.
  • Radiosodium, hailed by E. O. Lawrence in December 1934 as “superior to radium” for cancer, had little effect on tumours in animal and human tests p. 665.

Hopes of redemption

Hiroshima and Nagasaki did not dampen the hope that artificial radioactivity would revolutionize medicine; “if anything, the new scale of atomic energy heightened the level of expectation”. Albert Q. Maisel wrote in Collier's in May 1947: “The first benign results of atomic bomb research have been new tools for medical scientists which promise cures for hitherto incurable diseases. There have been failures, but the work is just starting and researchers generally are hopeful” p. 665.

Creager's view

“These hopes seemed to carry the burden of redemption in the age of atomic weapons” p. 665.

From the Oak Ridge laboratories that gave us the most painful problem of our age now flows a small but steady stream of radioactive isotopes. These strange by-products of atomic energy may yet prove as important to mankind as the atomic bomb or the cheap atomic power that is still only a promise on the horizon. For to scientists, radioactive isotopes are tools of power in the eternal fight against pain and death; they may be even a means to prying open the once tightly shut door to an understanding of the inner processes of life itself.

Maisel, Collier's, 1947 · p. 665

Maisel's key examples were radioactive phosphorus and iodine, which already showed “the two uses of atomic energy - as tracer detectives and as interbody medical bullets” p. 665.

Claim
Phosphorus-32 shows research and therapy feeding each other around the cyclotrons. Its clinical value was disputed, and its most original use was not medical at all.
Evidence
Chiewitz and Hevesy (1935); the Lawrences' leukemia work; Rhoads (1946); the AEC's 1948 report, analysed entry by entry; Hutchinson's Linsley Pond studies.
Against
Tansley's intent that “ecosystem” replace organism talk in ecology.

From rats to leukemia

  • 1935: Otto Chiewitz and George Hevesy showed that ingested radiophosphorus concentrated in rats' bones and, less, their muscles p. 665. fn 61 They worked with Niels Bohr in Copenhagen. Hevesy's tracer work was crucial to his 1944 Nobel Prize, twenty years after he was first nominated.
  • John Lawrence used Berkeley cyclotron P-32 and found that mice with leukemia took up more of it than healthy mice. He extended the study to other animals and humans, which gave a rationale for giving P-32 to leukemia patients p. 666. fn 62 Hardin Jones and Waldo Cohn also studied P-32 metabolism. Lenoir and Hays: “The first therapeutic use of artificially produced radioisotopes occurred on Christmas Eve 1936.”
  • Cost: in 1938 one clinical dose of P-32 meant running the 37-inch cyclotron “for a day and a night”. Better methods followed, but the cyclotron “could not compete with the nuclear reactor” p. 666. fn 63 In 1938 Robert Wilson and Martin Kamen found a way to make phosphorus and iron isotopes at once, with higher specific activity.
  • By fall 1946, 150 leukemia patients had been treated with P-32. Its value was disputed. C. P. Rhoads, head of the Sloan-Kettering Memorial Cancer Hospital, said it killed cancer cells and eased some symptoms but did not prolong life “more than a few months” p. 666.

What users reported in 1948

The AEC's summer 1948 Semi-Annual Report to Congress summarised work with its isotopes at 136 American institutions. Of 336 entries, 80 involved phosphorus-32, and only five of those were in the physical sciences p. 666. fn 65 Creager entered the report into a database (FileMaker Pro 6) to count them. More than half the P-32 entries were biological and medical research, many of them clinical research on patients, particularly cancer patients:

  • Emory hospital: “Radiophosphorus has been used in several cases of polycythemia vera with good results. Our impression is that it is a valuable therapeutic agent.” fn 66 Polycythemia vera is an acquired bone-marrow disorder in which too many blood cells are made.
  • Jefferson Hospital: P-32 was a “satisfactory therapeutic agent” for chronic leukemia and polycythemia. The group also used P-32 and I-131 to “study the therapeutic and clinical effect on patients with polycythemia, leukemia, hyperthyroidism and cancer of the thyroid” pp. 666–667.

Hutchinson: the pond as an organism

One important use of P-32 was not medical. Right after the war the ecologist G. Evelyn Hutchinson used P-32 from the Yale cyclotron to trace phosphorus cycling through the phytoplankton and inorganic matter of Linsley Pond, Connecticut. Higher-specific-activity P-32 from Oak Ridge soon made the study more quantitative p. 667. fn 67 The 1947 study's P-32 was made by biophysicist Ernest F. Pollard at Yale, probably with the cyclotron.

This built on an analogy Hutchinson offered in 1940, reviewing a book by Frederick Clements: “If, as is insisted, the community is an organism, it should be possible to study the metabolism of that organism” p. 667. His first isotope work on the “metabolism” of carbon in the pond used a stable isotope, carbon-13.

Creager's view

Isotope tracing made Arthur Tansley's 1935 “ecosystem”, with its biotic and abiotic parts, concrete. But Tansley meant “ecosystem” as a neutral category to rid ecology of the organismal term “biotic community”. Tracing “metabolic pathways” in both physiology and ecology “kept the organismal conception in play”. The “representational practices” of mapping circulation showed “the epistemological links between metabolic biochemistry and biogeochemical studies of ecosystems”, even though ecological diagrams included non-living parts p. 667.

Subsection of Early UsesRadioiodine

From iodine-128 to iodine-131 · the one undeniable clinical success · the cancer “mirage” · the only true fission by-product

Claim
Radioiodine was the clear clinical success, in hyperthyroidism and in diagnosis, not in cancer. It was also the one isotope that fitted the public idea of a bomb material turned healer.
Evidence
Hertz, Roberts and Evans (1938); Livingood and Seaborg (1938); Hamilton and Soley; AEC reports of 1948 and 1955; the Atomic Energy Act's wording.
Against
The AEC's and journalists' promise that isotopes would cure cancer.

Before the reactors

  • The first radioiodine for physiology was iodine-128. MIT physicist Robley D. Evans made it with a radium-beryllium neutron source, and Massachusetts General Hospital used it despite its 26-minute half-life pp. 667–668.
  • 1938: S. Hertz, A. Roberts and Evans showed that it concentrated rapidly and selectively in the thyroids of injected rabbits.
  • 1938, Berkeley: J. J. Livingood and Glenn T. Seaborg announced the longer-lived iodine-131 p. 668.
  • Joseph Hamilton, with Mayo Solley of the medical school (spelled “Soley” in fn 73), gave radioiodine to patients by mouth. Overactive thyroids took up more than ten times as much as healthy ones, the basis for using I-131 against hyperthyroidism.
  • After the war a New York endocrinologist reported curing some thyroid cancers with I-131, but few tumours responded fn 74. Most thyroid tumours, including the most malignant, do not concentrate iodine the way normal thyroid tissue does.

The undeniable success

Among isotopes used experimentally in medicine, radioiodine “stood out by virtue of its undeniable clinical success” p. 668:

  • by 1948 I-131 was a standard treatment for hyperthyroidism (toxic goiter), used in about 20 US medical centres and shipped to many clinics abroad;
  • the Mayo Clinic reported success in 80% of cases;
  • physicians routinely used it to diagnose thyroid conditions, and also blood volume, brain tumours, liver tumours and cardiac output.

The cancer mirage

But diagnosis “was not the holy grail”. From the outset the agency, like the physicians and physicists of the 1930s, hoped isotopes would cure cancer. The AEC told Congress in 1948: “Radioisotopes will become a major weapon against cancer when dependable means are found to concentrate them in the malignant tissue and thus selectively to destroy it” p. 668.

Creager's view

“Well into the 1950s, journalists and agency officials perpetuated the mirage that investment in atomic energy would generate cancer cures, while practical applications of radioisotopes accumulated in the realm of diagnostics” pp. 668–669.

Seeing inside the body

Diagnostics was tracer methodology applied to medicine. Isotopes “brought into view key biological molecules, revealing their dynamic movement and chemical transformations”. Diagnostic isotopes built on the X-ray tradition “while moving the source of radiation from without the body to within it” p. 669. The AEC called isotopes in 1948 “the most powerful and searching laboratory tools yet devised for the investigation of the functioning and malfunctioning of particular organs; for studying the action of vitamins, hormones, enzymes, and other biochemical substances; and for testing the efficiency of many kinds of treatments” p. 669.

The only bomb by-product that healed

Iodine-131 was also “nearly unique among biomedical isotopes” in being a by-product of uranium fission in the reactor, so it could be extracted chemically from “the stew of reactor fission products”. It therefore fitted the Atomic Energy Act, which let the Commission “distribute, with or without charge, byproduct materials to applicants seeking such materials for research or development activity, medical therapy, industrial uses, or such other useful applications as may be developed” p. 669. Extraction was hard, so at first Oak Ridge made I-131 by irradiating tellurium. By the late 1940s it switched to extracting it from fission products, which greatly increased supply.

Creager's view

“If the public perception of atomic benefits rested on an idea that the same materials that yielded atomic weapons could also be used to cure disease, iodine-131 was only isotope that realized this wish” p. 669.

Subsection of Early UsesRadiocarbon

Stable-isotope tracing at Columbia · Kamen and Ruben · Calvin's photosynthesis cycle · Dyson's awe · the founder effect · from gift to commodity

Claim
Carbon-14 could tag almost any biological molecule and made the Calvin cycle and the metabolic-pathway boom possible, though it had no clinical use. Government supply turned a local gift economy into a subsidised, supply-driven commodity system that later hid its own origin.
Evidence
The Columbia stable-isotope program; Kamen and Ruben's work; Calvin's experiments (Figures 3–5); Dyson's letter; AEC cost comparisons and contracts with firms.
Against
The political promise that state production would “democratize” access.

Stable isotopes and the metabolism boom

Carbon-14 was the third most frequently shipped Oak Ridge isotope p. 669. Its demand was shaped by the cyclotron precedent and by the stable isotope carbon-13, used as a tracer by Harold Urey with Rudolph Schoenheimer, David Rittenberg and Mildred Cohn at Columbia. This followed Urey and Schoenheimer's pioneering use of deuterium to trace lipid metabolism. Urey concentrated isotopes just as intermediary metabolism became “a consolidated subfield at the forefront of biochemistry” (Holmes) p. 670.

  • Metabolism researchers mapped the cell's chains of reactions, opening the organismal “black box” of 19th-century input-output physiology. Nineteenth-century chemists had identified the compounds: sugars, amino acids, fatty acids, dicarboxylic and keto-acids. Their successors traced the reactions linking them in vivo, each step controlled by an enzyme.
  • 1936: Otto Meyerhof and W. Kiessling published the steps of glycolysis, the Embden-Meyerhof pathway. 1937: Hans Krebs announced the citric acid cycle. For Frederic Holmes these became “exemplary achievements” guiding biochemistry.
  • Isotopes beat chemical micromanometers for detecting metabolites, which were made in small amounts and quickly transformed. Schoenheimer's group used deuterium, nitrogen-15 and carbon-13 p. 670.

Kamen, Ruben and the first radiocarbon

  • At Berkeley, Martin Kamen, Sam Ruben and W. Z. Hassid first used carbon-11 to follow carbon dioxide fixation by photosynthetic and heterotrophic organisms p. 670. fn 86 They showed “dark” fixation of CO₂ in plants, bacteria and even mammalian systems, “to the disbelief of many established biochemists”.
  • Robin Hill at Cambridge had shown in the 1930s that photosynthesis's “light” and “dark” steps could be studied separately. The Berkeley team traced the dark steps, CO₂ to carbohydrate, and found the first step to be the transfer of carbon from CO₂ to a carboxyl group p. 671.
  • Carbon-11's 21-minute half-life meant working right next to a cyclotron.
  • Carbon-14, first isolated by Kamen and Ruben in 1940, has a half-life of 5700 years. The war broke up the partnership. Kamen went to uranium separation at the Rad Lab and Ruben to war gases, especially phosgene. An accident with phosgene killed Ruben in 1943 p. 671.
  • The first biological tracer experiment with C-14 was published after the war by Kamen and H. A. Barker fn 90: Barker and Kamen 1945.
  • Carbon is everywhere in living systems, and C-14 was highly sensitive: it could be diluted one-billion-fold and still be detected p. 671.

Calvin's photosynthesis cycle

Late in 1945 Ernest Lawrence persuaded Berkeley chemist Melvin Calvin to take up biomedical studies with carbon-14 already at the Rad Lab. Calvin recalled Lawrence saying they should do something “useful” after the Manhattan Project p. 671. fn 92 What brought Calvin close to Lawrence was his work extracting uranium–plutonium fission products for the Met Lab, not their shared faculty.

  • Calvin started with a small vial of barium ¹⁴C-carbonate inherited from Ruben and brought in Ruben's former collaborator Andrew Benson.
  • They filed four papers, with Hassid and Barker, as Manhattan District declassified reports fn 94. The work ran through the Radiation Laboratory and was soon funded by an AEC grant to Lawrence fn 95, contract W-7405-Eng-48. Calvin was an early buyer of Oak Ridge C-14, whose specific activity far exceeded Berkeley's cyclotron material p. 672.
  • The result was the Calvin (or Calvin-Benson) cycle (Figure 3). The group grew the single-celled green alga Chlorella pyrenoidosa as a culture suspension, fed with ordinary carbon dioxide, in a flat glass apparatus called the “lollipop” (Figure 4). They injected ¹⁴C-labelled CO₂ for seconds to minutes, killed the algae, and analysed their contents.
  • The main tool was two-dimensional paper chromatography with two solvents: different compounds “migrated in the two dimensional space as discrete spots”. Laying the paper on X-ray film showed which compounds had become radioactive, and how the label spread over time (Figure 5). The method was “much more rapid than traditional methods of organic chemistry, and visually impressive”.
  • The “algae juices” were separated on the paper. The work was “immensely fruitful”. Finding: CO₂ is converted first into phosphoglyceric acid, then through several steps into sugars such as fructose p. 672.

On 26 August 1948 the physicist Freeman Dyson heard Calvin lecture and admired how the pictures “show, in the most possible way, the progress of the delicate and transitory reactions through which the radio-carbon is assimilated” p. 672. For Dyson, Calvin's research was “dramatic evidence of the advances brought by atomic science”. He wrote to his family:

The long-sighted people said, when nuclear energy first came on the scene, that the application to biological research would be more important than the application to power. But I doubt if anyone expected that things would actually get going as fast as they have. This blotting-paper-plus-radio-activity technique is completely revolutionary because it means that any substance can be fed to a cell and its transformations followed second by second in detail, even in quantities too small to be seen or weighted, and with substances too unstable to stand old-fashioned stewing and chemical extraction.

Dyson, letter of 26 August 1948 · pp. 673–674 · fn 96
Creager's view

“The tone was exuberant, but Dyson's perspective was not hyperbolic” p. 674. Liquid scintillation counters later spread carbon-14 and tritium through biochemical assays. Reliance on tracers produced “so elaborate a proliferation of metabolic pathways as to boggle the minds of students of biochemistry” (Fruton).

No clinical use, but the best advertisement

C-14 could tag almost every molecule of biological interest. Unlike phosphorus, iodine, sodium and iron isotopes, radiocarbon “was not used in clinical application”. The AEC still cited C-14 sales to show a reactor's savings. The Oak Ridge pile could make 200 millicuries of carbon-14 in a few weeks for about $10,000, whereas “it would take 1000 cyclotrons to equal this output, and the operating cost would be well over a hundred million dollars” p. 674.

The founder effect

By 1948 phosphorus-32, carbon-14 and iodine-131 made up two-thirds of reported uses of AEC isotopes. Others in regular use were sulfur-35, calcium-45, iron-55/iron-59 (a mixture) and sodium-24 p. 675.

Creager's view

Early use shows a strong “founder effect”: scientists who could already get isotopes from cyclotrons were more likely to order from Oak Ridge. So “the political impetus that government production and distribution would ‘democratize’ access to this specialized tool was not immediately realized” p. 675.

From gift to commodity

The new mode of production still changed the scale and scope of isotope use and the relation between producer and consumer. With cyclotrons, both production and use were local, and co-authorship often reflected physicists' part in preparing the materials.

Creager's view

“The AEC commodified what had been largely a gift relationship between cyclotron physicists and their biological and medical users. The new supply system was not a true market; it was a supply-driven economy, subsidized by the government to fulfill political as well as scientific agendas” p. 675.

An all-inclusive supplier

  • The AEC tried to become an “all-inclusive isotope supply site”. Oak Ridge's Isotopes Division also distributed stable isotopes. In the late 1940s it planned to distribute rare isotopes that only cyclotrons could make, because “cyclotron laboratories [in universities] are not in a position to handle the voluminous administrative detail involved in correspondence related to distribution, screening of formal requests, shipment problems, and legal arrangements” p. 675.
  • It would coordinate the cyclotrons at MIT, the University of Pittsburgh and the University of California, “maintaining a uniform policy on health safety requirements, on disposal procedures, and on criteria for use in human beings”.
  • Cyclotron isotopes were to be subsidised even more heavily: users would pay no more than one-third of the real cost p. 676.

Private firms, and a hidden origin

The AEC was careful not to discourage a commercial industry for radioactive materials and detectors. Biologists usually wanted a tagged molecule, not the isotope itself. So the AEC contracted Abbott Laboratories (Chicago) and Tracerlab (Boston) to develop labelled compounds for sale. Abbott built a radiopharmaceutical plant in Oak Ridge to use AEC radionuclides on site. By the end of 1949 AEC contracts with six private firms had made 65 radiolabelled compounds available p. 676. This mixing of public and private came from the Act's government monopoly over fissionable materials and nuclear patents.

Creager's view

Commercialisation “increasingly obscured the site of origin of isotopic tracers”. Researchers who bought labelled compounds from companies like New England Nuclear, which bought its radionuclides from the government, “were less likely to appreciate the centrality of the federal government to the laboratory supply of isotopes” p. 676.

Conclusions

Isotopes as a new way of seeing · radiobiology as an umbrella · rethinking the AEC beyond the military · “cure as well as kill”

Claim
Isotopes gave biology “a new mode of perception” focused on dynamic transformation. They pulled radiobiology towards biochemistry and molecular biology, and they show an AEC driven as much by politics as by military aims.
Evidence
AEC reports (1948, 1951); Hollaender (1963); the Forman–Kevles debate; the 1956 literature survey.
Against
Histories of physics that read the AEC through military patronage and complicity.

A new mode of perception

Since the 1930s scientists had praised tracers for making unseen life processes visible, and the AEC used “metaphors of visibility and illumination”, often comparing isotopes to the microscope pp. 676–677:

  • 1948 semi-annual report: “As tracers, [radioisotopes] are proving themselves the most useful new research tool since the invention of the microscope in the 17th Century; in fact, they represent that rarest of all scientific advances, a new mode of perception” p. 677.
  • 1951 report: “Because of the special ability to chart their course through living organisms and intact objects, isotopes have been called the most important scientific tool developed since the microscope.” fn 108 The citation is AEC 1952, p. 100. The trope was common: in the 1930s A. V. Hill argued that, as the microscope let biologists see cells, isotopes would let them “see” life at the atomic level (from Warren Weaver's diary, in Kohler 1991).

But tracers were meant to show dynamic transformations, not structures, “epitomized by the schematics of metabolic pathways”. Bioenergetics researchers used P-32 to follow ATP as an energy currency. Cycles mattered most, in biochemistry and ecology alike, showing energy and materials moving through organisms and communities, and they raised “new questions about the economy of life” p. 677. Combined with metabolic mutants, tracers showed how the cell regulated biosynthesis. The new study of feedback regulation also drew on cybernetics fn 109.

Radiobiology as an umbrella

Strictly, “radiobiology”, meaning research on radiation's effects on living things, “effectively excluded the use of radioisotopes as biological tracers”, since tracer users kept doses too small to disturb life fn 110. The definition was rarely enforced. Radiobiology “was less a coherent program of inquiry than it was an umbrella” for biomedical research tied to radiation or funded by national atomic energy authorities pp. 677–678. The isotope program took the AEC into far more laboratories than its targeted genetics and ecology programs. For years it was “the most conspicuous example” of atomic benefits to biology and medicine.

Alexander Hollaender wanted isotope results counted as radiobiology. In 1963 he argued that biochemistry and molecular biology had extended radiobiology beyond its physical approaches:

The new developments in our knowledge of the transfer of information from the nucleus to the cytoplasm which is involved in nucleic acid and protein synthesis have opened up entirely new fields and ways of looking at biology. These developments have not only influenced radiation research, but radiation research has often opened the way for these advances. Radio- and stable isotopes have made possible investigations which were formerly impossible.… The development in our understanding of genetic coding, which has become so important in the last few months, would have been impossible without the progress made in radiation biology.

Hollaender 1963 · p. 678 · fn 111
Creager's view

“The radioisotopes that had originated as a ‘by-product’ of atomic technologies became a driving force in postwar biology” p. 678.

Rethinking the AEC

Counting the AEC's contributions to biology and medicine, both its isotopes and its research programs, “necessitates a reassessment of the historiography about the consequences of the atomic bomb for postwar science” p. 678.

  • Historians of physics ask how military interest shaped physics, and whether defence money “discouraged fundamental science in favor of applied and often classified work” fn 112. Not all agree; the debate is Forman (1987) against Kevles (1990).
  • That image of scientists “complicit in the agenda of the military” can be extended to AEC-funded, often secret experiments in which people ingested or were injected with isotopes, especially studies seeking a “lowest permissible dose” p. 679. fn 113 The most egregious involved injecting human subjects with plutonium (Welsome 1999).
Creager's view

“However, this interpretive framework fails to capture the full range of the AEC's initiatives in biology and medicine, many of which were driven by political concerns rather than military objectives” p. 679.

Quantitative, not qualitative

  • Biological and medical uses of radioactivity predated the Manhattan Project, so the bomb's changes were “initially quantitative, not qualitative”: the scale and mode of production p. 679.
  • Still, affordable isotopes on this scale are “unthinkable without the existence of nuclear reactors”. fn 114 The US, first with the bomb, also had the highest scientific use of isotopes: in 1956, 39% of biochemistry papers in a leading American journal used isotopes, against 18% in a British journal and 7% each in German and Russian ones (Broda).
  • Unlike weapons, isotope production was “most closely allied with unclassified science”, and domestic distribution rules “focused more on safety than security”. fn 115 This was not true of international distribution (Creager 2002).
  • The AEC stressed isotopes in its reports and press releases “because it could say so much about this program, especially in comparison to the activities of its ‘Division of Production’” p. 679.
Creager's final sentence

“In the end, the isotope program represented the fundamental dream of the AEC - a civilian agency charged with designing and making nuclear weapons - that atoms could cure as well as kill” p. 679.

Abstract, acknowledgements, footnotes

Abstract and keywords · funding and thanks · all 115 footnotes classified · references not summarised

Abstract p. 649. Radioisotopes in biomedical research and therapy became “one of the major consequences of the ‘physicists' war’ for postwar life science”. Manhattan Project scientists, campaigning for civilian atomic energy, proposed a government-run distribution program using wartime infrastructure. The Manhattan Project began shipping from Oak Ridge after the Atomic Energy Bill passed but before the AEC was formally established. Reactor isotopes were put to many of the uses pioneered with cyclotron isotopes in the 1930s and early 1940s. Most early shipments were radioiodine and radiophosphorus, used to evaluate thyroid function, diagnose disorders and irradiate tumours. Researchers and politicians praised their promise against disease, especially cancer, but isotopes “proved less successful than anticipated in treating cancer and more successful in medical diagnostics”. In research they let biologists “trace molecular transformations from metabolic pathways to ecosystems”. Government production and promotion stimulated use at home and abroad until isotopes were routine. In the early postwar years they “signified the government's commitment to harness the atom for peace, particularly through contributions to biology, medicine, and agriculture”. Let there be light … and bread

The United Nations, the developing world, and atomic energy's Green Revolution · Jacob Darwin Hamblin

The article in one breath

Atoms for Peace was meant to feed the world as well as light it. From 1957 the new International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) pushed hard into agriculture: fertilizer tracers, insect sterilization, food and grain irradiation and mutation breeding. Most of it was aimed at “developing” countries where nuclear power was out of reach. The UN's Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) saw showmanship without agricultural expertise. Their 1964 compromise, a Joint Division based in Vienna, handed the field to the IAEA. Its FAO member, the British plant geneticist Ronald Silow, protested for years that the IAEA was using poor countries as “an experimental zone” for techniques rich countries had dropped: Tanzania's beef irradiator, Turkey's grain irradiator at Iskenderun, a herd of “white elephants”. The IAEA and its FAO allies isolated, ridiculed and suppressed him until he retired, and the official history later erased him. The Green Revolution's amber “Sharbati Sonora” wheat gave the IAEA the success it craved, though its protein “miracle” had nothing to do with radiation. Hamblin argues that by pinning the critique on one difficult man, the IAEA manufactured a UN consensus that “modern” meant “atomic”. An agency whose purpose was to promote a technology could not put development first: “Development was only a secondary objective at the IAEA” p. 42.

Introduction

Claim
Atomic agriculture at the UN is a story of manufactured consensus. The IAEA reshaped the UN towards one technological path by containing dissent in one man.
Evidence
The FAO archives on the FAO/IAEA Joint Division; Silow's own memoranda.
Against
The official history's “unity of purpose”; the ingenuity story of the Green Revolution; modernization theory.

A man no one believed

In the early 1970s the retired plant geneticist Ronald Silow was “raving to journalists about a ‘fraudulent conspiracy’ at the United Nations” p. 25. fn 1 His talk with the BBC is recorded in an unsigned FAO memorandum. His charges against the FAO and the IAEA were these:

  • both had silenced him and hid information from member nations;
  • they promoted unproven agricultural uses of atomic energy in developing countries, “despite the techniques being disavowed in richer countries”;
  • they wasted millions of the poorest countries' money so a few American and European scientists could continue projects “that their own countries had cancelled”;
  • worst of all, they suppressed all dissent, “he was the proof of that”.

“Few listened to his accusation of gross misconduct”, because “his words defied belief”. Most dismissed him as “an embittered former employee”. His opponents saw him as “an annoying obstacle” on “the obvious path that all countries, especially those of the developing world, needed to take” to modern cultivation p. 25.

Why readers today may listen

Today's readers “might be more sympathetic”, given widespread scepticism about science, technology and modernization. Most historians no longer assume innovation causes progress. They see that “scientific agendas and technological artifacts reinforce the priorities of those who design or promote them” p. 25. fn 2 Examples include Forman on military patronage, Latour, Hughes and Mumford on technology's power, and Hecht on the politics of French reactors.

Modernization theory is under scrutiny from scholars who see influence exerted “through ostensibly universal notions such as ‘modern,’ ‘advanced,’ or ‘cutting edge’” fn 3. Even Walt Rostow knew his “modern” was meant to compete with the Soviet future. Recent work on the 1960s Green Revolution, “when famine-threatened Asia was ‘saved’” by plant breeding, irrigation and fertilizer, shows the story “was constructed by foreign relations goals, taking into account the spectacle of providing science and technology to those in need” p. 26 (Cullather; Perkins fn 4).

Atomic energy's own Green Revolution

“Although it is less well-known, atomic energy also had its ‘Green Revolution’ in the 1960s.” It came from foreign policy and also from “the far-reaching apparatus of the United Nations and its specialized agencies” p. 26. The atom was “a symbol of American mastery of nature's terrible power” and a gift promised to the world, echoing Isaiah's swords into plowshares.

  • President Dwight Eisenhower's “Atoms for Peace” speech at the end of 1953 promised pooled fissionable material, peaceful reactors, and an international agency under the UN to keep peaceful uses from “tempting others to develop a bomb” fn 5. Chernus and Osgood stress the speech's propaganda value.
  • The IAEA was born four years later, in 1957. Many of its efforts were in agriculture: fertilizer studies, insect sterilization, food preservation, grain disinfestation and mutation plant breeding fn 6. Food irradiation is the one application historians have studied. Spiller shows UN endorsements helped its advocates. Buchanan calls it a “cold war science” that offered consumers little and gave scientists money and prestige.
  • By the late 1960s atomic energy had a place in the Green Revolution story through the amber-coloured variety of Sonora-64 wheat, marketed in India as Sharbati Sonora, which supposedly “prevented an apparent famine and delayed a Malthusian crisis” p. 26 fn 7.

Three questions

Behind “the conventional story of scientific ingenuity triumphing over nature” lay serious questions about atomic energy in “the countries dubbed ‘developing’ by the UN” p. 26:

  1. Why did so many path-breaking efforts to apply atomic energy to agriculture take place in developing countries?
  2. Did the UN encourage atomic agriculture “at the expense of other methods”?
  3. Atomic energy was a vision of modernization that reflected US foreign policy, so why did the UN “fall so fully in line” with it?

From the late 1950s the UN endorsed and promoted an array of agricultural uses. The FAO and the IAEA “worked in unison, even creating a special FAO/IAEA Joint Division in 1964”. The IAEA's official history “suggests unity of purpose between the two agencies and a refreshing lack of controversy compared to national pursuits”. “The archival records of the FAO/IAEA Joint Division tell a different story”: a controversy “more intense than any national one” and a bitter personal conflict p. 26 fn 8.

Hamblin's stance

Silow's experience is “extensively documented in FAO's archives in Rome”. “This essay does not resuscitate Silow as a hero; he made some poor choices, and the reader can be the judge, but his adversaries also were guilty of hiding information, suppressing ideas, and keeping issues invisible to the general public.” On one level it is the story of “an unlucky and difficult individual who saw himself as the sole voice for the developing world, but whose career, reputation, and influence completely disintegrated in the late 1960s” p. 26.

Two philosophies of development

Behind Silow's story lay “a sharp philosophical divide about the UN's role in fostering modernization” pp. 26–27:

  • Rational planners wanted UN policy guidelines for all the specialized agencies, so that science and technology served economic development.
  • “Science and technology first”: support local scientists doing world-class research and local officials running new projects, here atomic agriculture.

Whatever “the veracity of Silow's more audacious allegations”, for Silow the Joint Division's “original sin” was “its unwillingness to establish an overriding policy to align science and technology with prudent economic development”. “To Silow, the atom was promising, but not uniquely so.” Atomic applications had the status of “prestigious, modern technologies”, but promoting them “did not seem to be true modernization”. It “smacked of opportunism – by the IAEA and by individual scientists, politicians, and contractors – cloaked under the name of development” p. 27. The IAEA backed atomic agriculture “with no explicit rationale beyond its own mandate to promote the atom”. Through its conferences, its laboratories and “its generous research contracts”, it “seemed less interested in planned development and more interested in promoting particular technological solutions and pursuing particular scientists' research agendas”.

The IAEA's defence was that it forced no country to take its money, and developing countries were free to choose. “If developing countries believed that ‘modern’ meant ‘atomic,’ it was not the responsibility of the UN to disabuse them of that notion” p. 27.

The IAEA's three victories p. 27
  1. It secured dominance in all UN affairs dealing with atomic energy in agriculture, despite the FAO's annoyance that it tempted developing countries “toward dubious, unproven solutions”.
  2. It took total control of the FAO/IAEA Joint Division “by excluding, suppressing, and containing its FAO member (Silow)”, a process “that Silow fought tooth and nail”.
  3. It brushed aside the main critique, that promoting atomic energy in the developing world was “fundamentally irresponsible” and that the UN should include atomic energy “only as one of a range of agricultural possibilities for developing countries”.

By centring the objection on Silow, “from whom even FAO officials tried to distance themselves”, the IAEA “established a culture at the UN that virtually disallowed skepticism about atomic energy in agriculture, giving the appearance of unanimity and thus of international endorsement”.

The FAO's hopes

The UN's first reaction to Eisenhower's speech was enthusiastic, and each specialized agency tried to carve out a role p. 27. fn 9 UNESCO struggled to define its part. The 1955 Geneva conference on peaceful uses, proposed by the US, was in Krige's words a “masterpiece of marketing”, with a working US reactor, and both Cold War sides used it for promotion. Eisenhower had named “abundant electrical energy in the power-starved areas of the world” as the future agency's special purpose, alongside “agriculture, medicine, and other peaceful pursuits”.

Agriculture “especially tantalized” the FAO. Its director-general, the Indian Binay Ranjan Sen, easily connected nuclear energy to the agency's motto, fiat panis, “let there be bread” p. 27. fn 10 Sen had tried to fold the 1954 US Agricultural Trade Development and Assistance Act, later called “Food for Peace”, into his projects, leading to the Freedom from Hunger Campaign of 1959. Amy Staples notes that the Americans' cool response suggested they were “less interested in development than in bilateral programs to dump their surpluses”. Atoms for Peace was another way for the FAO to claim a stake. The FAO's plant experts saw uses for isotopes in fertilizer studies and for irradiation in preserving food, controlling insects and creating new seeds p. 28.

Ronald Silow

The FAO's leading voice on atomic energy was the British plant geneticist Ronald A. Silow. He had made his name at the Cotton Research Station in Trinidad, British West Indies, and co-authored its final report, published in 1947, which became “a standard for agricultural genetics”. The book showed man-made environments driving evolutionary change: human beings had been “the primary influence upon plant evolution” p. 28.

fn 11 The station was run by the Empire Cotton Growing Corporation from 1926 as a clearinghouse for the world's varieties of cotton (Gossypium); the corporation later moved its research to Africa. The authors controversially traced some South American cottons to India, implying humans carried the crop across the Pacific before Europeans arrived. Bentley Glass ranked the book “among the most important books on plant evolution”; later cytogenetic studies countered some of its claims. fn 12 The authors called the human–plant relationship “a true symbiosis”.

Silow expected the FAO to lead research coordination, expert training and advice to governments fn 13. After visiting laboratories in 1956 he found research lacking focus: Germans on meat irradiation, Norwegians on the storage life of fruit and vegetables, Danes on agricultural tracers, Swedes and Norwegians on radiation-induced mutations for breeding. He believed “we are only at the very beginning of a very important and rapidly expanding new phase in human experience”, and that the FAO could lead p. 28.

The IAEA arrives (1957–60)

After the IAEA's creation in 1957, “a turf battle consumed both agencies”. Eisenhower had mentioned agriculture, and agriculture “seemed the most sensible way to introduce economically relevant atomic energy into areas lacking the infrastructure and expertise required for nuclear-powered electricity” p. 28.

  • Fellowships grew from nine in 1958 to 130 by 1963, with training courses, exchange professorships, laboratories, research contracts, conferences and its own research at the Seibersdorf Laboratory in Austria fn 14.
  • Radioactive phosphate measured how much fertilizer crops took up, in seven countries from Hungary to the Philippines fn 15.
  • The first IAEA technical assistants went to Thailand and Tunisia in 1960.
  • In Southeast Asia the IAEA hoped radiation could control plant diseases such as cadang-cadang in coconuts fn 16. The virus ravaged the Philippines: San Miguel Island had 250,000 coconut trees before 1961, and by 1965 only 80 still bore fruit.

FAO leaders felt the IAEA “had asserted a dubious claim of agricultural expertise” and treated the FAO as a junior partner. The FAO resented four 1959 IAEA conferences, in the US, Poland, Argentina and Austria, that discussed food irradiation with little or no FAO consultation. At one IAEA General Conference a South African delegate spoke of a “crisis of confidence” caused by the IAEA's “claim of universal competence” p. 28 fn 17.

Karlsruhe, 1960

After “a prickly period of interagency bickering”, the two agencies co-sponsored a 1960 symposium in Karlsruhe on the genetic effects of radiation on seeds. Swedish scientists presented barley and mustard. Italians claimed improved wheat with stronger straw, more pest resistance and a thicker protective wax. “Clearly, breeders had found a powerful tool” p. 28. The American Arnold Sparrow, who studied plants' sensitivity to radiation, said Karlsruhe marked the “end of an era” in radiobiology p. 29.

“Amid acclamations about a new scientific era, the turf battle continued.” The IAEA trumpeted Karlsruhe as a major aspect of peaceful uses; the FAO linked it to its Freedom from Hunger Campaign. The IAEA's director-general, Sterling Cole, praised the conference “as an exemplar of cooperation across disciplines, across frontiers, and between international organizations”. “Diplomatically” he called the FAO the leading exponent of food and agriculture and the IAEA the agency “to whom the predominant role in the field of atomic energy has been allotted”. In the three years since the agency's inception, most had come to realize that atomic power was “a distant possibility in most countries”. Cole saw agriculture as a way to “direct our attack on hunger and disease” among peoples “not ready for power reactors” p. 29.

Showmen, not managers

FAO leaders felt the inexperienced IAEA was running the show, and “as showmen rather than as rational managers” p. 29. The FAO's Gösta Julén said irradiation was just one more way to produce changes in varieties: perhaps one mutation in a thousand might yield a useful trait, and no one should replace classical breeding with irradiation fn 21. Silow called the IAEA's work “haphazard” and “chaotic”, run by staff “with little or no contact with world needs in agriculture”, and predicted an “explosive point” when governments would stop tolerating it.

Hamblin's view

“What began as a turf battle was becoming a serious divergence in outlook: the IAEA was promoting atomic solutions, whereas FAO saw itself promoting sound agriculture, possibly using the atom” p. 29. The FAO saw a conflict of interest between the IAEA and the developing world.

The IAEA's scientific advisory committee made the point. It was made up of senior figures from national atomic establishments: John Cockcroft (UK), Isidor Rabi (US), Vassily Emelyanov (USSR), Bertrand Goldschmidt (France), Homi Bhabha (India) and Wilfrid Lewis (Canada). “They knew little of agricultural practices”, but their prestige and money threatened to drown out the FAO. The IAEA's high-profile “omnibus” conferences drew good people away from the FAO's specialised meetings fn 22. Silow complained that the IAEA promoted “everything under the sun (or rather, under the atom)” with no plan or defined agricultural policy p. 29 fn 23.

Sterile insects

The sterile male technique was a “‘biological’ form of pest control”: irradiate and release hundreds of millions of insects a week; the sterile males out-compete fertile ones for females p. 29. In the early 1960s the US Department of Agriculture used it with “stunning success” against the screwworm fly in North America. fn 24 The success was premature, since the screwworm returned, but the method was vindicated. The IAEA's leading agriculture scientist, the American Maurice “Mac” Fried, thought it could work on the Mediterranean fruit fly, the tsetse fly (sleeping sickness), the mosquito (malaria) and even the housefly. “Journalists devoured the IAEA promises.” After Fried spoke at the 1964 Conference on the Peaceful Uses of Atomic Energy, Walter Sullivan of the New York Times asked: “Will the screened porch be obsolete a generation from now?” p. 30

Irradiated grain and food

Since World War II, international bodies including the FAO had sought ways to save grain from infestation: “as much as half a shipload” might be lost in transit. Scientists proposed irradiating grain to kill or sterilize stowaway pests, and food to extend shelf life and kill bacteria such as salmonella in meat and eggs p. 30. fn 27 Food and agriculture had been one of six fields in the US National Academy of Sciences' 1956 report on the biological effects of atomic radiation, with “very encouraging” results.

Because many salmonella-carrying foods came from developing countries that lacked sanitation, “the IAEA targeted them first”. Fried said that without salmonella, countries like Thailand could export eggs, poultry and pork to Europe and the US: with atomic energy they “soon would possess valuable export goods that presently perished far too quickly to be significant” p. 30. “FAO looked upon these predictions with dismay.”

Protest, then compromise (1963–64)

Sen saw “an ebullient new agency stepping far outside its own realm of expertise and trampling on FAO's efforts around the world”. He wrote to the IAEA's director-general, the Swede Sigvard Eklund (who replaced Cole in 1961), that the IAEA's unilateral activities in food and agriculture worried member governments fn 29. FAO scientists complained that the IAEA exploited the fact that “the term ‘atomic energy’ began to assume almost magical powers to the uninitiated”. They believed only the FAO could use old and new techniques and judge between them. The IAEA was “only capable of developing sound practices insofar as they involved the use of atomic energy” p. 30.

In 1964 the two directors-general created the FAO/IAEA Joint Division for Atomic Energy in Agriculture. “If Sen hoped to increase FAO's influence with this arrangement, he made a grave error in judgment. FAO's independent voice in the field of atomic energy would never recover” p. 30.

  • In a personnel swap “intended to mark the compromise and diminish inter-agency rivalry”, the IAEA made Fried an FAO employee, and the FAO moved Silow to the IAEA.
  • The whole division stayed at IAEA headquarters in Vienna, near Seibersdorf.
  • Fried welcomed Silow's group with Austrian wine and Viennese songs. The IAEA staffer Björn Sigurbjörnsson later recalled: “our colleagues interpreted this as a celebration of our victory over them. Maybe this was not so far from the truth: we had stayed in Vienna and our beloved chief, Mac Fried, continued as our boss” p. 30.
  • Fried became director and Silow deputy-director, “giving the appearance but not the reality of FAO dominance”. “The seeds of intense personal conflict began to germinate.”

The IAEA's picture of Silow as a mere troublemaker. Hamblin also records Silow's own bad judgment.

The 1966 appraisal

“In little more than a year, Ronald Silow made many enemies at IAEA and kept open the festering wound” between the agencies p. 30. Working in Vienna showed him the IAEA's activities and, in his view, its “blindness toward sound agricultural policy in the developing world”. In early 1966 he sent a report to Eklund and Fried and copied it to all his former FAO colleagues p. 31. fn 32 It was the interoffice memorandum of 20 January 1966, “Appraisal of the Programme of Work of Atomic Energy in Agriculture”.

  • The FAO and its member governments were “being seriously misled”.
  • The IAEA had misspent US$1.5 million between 1962 and 1966, about half a million of it “from the already cash-strapped coffers of developing countries”.

It was “the first of many extremely long-winded memoranda against the joint division's work”.

Silow's case, as Hamblin summarises it

Developing countries had explicitly asked the FAO not to encourage techniques until industrialized countries had proven them feasible. The Joint Division did the opposite, “using the developing world as an experimental zone for ideas that industrialized countries were not willing to adopt”. Advocates who had lost funding battles at home promoted discontinued projects as “modern” and “advanced” abroad. The IAEA justified them by “continued reiteration of transparently excessive claims”, transparent only to trained scientists in industrialized countries. Such promises were why the FAO set up its own atomic program in 1954, to give governments objective advice. Now those efforts were “overshadowed by the IAEA's extravagant claims and expenditures” p. 31.

The FAO's half-support

FAO scientists reacted more positively than IAEA ones p. 31:

  • On fertilizer tracer studies, which Silow said were unlikely to give definitive results, they said guardedly that more study was needed.
  • They agreed that grain disinfestation by irradiation would be premature in developing countries, since it “had not even been approved in industrialized countries” fn 34.
  • On mutation breeding, they agreed atomic energy “was simply a tool to increase variability, hardly a panacea”. J. Vallega, director of the Plant Production and Protection Division, agreed with Silow “that it is very dangerous to induce developing countries to concentrate on these techniques which can divert them from specific objectives, forcing them to invest substantial amounts of money and hampering practical results”.
  • A breeding program needed a specialist leader trained in agronomy, plant breeding and seed production.

Sidelined and abandoned

The IAEA leadership saw Silow as “a troublemaker in their midst”. “Miffed at his inter-agency exposé”, Eklund gave him a new job: writing a report on the history of nuclear techniques in food and agriculture p. 31. The reassignment “sidelined FAO influence completely”, especially as Fried was now technically the FAO member.

In August 1966, alone inside the IAEA, Silow sent Sen a 26-page handwritten letter. He complained that IAEA colleagues disparaged his part in the FAO's work and questioned his competence, and that his former FAO supervisors had all left. He pleaded for help “after 35 years of productive and recognized service, in increasingly responsible positions, almost all in or on behalf of developing countries, under conditions that were often by no means easy, and sometimes entailed considerable risk to my family” pp. 31–32.

Sen kept his distance, for reasons that “are obscure” p. 32. Hamblin offers three guesses:

  1. Sen cared more about keeping the FAO's name on the division than about its work.
  2. He knew he had been outmanoeuvred and did not want to fight the IAEA and its powerful backers in the US and Europe.
  3. He sensed Silow was a poor ally.

“In a strange reversal, Fried henceforth became the principal FAO voice in atomic energy matters.” Sen passed Silow's confidential letters to subordinates, so his personal appeals, mixed with policy objections, reached mid-level FAO staff. This “made him a laughing stock” p. 32.

The Consultant Group (1966)

A Consultant Group was formed to give the division the policy coherence Silow said it lacked. Its members were Sir John Cockcroft (UK), A. W. Lindquist (USA), A. Gustafsson (Sweden) and C. Dakshinamurti (India). Cockcroft met Silow, but the meeting only confirmed “what the consultants already had heard from Fried and others”. Fried noted that the consultants “often spoke about Silow in official deliberations and in informal conversations”, and wrote that “from a subjective point of judgment” he and others “had the definite impression that the members of the Consultants' Group were acutely and quite fully aware of the extreme unhappiness of Dr Silow” p. 32. “In other words, Silow was perceived as a depressed and emotional man whose objections had little relevance to the matters at hand.”

  • The group's 1966 report noted the agencies' “somewhat different” outlooks but fully endorsed the IAEA's plans, “invoking the world protein crisis as a justification”. Its only criticism was that the division could not yet support fields such as biochemistry and plant physiology fn 39.
  • The problem with the FAO “increasingly materialized around Silow rather than the agency as a whole”. The group called his criticisms “unjustified”.
  • Lindquist said the group helped “clear the air”. That was “no surprise, since ‘FAO’ now meant ‘Fried,’ and Silow was marginalized, undoubtedly the butt of gossip and disdain”. He added: “Regarding Dr Silow, we all feel sorry for the man. He has written numerous highly critical and unjustified letters to many people. His last 30-page letter went beyond good taste and was really acrimonious” p. 32. Leaving Silow aside, the group “envisioned a much more intensified effort to incorporate atomic energy into agriculture”.

The 49-page memorandum (March 1967)

Silow formally asked for the Consultant Report to be withdrawn from distribution and publication, because it “sidestepped many of the views within FAO, while completely disregarding his own”. In March 1967 he sent Sen and Eklund a 49-page memorandum restating the FAO philosophy that techniques should not be encouraged in developing countries until proven in industrialized ones p. 32. Because of “stark policy differences”, IAEA attitudes to him had become “vindictive” and “hostile”. His work for the FAO “has led to the virtual termination of normal professional life for me for the past two-and-a-half years since being transferred to the IAEA staff”. He could not travel to conferences, was excluded from policy talks, and was ridiculed: “I have been almost completely isolated from my professional contacts, both inside and outside the United Nations family, and from my agricultural profession of a previous 35 years standing” p. 33.

Hamblin's view

“No one listened.” Silow's choice to treat the report as a personal affront, and “his poor judgment in crafting such long and vitriolic diatribes”, hardened the IAEA against him. Such behaviour made it hard for Sen to back his policy recommendations p. 33.

Claim
Silow widened the fight into an attack on motives. Commercial pressure and scientists who had lost at home drove the IAEA to push unproven irradiation on developing countries. The IAEA answered by suppressing his paper and making him the issue.
Evidence
Silow's 1967 memoranda and conference paper; PSAC's 1967 report; Goddard's speech; the IAEA's rebuttal; Eklund's letter; Nucleonics Week; the official history.
Against
The IAEA's claim to be merely “the servant of the Member States”.

Sales pressures

Silow now claimed that the IAEA “bowed to commercial demands and forced the pace of atomic energy research in the areas least appropriate – developing countries” p. 33.

  • Isochem, a US company making industrial isotopes from radioactive waste, worried in 1967 that production was outstripping demand. Isotope production had become a “white elephant” doomed to failure, so the firm looked abroad, especially to the developing world. For Silow, “sales pressures” translated into “programmatic pressures” in international agencies.
  • In the US the only major buyer of irradiated food was the Department of Defense, and the USDA hesitated to approve it despite over $20 million of US research.
  • Yet with IAEA guidance Tanzania agreed to build, with an American firm, its own $4 million irradiation plant for sterilized beef, with isotopes from Atomic Energy of Canada. It was to start in 1969, “even prior to the first US one” p. 33.

There should be at least three years commercial production and consumer experience with such a radically new process, in a country like the United States with almost unlimited resources for technical and economic evaluation of the process, before thought is given to transferring such an extremely complex technology to countries in the very early stages of their technical and economic development.

Silow, March 1967 · p. 33

He accused the enthusiasts of trying to “force the pace” where “even the slightest unforeseen circumstance could cause the breakdown of an entire operation”.

Turkey's grain irradiator

IAEA disinfestation programs followed failed ones in rich countries, and biased information put the UN in an “untenable” and “embarrassing” position p. 33.

  • Canada had spent $1.5 million on potato irradiation and judged it unpromising.
  • Yet the UN planned to spend more on grain irradiation in Turkey, which, Silow said, “will inevitably fail”. It would be inappropriate “to convey any hope of economic viability to the Turks”.
  • The facility at Iskenderun would cost “at least five to seven times” more than other disinfestation methods, and could handle less than 1% of Turkey's grain. “Even if the radiation process were economically viable there – which it cannot be – there are no other known locations in Turkey where it could be used” pp. 33–34.
  • The site was chosen for a pest, the khapra beetle, but there was no entomological evidence that it suited irradiation, and no commercial potential “or anywhere else in the world”. Turkey would do better with conventional fumigation in farm granaries and regional depots p. 34.

Scientific refugees

Silow suspected that atomic scientists who had failed to win support at home “were carving out a haven at the UN” p. 34.

  • In the USDA, atomic advocates were outnumbered, and the disaffected went to the IAEA. Fried was a former USDA scientist.
  • Britain's Wantage Radiation Laboratory had cut back grain irradiation. If Britain found it not worth the money, why should a developing country go ahead? Silow implied that British scientists used international bodies to extend programs cancelled at home.
  • Henry Seligman had supervised Wantage's work. After Britain cut the agricultural program to about a third and dropped several projects, Seligman became IAEA deputy director-general and started the Seibersdorf Laboratory. His former boss, Cockcroft, chaired the Consultants Group.

Silow: “the grain irradiation project, which was terminated for good reason by the UK Government at the Wantage Research Laboratory, was immediately reconstituted in virtually identical form here on the international level” p. 34.

Hamblin's view

“This characterization is not far from the one made decades later by IAEA's official historian, David Fischer.” In a footnote Fischer says that Seligman and the Italian physicist Carlo Salvetti had been “dissatisfied with the direction their establishments were taking and sought scientific refuge in the IAEA” p. 34. Silow said that when he and the FAO insisted on policy controls he was “silenced and discredited”.

“Judge, as well as expert adviser”

Silow alleged he had been pressured to go along with IAEA decisions, with professional ostracism as the penalty. One letter suggested duplicity by Eklund p. 34:

Dr Eklund, you will remember that my criticisms were continued in 1966 because no effective action was taken on my earlier suggestions on this matter … following which you had told me that no option was given to me but to agree always with the Director of the Division … . You will remember that during your discussion with me … I agreed to your proposal, only for the time being, in order to give you the time that you said you needed to correct the situation quietly … .

Silow to Eklund · p. 34

Instead the programs continued and Silow was humiliated. He wrote of “untold damage” and said the IAEA bore the “moral responsibility for having raised false hopes” in the developing world. He asked Sen and Eklund to ensure that, when expensive and controversial proposals were judged, “an extremely limited group of individuals and interests do not act singly or jointly in various combinations at different times as originator, plaintiff, defendant, witness on both sides, advocate, clerk of the court, and sometimes even as judge, as well as expert adviser to all of them” pp. 34–35. Iskenderun, Tanzania, Wantage and the rest were “the herd of other expensive but dying ‘white elephants’”, born of a forced marriage of atomic energy and agriculture by national governments, foreign policy, and now the IAEA p. 35.

PSAC's doubts (April 1967)

In April 1967 Silow tried to go public with a paper for the IAEA General Conference fn 60. He leaned on the US President's Science Advisory Committee (PSAC), whose 1967 report The World Food Problem doubted whether irradiation made food toxic. PSAC concluded: “[t]hese and other problems indicate that radiation is not likely to have a significant application for food preservation in the foreseeable future” p. 35. Silow cited experiments showing toxic effects in organisms raised on irradiated food: slower cell growth and division, more mutations and chromosome breakage, in fruit flies, rats, mice and human tissue cultures. Worldwide adoption through the UN, especially in developing countries, was “clearly entirely premature and unwise”.

The IAEA's counter-attack

  • It cited other Americans and cast doubt on the person who drafted some PSAC statements, “belittling him as a mere employee of Campbell Soup Company” p. 35.
  • The US Army had already called irradiated food wholesome, and the IAEA backed the Army.
  • It cited Glenn T. Seaborg, Nobel chemist and AEC chairman, who had told PSAC that massive animal feeding experiments showed no harm. Members of Congress's Joint Committee on Atomic Energy also challenged PSAC.
  • The IAEA said the PSAC report was “published without consulting the American authorities in the field of food irradiation”.
Hamblin's view

Those “authorities” were the AEC, the JCAE, the IAEA and the US Army, “all of which had interests in promoting rather than questioning the applications of atomic energy” p. 35.

Goddard: the burden of proof

There was “more support in the USA for Silow's claims than the IAEA wished to admit” p. 35. In 1967 the US Commissioner of Food and Drugs, James L. Goddard, urged caution at a meeting at Oak Ridge National Laboratory. “It can be very discouraging for industry executives and the consumer as well … to be led in any way into thinking that this or that process for this or that commodity is in effect today, when this may not be the case.” US law and practice set the world standard: “Our laws are taken to be model laws. Our business practices are imitated … our experience with irradiated food processes will be not only carefully watched – as they already are – they will be carefully copied as well.” “The burden of proof is heavy – but it does weigh upon the innovator.” Proof had to come from objective food policy, not “prejudiced advocacy” p. 35.

“The servant of the Member States”

Goddard's arguments “were music to Silow's ears” but did not appeal to the IAEA. It spent some 25 pages refuting Silow point by point, arguing that past research justified “optimism rather than pessimism” p. 36. It cited US research and reported commercial success in the USSR, where the Ministry of Health had approved irradiated food, calling it “one of the most advanced countries in the industrial application of food irradiation”. The evidence was a single paper by V. Rogachev at the 1966 Karlsruhe Food Irradiation Symposium. It denied any need for agricultural policy:

The decision as to whether technical assistance shall be suspended does not lie with Dr Silow. He should know better than those he criticizes that technical assistance is requested by Governments and that the IAEA is the servant of the Member States and not in a position to dictate what assistance is best for them.

IAEA comments on Silow's paper, August 1967 · p. 36

If India, Pakistan and Argentina were building plants with their own money, why should the Agency correct them and deny assistance? This denied the need for the supervisory policy Silow wanted p. 36.

Suppression

Silow's paper “never saw the light of day and was suppressed within the UN specialized agencies”. Eklund wrote to Sen: “It is clear that statements in the papers will not stand critical examination and I do not consider it appropriate to give any of the documents broader distribution, for instance as a document for our General Conference” p. 36.

Hamblin's view

Sidelining Silow over a document “that simply repeated US scientists' arguments marked a decisive step to quash dissent. Because it had come from the much-derided Silow, he got away with it without reproof” p. 36.

Iskenderun stalls (August 1967)

Later that month (August 1967) Nucleonics Week reported that several senior executives had resigned from the British company building the Turkish grain irradiator. fn 69 The firm was Nuclear Chemical Plant Ltd, whose parent was John Thomson Ltd. Its biggest contract had stalled because of “considerable public apprehension about the grain irradiator, reportedly stirred up by anti-Western elements”, and Ankara planned a publicity counter-campaign p. 36. To Silow this showed the Turkish government was being misled: “Why would it strain its already thin resources to promote a project that could not possibly improve economic conditions, even in the face of domestic resistance?” The IAEA, he believed, was complicit, shielding Turkey from FAO assessments that the plant was “fundamentally ill-advised”. To the IAEA the Turks had become “hysterical”, imagining the plant would sterilize the whole population. The official history says the project died because of “public fears and lack of understanding about the purposes of radiation” p. 36.

Out of Vienna (1968)

  • “Fortunately for his enemies”, the mandatory retirement age forced Silow out of the IAEA in March 1968. His deputy post already belonged to an IAEA insider, “the decidedly pro-atomic” Björn Sigurbjörnsson, an Icelander and “a devotee of Maurice Fried” p. 36.
  • By prior arrangement he returned to the FAO, where the retirement age was slightly higher, but with a demotion and a new assignment, which he read as another attempt to silence him.
  • Sen was gone, replaced in early 1968 by Addeke Hendrik Boerma of the Netherlands, whose outlook “complemented the technophile leanings of atomic energy advocates” pp. 36–37. Fried had cultivated FAO allies, including the assistant director-general for agriculture, Otto E. Fischnich.
  • Boerma and Fischnich saw Silow as “personally invested in destroying the joint division's program”. Boerma regretted that Silow took up everyone's time “with petty questions of rank” p. 37.
Hamblin's view

“Skepticism toward atomic energy in agriculture, for better or for worse, was associated with him personally, and his pariah status hardly invited others to join him” p. 37.

Atomic energy and the Green Revolution

Borlaug's wheat · Swaminathan's amber mutant · the protein “miracle” · Silow's last campaign · the Aresvik affair · retirement, tribunals and the BBC

Claim
The Green Revolution gave the IAEA its success story through an irradiated wheat that changed colour, not protein. Silow's escalating attacks ended in his isolation, and a second critic was quietly neutralised.
Evidence
Science and Indian sources; FAO correspondence of 1969–70; Fried's condemnation; Aresvik's draft and Fried's comments; ILO Tribunal judgments.
Against
The atomic chapter of the Green Revolution story; Sigurbjörnsson's defence of irradiation breeding (fn 78).

Borlaug's revolution

The Green Revolution “ultimately gave the IAEA the public relations boost” it needed p. 37. The name covers stunning increases in cereal output from the postwar work of breeders like the American Norman Borlaug and their patrons, the Ford and Rockefeller Foundations. Praised for his “Yankee ingenuity”, Borlaug made Mexico self-sufficient in wheat. His high-yielding varieties thrived in diverse conditions, responded to fertilizer and were short enough not to topple. Others grew the Mexican wheat, sparking the Green Revolution, in Science's words, “throughout the broad latitudinal range from Turkey to Paraguay”. fn 75 In the late 1960s West Pakistan became a wheat exporter, and India's annual yields rose from 12 million to 21 million tons, outpacing its population. “Giddy predictions” followed of ending hunger through high technology. fn 76 Sceptics such as Paddock pointed to the need for subsidies, expensive irrigation and fertilizer, and exposure to species-specific diseases.

Sharbati Sonora

The IAEA stressed irradiation's role in inducing mutations for “miracle seeds”. Yet Borlaug had succeeded through years of trial-and-error breeding, which “should have supported Silow's position that atomic energy techniques were not necessary” p. 37.

  • Indian buyers disliked the small amounts of Mexican wheat, Sonora 64, that reached market because of its colour. They “found the reddish hue unpalatable”.
  • Indian scientists led by the geneticist Monkombu S. Swaminathan irradiated the wheat and found a mutant with a more appealing amber colour.
  • Renamed “Sharbati Sonora”, it was planted on a large scale by the Indian Agricultural Research Institute (led by Swaminathan), with intensive irrigation and fertilizer.
  • The yields convinced other farmers, made Swaminathan famous, and gave the IAEA “the example of success its leaders craved”. They also gave “the appearance of Indian ingenuity”. Indians claimed the new wheat had more protein than the Mexican one.
  • India's State Minister for Food, Anna Saheb Shinde: “The farmer has gained faith in science. This is the corner turned.” p. 37

fn 77 Don Paarlberg: “the Rockefeller people had the grace and wit to buoy up the Mexicans and the Indians rather than claim credit for themselves.” After Nehru's death in 1964, Lal Bahadur Shastri's brief government wanted a more aggressive food policy through crop intensification. His food and agriculture minister, C. Subramaniam, wrote to Sen on 18 October 1966 that India was “deeply interested in exploiting atomic energy to the fullest possible extent for the betterment of human welfare”. He asked for help with “scientific cropping”, since India was “in a stage of transition from a traditional to a progressive agriculture”, and put a major expansion of atomic research into the Fourth Five Year Plan. After Swaminathan's success, mutation studies in India gained their own momentum. With famine threats and war with Pakistan in 1965, food security through genetics became part of national security (Perkins).

Hamblin's view

“Although radiation had changed the color of the wheat from red to amber, the protein ‘miracle’ had nothing to do with atomic energy” p. 37.

Silow on protein

Silow said so when the Joint Division used the Green Revolution to justify spending on high-protein grain p. 37. fn 78 His successor Sigurbjörnsson defended the program. Irradiation in India had changed Sonora-64's seed colour in three and a half years: “It is very difficult to match this by using any other technique of breeding … I see no reason why developing countries should be deprived of a proven method of efficient plant improvement.”

  • Silow doubted Swaminathan had found a higher-protein mutant. Countries wanting more protein should breed for it as Borlaug had, not hope radiation would “spontaneously produce a miraculous mutant” pp. 37–38.
  • Mutation breeding should be left to countries “best able to afford it”, and useful seeds then transferred, as with the Mexican wheat.
  • When Indian scientists toned down the protein claims, Silow “became convinced that Swaminathan was a fraud and that the IAEA was covering it up” p. 38.

The last campaign (1969)

“Emboldened by the revelation that the Indians had exaggerated radiation's contribution to protein increase”, Silow fought “more vigorously than ever, piling on example after example about international agencies supporting research fruitlessly” p. 38:

  • irradiated potatoes in Chile and strawberries in Vietnam;
  • the division “propping up institutes in places like Iran”, where “previously enthusiastic backers such as the Central Treaty Organization (CENTO) had become disillusioned, withdrawing their support”, yet the UN still encouraged them;
  • projects that developing countries would hardly have requested “spontaneously, without prompting or encouragement from the IAEA”.

After a heated meeting in which Fischnich's assistant said Silow thought he “knew everything about everything”, he sent a vitriolic memo suggesting they were “just coming to heel with the IAEA”, content “to throw insults at Silow while believing blindly in the rhetoric of atomic energy enthusiasts”:

The world history of food irradiation is one long story of excessive claims of its economic value and repeated financial failure and bankruptcy, without a single commercial success, coupled now with lack of confidence on the part of the public health authorities in the data, purporting to demonstrate the safety of irradiated foods, that have been given to them by the promoters of food irradiation.

Silow to T. E. Ritchie, 30 April 1969 · p. 38

Then came “a bizarre spat”. When his secretary fell ill, Fischnich would not replace her full-time and told Silow to use his own secretary or his assistant's, which meant submitting everything to his adversaries. So Silow wrote his memos by hand, which undermined his authority, added desperation, made them harder to read, and “made him an object of ridicule” p. 38.

He narrowed his attack to a few individuals and “professional and administrative irregularities”. He accused one unnamed man of falsifying his degree and lying about a university affiliation, and Fried and Sigurbjörnsson of covering it up. They had promoted Swaminathan's protein claim without checking it, though other geneticists showed it was “grossly exaggerated”. They hid proposals from him until too late, and their promotion had caused “a crisis in confidence from the developing world – first in the staff, then in FAO, and ultimately the whole UN system” p. 38.

“A stop has got to be made”

Fried wrote a long condemnation, with a handwritten note to Fischnich: “This is much stronger than the form of my usual answer but it is all true and somehow a stop has got to be made” pp. 38–39.

  • Fried said the FAO had never explicitly claimed Swaminathan raised the protein content. He had tape-recorded the meeting in question: the subject came up once, with the claims qualified as unsubstantiated. So Silow “stands exposed as having deliberately lied to you” p. 39.
  • He called Silow's work a “crusade against the joint division” and Silow a “self-appointed policeman”. He resented answering “fabricated and false, malicious and slanderous accusations”, and wanted “some disciplinary action”: “I would now kindly request that a stop be put to this nonsense.”
  • One official suggested suspending Silow with pay for a few months, “to keep him out of the building and worrying delegates”. FAO leaders chose to wait for him to retire.

The Aresvik affair (1970)

Meanwhile the FAO hid anything that might arm Silow p. 39. Oddvar Aresvik, a former professor of agricultural economics at the Norwegian University of Agriculture who had worked for the Ford Foundation in Pakistan and Lebanon, sent a draft book to Norway's FAO national committee, and it reached Boerma. It attacked grain irradiation, and “the fundamental critique was identical to Silow's”:

  • UN encouragement led cost-cutting politicians in developing countries to dubious priorities; the best scientists “followed the money, leaving the huge store of existing genetic material understudied”.
  • National pride encouraged politicians “to focus on such cutting-edge research”: “they have got the impression that to be considered developed they have to have atomic reactors and institutes for irradiation genetics”.
  • Irradiation was promising and might “one day” lead “to fantastic results”, but the burden should not fall on the developing world. “I have no objection to rich countries … which are experimenting with moon rockets and similar projects, sacrificing large amounts on radiation genetics.” The UN should not encourage politicians to choose “such large-scale, expensive ‘white elephants’” p. 39.

The FAO dreaded such criticism from someone other than its “exasperating” employee, in case Norwegian doubts spread. Instead of answering Aresvik's main point, Fried “picked nits”: whether the division funded new institutes or just new buildings, and whether irradiation researchers really got more money and pay. He rejected leaving the research to rich countries as “spoon-feeding of crop varieties”; local people should do it themselves with the best brains available p. 39.

Someone, “accidentally or surreptitiously”, delivered a copy of Fried's comments on Aresvik's manuscript to Silow. He saw it on his desk and read it; while he was out, Fischnich's secretary came and took the document back without his permission, arguing with Silow's secretary. Outraged, Silow demanded that Fischnich send him a copy, “bemoaning the ‘increasing trend toward secrecy in scientific matters’ at FAO in recent years”. Secrecy, he said, was “one of the surest indications that something was wrong with the program” p. 40. Fischnich refused him a copy. Aresvik “seems to have been cowed”: his later books were not especially critical of atomic energy.

Retirement, tribunals and the BBC (1970–73)

  • Silow fought retirement at the mandatory age of 62 fn 90. He argued it was wrong while his case was before the ILO Tribunal; the point “fell on deaf ears”.
  • “As a last-ditch effort to derail atomic energy in agriculture”, he wrote a 38-page memorandum, handwritten with crossed-out paragraphs. He asked Boerma to withdraw FAO support from a projected 20-nation project of the FAO, the IAEA and the European Nuclear Energy Agency. It read as if “composed by a megalomaniac”: “I ask you that you please do not fail to provide me at my residence by 22 December 1970 evidence acceptable to me that you have complied effectively and successfully …”. The only answer was a two-sentence reply from Fischnich saying the project was worthwhile p. 40.
  • In 1971 “FAO closed the book on Ronald Silow”: Boerma decided a further memo needed no answer, since Silow was now a private citizen and could be ignored. His name later surfaced “in letters to the editors of newspapers”.
  • His legal action against the FAO had “come to naught in late 1969”. The ILO Administrative Tribunal held that his superiors could remove him because he had openly criticized them and circulated his criticisms fn 93: Silow v. IAEA, Judgment No. 142, 3 November 1969. “The remaining complaints were dismissed, and his appeal went nowhere.”
  • In 1972 he was barred from an FAO meeting, and his complaint was dismissed fn 94: In re Silow (No. 5), Judgment No. 205, 14 May 1973.
  • In 1973 he told a BBC correspondent of a “fraudulent conspiracy” at the FAO around the claim that irradiation raised wheat protein. “After that, his voice faded into insignificance” p. 40.

Claim
An agency whose purpose was to promote a technology was bound to clash with agencies devoted to development. The IAEA won by personalising and suppressing dissent, and the Joint Division was “never more than an IAEA instrument”.
Evidence
The Joint Division's later record; Fischer's official history; Sigurbjörnsson's memoir.
Against
The official accounts that erase the conflict and its protagonist.

A promoter among developers

Hamblin's view

The IAEA's “laudable aim” of turning a terrible weapon into a force for good masked a consequence. Unlike the other UN agencies for food, agriculture, science, culture, education and health, “the IAEA's raison d'être would be to promote a particular set of technologies”, which put it “into inherent conflict” with any agency that did not rank atomic energy high. “Although nominally committed to the developing world, the IAEA's goal was not to foster development, but rather to promote peaceful uses of atomic energy” p. 40.

Its programs “in the name of ‘development’” rankled those committed to development that was “planned, rational, and detached from particular scientific agendas and technological choices” pp. 40–41. Silow lost, the FAO failed to back him, and he made it his “life's ambition” to bring down what he saw as the IAEA's “misguided and even corrupt agricultural empire”. His adversaries “kept him in the dark, outmaneuvered him, silenced him, and ignored him”. “What began as a legitimate conflict of philosophies between two agencies ended up seeming an irrelevant point raised incessantly by a desperate saboteur inside the UN system” p. 41.

The Joint Division after Silow

  • With Silow gone, the division “freely stated its desire to promote the use of atomic energy in the developing world”, expecting it to rely more and more on nuclear techniques p. 41.
  • Thanks to Swaminathan, plant breeding was its cause célèbre in the late 1960s and early 1970s. The most persistently controversial project has been food irradiation. Sigurbjörnsson: “It has been said that without the Joint Division, this technology would not be pursued at all!”
  • In the 1970s critics said biotechnology would make mutation breeding obsolete. In the 1980s the division turned to nuclear techniques, mainly tagging, in biotechnology. It is proudest of its 1990s work on the sterile male technique, and resented the FAO's resistance.
  • Sigurbjörnsson: “cultivating good relations with FAO took a lot of time and a lot of effort”. “Such resentments, directed from Vienna to Rome, reinforce the reality that the ‘Joint Division’ was never more than an IAEA instrument”. In its early years its main struggle was “to shut up the ‘exasperating’ FAO member, Ronald Silow” p. 41.

fn 97 Garrett Hardin wrote that “no one desires irradiated food”, and that the industry should bear the burden of proof, with the practice forbidden meanwhile. In the 1990s James H. Steele complained that “these antis prey on the fears of nuclear destruction which have hung over the world since 1945 …”. The issue revived in the late 1990s over E. coli (Lutter; the charge of “scaremongering” in Grierson). When “organic farming” was defined in the 1990s, many wanted to exclude irradiation, along with genetic modification and sewage sludge.

Erased from the history books

“As for Silow himself, his name quite literally has been erased from the history books” p. 41.

  • Fischer's official history says only that “an unhappy and unwilling FAO official was transferred to the staff of the IAEA”. It does not describe the controversy, and concludes that “[a]lthough it took several years before the effects of previous disputes wore off … the practical and common sense concept reflected in the Joint Division proved to be a great success”.
  • Sigurbjörnsson's Personal Reflections tells the story in detail but omits Silow's name. He describes the IAEA fighting for its program's survival in the late 1960s because of an unnamed person, and how staff prepared before conferences where this man would attack them:

We all stayed in the same hotel. Mac Fried held strategy meetings and we, in effect, divided the delegations between us and made contacts … . This was certainly not very ethical but in the face of hundreds of memo pages which had been distributed to delegates by our adversary, our choice was either to fight or give up – which would have certainly meant the end of the Joint Division.

Sigurbjörnsson, quoted p. 41

Sigurbjörnsson suggests the division's coherence and unity came partly from constantly defending itself against this unnamed “adversary” p. 41.

The question Silow raised

The UN's promotion of atomic agriculture “has not yet received serious attention from scholars” p. 41. The article “attempts no lionization of Ronald Silow, nor does it offer a scientific critique” of the techniques. It revisits the question his enemies ignored: “was the UN shaping developing countries toward a particular vision of modernity that subsumed their interests to a single agency's goals and/or individual scientists' research agendas?” p. 42. It is natural that the IAEA saw a bright future for atomic energy; “that other UN agencies should fall into step is perhaps more surprising”.

Hamblin's verdict

The essay shows “how the supposed FAO/IAEA consensus was created … by revealing how the controversy was brushed aside by identifying it with one troublesome individual”. “Silow handled the issue ineptly, by lashing out and creating enemies. Sigvard Eklund, Maurice Fried and others handled it more successfully but perhaps wrongly, by alienating and isolating Silow rather than engaging him, and by suppressing his objections so that he could not influence others”. This made a bitter foe rather than addressing the developing world, and “even IAEA scientists later admitted that it was ethically questionable” p. 42.

The IAEA avoided Silow also because his argument for UN agricultural policy oversight, once the FAO's own, “might have shut down most of the IAEA's activities in agriculture”. “Development was only a secondary objective at the IAEA. Implementing legitimate peaceful applications of atomic energy always was the first priority.” These activities were much of what the IAEA could offer poorer countries, since power reactors “seemed entirely unrealistic”. “To abandon food and agriculture would have been to undermine a crucial component of ‘Atoms for Peace’ that specifically targeted the developing world.” “Because of its inherent commitment to atomic solutions, any real engagement with the issue of misleading developing countries may have been impossible” p. 42.

Atomic Kids

Duck and Cover and Atomic Alert Teach American Children How to Survive Atomic Attack · Bo Jacobs, Hiroshima Peace Institute

The article in one breath

Cold War children feared something their parents did not: “the loss of a future they could grow into and inhabit” p. 25. Jacobs reads the two 1951 school films, the FCDA's Duck and Cover (starring Bert the Turtle) and Encyclopedia Britannica's Atomic Alert, as texts “coded with both intended and unintended subtexts”. The intended message was that survival was a choice, and that government and teachers were in control and would quickly restore order. The unintended message was the opposite. The constant vigilance the films demanded said nuclear war was imminent, that adults would not be there (“Then, you're on your own”), and that the government was unreliable. Dog tags to identify children's bodies, “Atomic Doom” bubble-gum cards and science fiction drove the lesson home. Early-1960s surveys found children who expected war and feared a future not worth living. So children concluded they would have to save the world themselves. The teen heroes of The Blob (1958) outwit adults who will not listen. The Baby Boomers raised on these films became the New Left, whose Port Huron Statement (1962) spoke of “the last generation in the experiment with living”. In Jacobs's words, “Duck and Cover had come true—they were on their own” p. 40.

Introduction

Claim
Cold War children learned to fear the loss of a future. School materials meant to teach survival and trust in authority taught the opposite, so children decided they would have to save the world themselves.
Evidence
Collier's on Indian Springs School (1952); an AEC spokesman; classroom materials; Englehardt's “victory culture”.

Kids of the Atomic Age

“The experiences of American kids in the Cold War were very different from those of their parents.” Adults saw a threat to the American way of life and to their families' health. Their children “learned to fear the loss of a future they could grow into and inhabit”. “These kids of the Atomic Age wondered if they might be the last children on Earth” p. 25. fn 1 Much scholarship examines FCDA efforts aimed at the family and at children in school, “but almost none on efforts aimed at children as individuals” (Henriksen, McEnaney, Oakes, Weart, Rose, JoAnne Brown).

“The prospect of a war fought with nuclear weapons pervaded American culture even when the United States was the sole possessor of such weapons.” The fear grew in the late 1940s as the Soviet Union acquired its own and the Cold War “began in earnest”. “Massive government efforts to design, construct, and deploy nuclear weapons” helped fuel what Dwight Eisenhower later called the military-industrial complex fn 2. The historian Michael Scheibach notes that “educators, government officials, and parents realized the necessity, even the urgency, of preparing the country's youth for a new, more precarious world” p. 25.

Indian Springs School, Nevada

The pupils of the Indian Springs School met the Atomic Age first-hand. Collier's took readers into the two-room school in “a converted supply room at the Indian Springs Air Force Base, a security area attached to the closely guarded atomic testing grounds. The children have witnessed four atomic blasts in the last few weeks. Some of the children have seen as many as a dozen of the atomic test detonations” p. 25 fn 4. An Atomic Energy Commission spokesman held them up as models:

The children in this school by their sheer proximity to the tests are getting the same type of psychological indoctrination we are giving some of our combat troops. If all the school children in the nation could witness an A-bomb blast, it would do much to destroy the fear and uncertainty which now exist.

AEC spokesman in Collier's, 1952 · p. 25

Coded messages

Most children got their lessons “in less direct form”, through pamphlets, films and other materials made by government agencies for classroom use. These taught survival steps, “but it was coded with both intended and unintended subtexts” p. 26.

  • Intended message 1: “you could survive an atomic attack if you learned the preparatory steps and took the correct actions. Survival, therefore, was a choice.”
  • Intended message 2: during an attack “the social leaders, the government and the teachers, were in control of the situation”. They could prescribe simple steps and “quickly restore order”. Do your part, and you could trust authorities to maintain order and society itself.
Jacobs's view

“Paradoxically, implicit in these texts was the exact opposite message.” The hyper-vigilance they demanded said nuclear war “was not only inevitable—it was imminent”, and portrayed “an adult world that was spinning out of control”. Children could not rely on adults to prevent the war, “or even to be present to protect and guide them through the experience” pp. 26–27. By trying to enlist children as vigilant Cold Warriors, “these texts, in actuality, conveyed the message that their own Cold War government was unreliable. The children concluded that, ultimately, if their world was to be saved, they would have to act to save it” p. 27.

Victory culture

These children “were already primed not to see themselves as passive victims” p. 27. School taught them that Americans were the good guys: against a corrupt monarchy in the Revolution, to free the slaves in the Civil War, against fascism in World War II. Their ancestors “had made the future a better place”. Tom Englehardt calls this triumphalist heritage victory culture. The Good War and the Cold War did not fit together:

“The question of whether or not to use triumphal weapons of a suicidal nature to accomplish national ends proved deeply unsettling not just for adults planning global strategy, but also for children experiencing both the pride of parents returning victorious from the world war and the fear that that war's most wondrous weapon engendered.” The tension had “unforeseen and profound effects” on the Baby Boomers p. 27:

If the story of victory in World War II was for a time endlessly replayed in the movies, in comics, and on television, other cultural vistas were also opening up for the young, ones that led directly into whatever terrified grownups. To escape not into the war story, but into places where that story was dissolving, held unexpected pleasures, not the least of which was the visible horror of adults at what you were doing…Many children instinctively grasped the corrosiveness of the postwar transformation, gravitating toward new forms of storytelling that seemed to rise unbidden from alien worlds.

Englehardt 1995, pp. 7–8 · quoted p. 27

For Englehardt, the children of the 1950s “grasped the pleasures of victory culture as an act of faith, and the horrors of nuclear culture as an act of faithless mockery” of World War II's virtues p. 27.

Alone in the Flash

From Hersey to the first pamphlets · making Duck and Cover · Bert the Turtle · the narration · Paul, Patty and Tony · “Then, you're on your own”

Claim
Duck and Cover taught children to survive a nuclear attack alone, “without adult assistance”. It turned every childhood setting into ground zero and told “a tale of a dangerous present and a dismal future”.
Evidence
The film's cartoon, narration and scenes; FCDA distribution; the wider flood of atomic publicity from 1946 to 1950.
Against
The film's later reputation as camp, via The Atomic Café.

Before the film (1945–50)

Public discussion of nuclear danger began the day Hiroshima was bombed, but the Soviet bomb of late 1949 made things seem more dire p. 27.

  • The New Yorker gave its entire August 1946 issue to John Hersey's “Hiroshima”, “with its unflinching reports of horrifically injured survivors”.
  • The Beginning or the End (1947), a docudrama about the Manhattan Project, asked whether mankind would survive its invention.
  • Five years after Hiroshima and a year after the first Soviet test, Collier's ran a cover painting of a mushroom cloud over Manhattan for the article “Hiroshima USA” fn 8.
  • “Hysteria” over Soviet weapons justified US stockpiling and more tests in Nevada and the Pacific pp. 27–28.
  • In 1950 the government began mass distribution of civil defense pamphlets. Survival Under Atomic Attack, the first, went to over 20 million households. “Survival guides filled mailboxes, newspapers, and bookshelves”, and anxiety over a Soviet attack “fueled atomic fears across the United States” p. 28.

Making Duck and Cover

Among the most influential documents of the 1950s was one “aimed at children and produced by the U.S. government”: the film Duck and Cover and its pamphlet, distributed by the Federal Civil Defense Administration (FCDA) p. 28. fn 10 It was directed by Anthony Rizzo and produced by Leo M. Langlois for Archer Films, 1951.

Jacobs's view

“In recent years, Duck and Cover has become a classic of comedy and camp, thanks largely to the inclusion of clips from it in the 1982 documentary The Atomic Café. But in its original release, the film was deadly serious” p. 28.

  • It was part of the “massive, sometimes hysterical” response to the first Soviet test in late 1949 fn 12: 29 August 1949 at Semipalatinsk, Kazakhstan, the test known as “Joe 1”.
  • Its purpose was “to teach children how to survive a nuclear attack by themselves, without adult assistance” p. 28.
  • Archer Films made it under contract to the FCDA. It was shown in thousands of schools, and the companion pamphlet went to millions.
  • It had a cartoon mascot, Bert the Turtle, and “a catchy song written by the same team whose jingle had advised Americans to ‘See the USA in a Chevrolet’” fn 13: Leo Carr and Leon Corday, with Leo Langlois.

Bert and the monkey

The film opens with “a cartoon segment featuring Bert the Turtle”, the part “most frequently reproduced and ridiculed”. Bert walks along, “accompanied by his theme song”, past a monkey hanging out of a tree, “holding a twig from which hangs a stick of dynamite that dangles menacingly close to Bert”. “Ever alert”, Bert drops and withdraws into his shell p. 28. fn 14 The explosion “would seem to annihilate the monkey”.

Even here “the iconography is a bit skewed”. Bert, with whom children are meant to identify, is drawn almost as an old man, with a bow tie, a wrinkled neck and a civil-defense-style helmet, while the monkey is “a classically mischievous kid”. Even Bert's voice sounds old. “Clearly, for Atomic Kids, Duck and Cover truly was grown-up business” p. 28.

The narration

“The narrator instructs the viewer that Bert is always prepared for danger and that is why he has his shell”: “Sometimes it even saves his life!” Children must learn to duck and cover as Bert does. “Immediately, the narrator turns his attention to the bomb”: “We all know the atomic bomb is very dangerous. Since it may be used against us we must be ready for it” p. 29. “In order to make this threat normative”, the narrator describes familiar dangers like fires and automobile accidents, each with “methods for avoiding or responding”. His introduction to the bomb is “truly grim”:

Now, we must be ready for a new danger, the atomic bomb...You will know when it comes. We hope it never comes, but we must be ready. It looks something like this: there is a bright flash! Brighter than the Sun! Brighter than anything you have ever seen! If you are not ready and did not know what to do, it could hurt you in different ways. It could knock you down hard, or throw you against a tree or wall. It is such a big explosion it can smash in buildings and knock signboards over and break windows all over town. But, if you duck and cover like Bert, you will be much safer. You know how bad sunburn can feel. An atomic bomb flash can burn you worse than a terrible sunburn.

Duck and Cover narration · p. 29

Next comes “the basic rationale for their training”: there are two kinds of attack, with warning and without. With warning, “there will be time for us to get in our homes, schools or some other safe place”, and adults may help. Most attacks will be signalled by national defense and warning systems p. 29. The narrator lists where a child might hear the sirens, places that until then “had certainly seemed to be the safe, idyllic settings of childhood”:

You may be in your schoolyard, playing, when the signal comes. That signal means to stop whatever you are doing and get to the nearest safe place fast. Always remember, the flash of an atomic bomb can come at anytime, no matter where you might be. You might be out playing at home when the warning comes. Then be sure to get into the house fast, where your parents have fixed a safe place for you to go.

Duck and Cover narration · p. 30

Then the reassurance goes: “sometimes, and this is very, very important, sometimes the bomb might explode without any warning. Then, the first thing we would know about it would be the flash, and that means duck and cover fast! Wherever you are! There's no time to look around and wait!” p. 30

The drill

A hallway demonstration shows ducking and covering: shelter against any nearby structure, fall to the ground by a curb or crouch by a wall, cover head and neck. The narrator says children will be safe crouching against a wall, “even though just a minute earlier he had told them that the atomic bomb can ‘smash in buildings’”. They cover faces and necks so that “if the glass breaks and flies through the air, it won't cut you”, while the camera shows their backs exposed, “hardly inspiring a deep sense of safety from flying glass” p. 30.

Paul, Patty and Tony

  • Paul and Patty leave their handsomely furnished suburban home. Their mother takes their hands and kisses them goodbye; they look cheerful as they head out, and she calls “Good-bye!” after them. “They are now on their own on the front lines of the Cold War, ready to face the Russian nuclear weapons.” The narrator: “Getting ready means we must all be able to take care of ourselves … the bomb might explode when there are no grown-ups near. Paul and Patty know this, and they are always ready to take care of themselves.” On the way to school “on a beautiful spring day”, a flash: “It's a bomb! Duck and cover!” p. 30
  • Tony, cycling to a Cub Scout meeting, dives from his bike into the gutter: “Tony knows the bomb can explode any time of year, day or night … That-a-boy, Tony! That flash means act fast! … Notice how he keeps from moving or getting up or running? He stays down until he is sure the danger is over.”
  • In a corridor, a cafeteria, a school bus, at a picnic, at home or in the park, “bright flash after bright flash turn all the normal settings of the daily lives of children into the ground zero of an atomic attack” pp. 30–31.

“Then, you're on your own”

While children were “supposedly being trained to physically survive an atomic attack”, the film also carried “a subtle message about the relationship of children and their world to the world of their parents” p. 31. The narrator “somberly reminds them”: “Older people will help us like they always do. But there might not be any grown-ups around when the bomb explodes … Then, you're on your own.” The film was designed to teach children “that they could survive a surprise nuclear war even in the absence of adult caretakers, conveying a powerfully mixed assurance”. The threat was always imminent and survival depended on constant readiness: “No matter where we live, in the city or the country, we must be ready all the time for the atomic bomb…Yes, we must all get ready now so we know how to save ourselves if the atomic bomb ever explodes near us.” But “the safe world of their childhood, could dissolve at any moment. And when that debacle happens, the adults will be gone; the youngsters will be on their own”.

Jacobs's view

This was the story of nuclear war, the Cold War and childhood that millions of Baby Boomers got from their government and teachers: “a tale of a dangerous present and a dismal future. Ducking and covering is after all, a catastrophic pose, one in which the emphasis is on avoiding head injury at the expense of bodily injury: it is the desperate posture of an attempt at bare survival. To duck and cover is to fall to the ground and hope that you live to stand back up” p. 31. With no grown-ups in sight, it is up to the children “to survive the world that their parents have made for them—a world seemingly without a future, where survival is measured day to day, minute to minute” pp. 31–32.

Soldiers in the Cold War

Dog tags · mobilised around their own deaths · Atomic Alert's team · Ted and Susie · restored order vs science fiction

Claim
Civil defense mobilised children as soldiers, but around their own deaths, not a distant war effort. Atomic Alert added teamwork and a quick restoration of order, which children raised on science fiction did not believe.
Evidence
School dog-tag programs; Furtwangler's memory; Atomic Alert's narration and scenes; science fiction from 1948 to 1962.
Against
The civil defense promise of “the rapid restoration of social order”.

Dog tags

“If parents couldn't be present to help their children to survive, at least they could organize to identify their bodies” p. 32.

  • From 1951 many cities issued dog tags to students: over 2.5 million in public and private schools in San Francisco, Seattle, Philadelphia and New York City.
  • FCDA education experts chose dog tags after considering tattooing, marking clothes and fingerprinting.
  • The heat- and corrosion-resistant tags would reunite children with families after the war, and could also identify bodies fn 17. JoAnne Brown shows civil defense in school curricula served professional as well as civic purposes.
  • In 1981 Albert Furtwangler, a professor of English, recalled Seattle in the early 1950s: “In fifth and sixth grades, I also dressed every morning with a bit of cold metal against my chest—a dog tag with my name and address, furnished at cost by the Parent-Teacher Association.”

The military-style tags told children that “they were soldiers in the Cold War” p. 32.

A different mobilization

Mobilizing children in wartime was not new. On the World War II home front they bought war bonds with their nickels and dimes instead of candy, planted victory gardens in 4-H clubs, learned German and Japanese aircraft silhouettes, and collected scrap metal and kitchen fats, sometimes in ranks of “lieutenants”, “sergeants” and “privates” p. 32. Cold War mobilization “focused around the idea of the children's own deaths rather than on their aiding an effort that was far from their homes”. The front lines “ran down their own suburban and city streets”. “They were being prepared for an attack, a much more intimate experience than gathering materials to contribute to attacks on the other side of the world.”

Atomic Alert (1951)

In 1951 Encyclopedia Britannica Films released Atomic Alert, “a film similar to Duck and Cover”, for elementary schools fn 20. Both stressed self-reliance, “but the messages they delivered were subtly different”. It was made with the Institute for Nuclear Studies at the University of Chicago. Being shown in classrooms gave both films “a chilling authoritativeness” p. 32.

Ted and Susie

  • Home alone when a warning comes, they learn that “survival is the primary job of young defense-team members”, and that it depends on preparation and practice. “If you're home, you've got work to do.” p. 33
  • Their checklist runs from closing curtains to shutting off the stove, “turning off the burners but unwisely leaving the gas line itself open”.
  • The radio says it is only a test. When Susie asks why they must check everything, Ted lectures her: “We need this practice. Now come on, let's do our jobs.” They shelter in their well-prepared basement and check the supplies they have stashed.
  • Without warning, “What is your job then?” The film shows children responding in “a number of outdoor situations”, much as in Duck and Cover. They take cover in school buildings at recess or run into apartment buildings. They are told to knock on the door of any house far from home, “and that strangers will admit them”. They jump into ditches in fields and throw off their (presumably contaminated) outer clothing.
  • On a battery radio in the basement they hear that a bomb has exploded between “Fourteenth Street and the waterfront”, then that another has burst underwater and radioactive mist is falling on the city. Ted tells Susie what to do if it falls on her.
  • The civil defense block warden arrives with a radiation monitor. His deadpan tone says all is under control. Their mother is at the shopping centre, their father at headquarters: the adult team is doing its job. “You've done a good job.” p. 33

“These little soldiers in the Cold War have done their part to help the team and to defend the nation” p. 33.

The restoration of order

Part of the civil defense story was the rapid restoration of order after an attack. US government films on Hiroshima and Nagasaki “would dwell endlessly on flattened and depopulated images”, but “civil defense texts dared not envision a devastated American landscape”. Recovery would be “natural and controlled”; in both films adult workers appear quickly once children have saved themselves p. 34.

What children read and watched

“But most youngsters knew that this scenario was not what they could really expect” p. 34. Science fiction, whose core audience was teenage boys, had been full of nuclear devastation since the late 1940s fn 22:

  • for hardcore fans: Judith Merrill's “That Only A Mother” (1948), about “a woman's fierce love for her radiation-disfigured child”, and Ward Moore's “Flying Dutchman” (1953), a robot bomber flying sorties over a dead Earth;
  • “more mainstream offerings” that “touched many more”: Ray Bradbury's The Martian Chronicles (1950), with atomic war on Earth as backdrop;
  • atomic-mutation films like Them! (1954), nuclear-crisis thrillers like Peter Bryant's Red Alert (1958), and post-holocaust stories like Dean Owen's The End of the World (1962) spread these images “far beyond what fans proudly called the ‘science-fiction ghetto’”;
  • by the end of the decade the obliteration of cities (Burdick and Wheeler's Fail-Safe, 1962) or of human life (Nevil Shute's On The Beach, 1957) was “an established part of mainstream popular culture”.
Jacobs's view

Civil defense officials, teachers “and even presidents” might call nuclear war winnable or followed by restored order. “Children heard the message loud and clear: popular culture convinced them that no such comforting illusions should be harbored and that they would be just as alone after the war as they were when the bombs went off.” The flash might bring “the end of the world” pp. 34–35.

No Future

“Atomic Doom” · “if you grow up” · adults remember · Schwebel's and Escalona's surveys · memory checked against data

Claim
Children's nuclear fear was real. It shows in the ephemera and films of the time, in adults' memories, and in early-1960s surveys where children expected war and feared a future not worth living.
Evidence
The Bowman card; Atomic City; Musil, Rhodes, Carey, Gitlin; Schwebel's and Escalona's studies.
Against
The suspicion that later memories exaggerate. He tests memory against data from the time.

Atomic Doom (1950)

In 1950 the Bowman Gum Company of Philadelphia, a leading baseball-card maker, issued its first Wild Man Picture Cards, sold beside baseball cards in candy stores and five-and-dimes. Card #34, “Atomic Doom”, asked: “What will a war be like—if it comes?” p. 35 It continued: “Science is constantly increasing the destructive power of the atomic bomb. In a future war, not one or two but many of these weapons could be let loose on target areas.” The result would be catastrophic: “Explosions might cause a chain reaction destroying the earth or rendering it so barren that it could not support human or animal life.” The front showed the earth exploding. “The cards even came with a stick of gum to chew on while contemplating the end of the world.”

“If you grow up”

In the 1952 film Atomic City, young Tommy plays with a friend on the living-room floor while his mother cooks in the kitchen. He asks the friend, “What do you want to be if you grow up?” His mother, “noticeably startled”, insists: “When you grow up, not if, when!” The scene frames a dilemma central to many Boomer childhoods: “would the world be there when they grew up?” p. 35

Adults remember

  • Robert Musil (Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, 1982) watched newspaper maps each year as “widening circles, representing atomic destruction on a map of New York crept outward from Manhattan. Only with the advent of deliverable Russian thermonuclear weapons in the mid-1950s did those circles finally reach my home. I began to worry” pp. 35–36. fn 24 Musil was an antinuclear activist and American Studies lecturer, answering Furtwangler.
  • Richard Rhodes, later author of the Pulitzer-winning The Making of the Atomic Bomb, wrote a haiku as a fifth-grader in the late 1940s:

Look up in the sky.
See the pretty mushroom cloud.
Soon we will be dead.

Rhodes · quoted p. 36
  • In the late 1970s the journalist Michael Carey and the psychiatrist Robert Jay Lifton collected oral histories of Boomers' childhood fears. Carey concluded in the same 1982 Bulletin issue that the bomb “had permanently marked this generation” p. 36.
  • A Philadelphia respondent, told the US was a melting pot, thought “‘It really will be a melting pot’ because of that business with the bomb.”
  • Carey grew up in Fairbanks, Alaska, near a Strategic Air Command base “busy with the comings and goings of bombers laden with nuclear weapons”: “My school had unsettling air raid drills, but I recall more vividly my terror of the citywide blackouts, when we had to cover our windows with blankets to ensure no enemy could find Fairbanks. I was curious about the bases by day but frightened of the bomb at night.”
  • Another had seen film of the Nevada Test Site's House One (“Test Shot Annie”, 17 March 1953) bursting into flames: “It undercuts the sense of reality. You kind of grow up knowing that certain things are stable…that certain presuppositions about the way things are remain constant. Namely, buildings stand … When you suddenly see a picture like that, it's kind of like getting the rug pulled out.”
  • A Californian heard a missile test as a child: “He dived behind the couch, yelling ‘Get down! It's happened!’ only to discover that he had made a fool of himself.”
  • The historian Todd Gitlin: “Every so often, out of the blue, a teacher would pause in the middle of class and call out, ‘Take cover!’ We knew, then, to scramble under our miniature desks and to stay there, cramped, heads folded under our arms, until the teacher called out, ‘All clear!’” p. 36

What children said at the time

“How much do we really know about the thoughts of children at the time, as opposed to their later memories? How seriously did children take the presence of nuclear weapons looming between themselves and their imagined futures?” In the early 1960s several researchers tried to find out pp. 36–37.

  • Milton Schwebel, chair of the Department of Guidance and Administration at New York University's School of Education, led interviews with thousands of public-school students, mostly in high and junior high schools in New York and Pennsylvania, right after the Berlin crisis of 1961 and the Cuban missile crisis of 1962. Three questions: “Do I think there is going to be a war?”; “Do I care? Why?”; “What do I think about fallout shelters?” p. 37
  • Schwebel reported “a broad range of responses”. Students were roughly split on whether war would come, but almost all cared very much: “I will die; my parents, brothers, sisters, friends will die; My family and I will be separated; If my family dies and I live, I'd rather be dead; I am young and want a future to live, marry, have a family, work, create, paint…Even if I survive, what will there be worth living for, with millions dead?” Some revealed “an intense personal anxiety about the future”: “I worry. I cry. Sometimes I am disturbed at night.”
  • Sibylle K. Escalona, a psychologist at the Albert Einstein College of Medicine, “offered corroborating data”. She asked 311 children aged 10 to 17 in the New York area in 1962: “Think about the world as it may be ten years from now. What are some ways it might be different from today?” Seventy percent raised war without being prompted, and most of these 219 were pessimistic. A twelve-year-old: “Maybe we will not even be here 10 years from now … Maybe there will be no such thing as a world.” Another: “I think I would be all broken up because of the war which will come. The war destroys New York.” A fourteen-year-old: “the threat of war and complete destruction will hang as a cloud of fear over the world.”
Jacobs's method note

Schwebel's interviews “put the notion of war into the heads of the students”, “allowing them to respond positively or negatively to this suggestion by an adult”. Escalona's open-ended questions “allowed the students to individually prioritize their own beliefs about the future” p. 37. Both the reminiscent and the contemporary data show “American children were deeply troubled and worried about the impact of nuclear weapons on their lives”. They feared death and losing family and friends, and that life after nuclear war “might not be worth living”.

Kids to the Rescue

Disdain for authority · “No future” · passive children in early science fiction · teen saviours · The Blob · the New Left and Port Huron

Claim
Taught that the adult world could not protect them, the Boomers concluded the future was theirs to save. Late-1950s science fiction showed teenagers saving a world whose adults would not listen, and the generation became the New Left.
Evidence
Musil's memory; 1950s and 1960s science-fiction films, above all The Blob; Biskind; the Port Huron Statement; Gitlin.
Against
The passive children of early-1950s films, and “the platitudes of Bert the Turtle”.

Growing up anyway

Taught to prepare for death “at every waking moment”, the Boomers “still managed to find ways to grow up believing in a future”, from ducking and covering on the way to Scout meetings to “reading about superheroes bitten by radioactive spiders” pp. 37–38. Where parents saw fear and threats, the children walked “an unstable landscape, still fearful, but also filled with heretofore unimaginable possibilities” p. 38.

“The flash could come at any moment!” warned Bert. Musil: “It was with that awful knowledge—we were not safe at all—that I experienced duck and cover drills, and developed an early disillusionment with, even disdain for, authority” p. 38.

Jacobs's view

“These little soldiers played in a million parks across America” and knew that any future world “would have to be very different from the world of their parents”. Two world wars made their parents' world a world of war. If that “militaristic and nationalistic way of life” continued, only one outcome was possible: World War III, “clearly a war of their parents' generation's making”, followed by “…nothing. No future.” Since their parents did not seem to be working for change, “it was up to the next generation, the children, to change the world” p. 38 (after Broderick, “Rebels with a Cause”).

Passive children in early science fiction

Science-fiction films, where threats came “from the realm of the fantastic rather than the political or ideological”, were “an early indicator of the emerging role of young people as the saviors of the future”. While the wider culture “whirred and boomed with space launches”, early films “stayed much closer to home and the present”, leading young audiences through “familiar territory, now beset by giants, monsters, prehistoric cave dwellers, mad scientists, menacing slime, and, of course, alien life forms”. They explored science, coming of age, and “the need to take action in the face of imminent danger” p. 38. But the films of the early and mid-1950s “were often child-free”, and the children in them were “largely passive, and peripheral to the action”. Crowds fleeing a rampaging monster “typically included a small child being dragged to safety by a parent or other authority figure”, and even central child characters “did little to shape the course of events”:

  • Bobby Benson watches but does not act in his mother's encounter with an alien in The Day the Earth Stood Still (1951);
  • the girl who first alerts authorities to the giant ants in Them! (1954) is “nearly speechless” with shock;
  • the children of Invaders from Mars (1953) and The Creature with the Atom Brain (1955) watch, powerless, as monsters invade;
  • the teen heroes of I Was a Teenage Frankenstein and I Was a Teenage Werewolf (both 1957) are “helpless experimental subjects at the mercy of mad adult scientists” p. 38.

Teen saviours

From the late 1950s a new wave “portrayed teenagers not just as active participants in action, but as saviors” p. 38. The film historian Peter Biskind described 1950s science fiction as a battle between “cops and docs”: brute force (police and military) against reason (scientists) as the way through the atomic age's dilemmas pp. 38–39. The new teen heroes added “a generational tension”. Their parents and other adults refuse to listen, so, “alerted to unnatural threats to human society, they take matters into their own hands”, save the Earth themselves, and show their parents' generation what must be done “to preserve a viable future for the human race” p. 39.

The Blob (1958)

  • The film “makes its generational allegiances clear from the outset”. A meteorite lands near “an archetypal American small town” and releases an amorphous, pulsating red blob that devours every living thing it touches, growing with each feeding p. 39.
  • Steve and Jane try to warn the town. The police sergeant accuses them and their hot-rodding friends of causing the trouble; another teen who calls the police is rebuffed. “Is every kid in town in on this?” brays the sergeant, adding: “You know, I think they got it in for me, they've heard about my war record and it bugs ‘em.”
  • Steve urges direct action: “All right, we tried to do it the right way, now we're gonna wake this town up ourselves.” The teens gather their hot rods in the town centre and lean on their horns, and one sets off the air-raid siren.
  • An old man leaps out of bed: “An air raid! Where is my civil defense helmet?” As the adults converge on the town centre, Steve, backed by his friends, pleads: “This town is in danger! We had to make this noise, we had to make it so you'd listen to us so we could warn you.” He enlists the nearest authority figure, the police chief: “Make ‘em listen to me. There is a monster! We saw it in Dad's store, only it's bigger now!”
  • The Blob appears, “proving to the adults that their children were right all along, but also inciting panic”. Trapped in a diner with Jane and her little brother, Steve takes “decisive action when the adults freeze”. He notices that the Blob retreats from the cold spray of a CO₂ fire extinguisher and relays his discovery, “the Blob hates cold”, to the policemen outside. The monster is vanquished, first by local adults “wielding more fire extinguishers”, then by “even bigger adults in Washington, DC”, who send forces to transport it to the Arctic, where it will stay frozen, “no longer a threat to society” p. 39. fn 37 The salvation is in doubt: the final “The End” turns into a question mark.

“Here we can see, as early as 1958, the receptivity among the teen audience to the message that the previous generation just didn't get the nature of the threat facing the human race.” In 1960s films like The Horror of Party Beach (1964), Village of the Giants (1965) and Eegah! (1962), teen heroes were aligning with, “and often surpassing”, adult authority figures pp. 39–40.

Jacobs's view

“Duck and Cover had come true—they were on their own” p. 40.

The New Left

The Boomers, raised on civil defense films, tales of annihilation and stories of teens saving the world, “came of age, politically, in the 1960s, becoming founders and leaders of the New Left” p. 40. fn 39 Gitlin: “to many in my generation, especially the incipient New Left, the grimmest and least acknowledged underside of affluence was the Bomb. … We grew up taking cover in school drills—the first American generation compelled from infancy to fear not only war but the end of days.”

  • Raised to believe the world stood on the brink, they were primed to see the need for action. Taught from childhood that they had “the power and responsibility to save themselves and those around them”, they were primed to act.
  • Students for a Democratic Society organized many campus protests against the Vietnam War.
  • The Port Huron Statement (1962), “a founding document of the New Left”, begins: “The enclosing fact of the Cold War, symbolized by the presence of the Bomb, brought awareness that we ourselves, and our friends, and millions of abstract ‘others’ . . . might die at any time.” It calls for action against racism and war: “our work is guided by the sense that we may be the last generation in the experiment with living” p. 40.
Jacobs's conclusion

A childhood of drills, warnings of flashes in the sky, and films like Duck and Cover and Atomic Alert “laid the foundation for the path traveled by these young activists and revolutionaries”. As children they were told to throw themselves to the ground, huddle into a gutter, and hope the world was still there when they got up. “As young adults, they refused to live in the basement of fear and fatalism toward nuclear war, a shelter that was prepared by the preceding generation” p. 40. Many believed that Cold War militarism had to change “if it was not to lead to nuclear warfare and the end of their future”. “They placed flowers in the gun barrels of American militarism and demanded that their government stand down in its war in Vietnam”, and, like the teens in The Blob, used “increasingly bold strategies in order to be heard and to draw attention to the threat of Cold War escalation and nuclear holocaust”.

“As a generation, the Baby Boomers did not solve the problems of militarism or the threat of nuclear war, but they did become adults, creating a different set of expectations for their world, beyond the platitudes of Bert the Turtle's message to duck and cover” pp. 40–41.

Mutant Ecologies

Radioactive Life in Post–Cold War New Mexico · Joseph Masco, University of Chicago

The article in one breath

In the 1954 film Them! the first atomic test breeds giant mutant ants in the New Mexico desert. The enemy turns out to be “ours”. Masco takes the film's insight seriously. With the Trinity test, Americans “entered a postnuclear environment of their own invention”, and the nuclear apocalypse is “already here”, moving through bodies and the biosphere. To study it he proposes mutation in place of the fashionable “hybridity”, because mutation tracks change across generations, whether as injury, improvement or noise. The article has three parts. First, Cold War testing treated living beings as instruments. At Operation Plumbbob (1957), 845 pigs were blasted, burned and irradiated, while fallout reached “all organs and tissues” of every American. Second, after the Cold War the Department of Energy reinvented contaminated weapons sites as wildlife preserves, celebrating “survivals” of a sacrificed landscape alongside radioactive alligators, tumbleweeds and fruit flies. Third, the idea of the nuclear worker expanded. Tritium-sniffing honeybees, DARPA's cyborg insects, Pueblo youth trained as monitors, and residents whose tissues were taken without consent all became “environmental sentinels”. At Los Alamos's Area G waste site, planned for a thousand years of state control, a worker's videotape ends in play-acted mutation. The Manhattan Project, Masco concludes, “has reinvented the biosphere as a nuclear space” p. 541.

Thesis
“One of the most profound legacies of the Manhattan Project has been to put in motion changes in specific social and biological ecologies that are highly mutable” p. 518. The weapons complex has “collectively turned the entire biosphere into an experimental zone—one in which we all live” p. 533.
Against

(2) Hybridity theory (Latour, Haraway, Rabinow), whose “focus on parental elements and temporal orientation toward the present” cannot see change across generations p. 522. (3) The DOE's post–Cold War language of “preservation”, “purity” and “stewardship”. Claim
Them! shows the bomb as a domestic transformation of nature, not a future war. Masco proposes “mutation” to study the Manhattan Project's multigenerational legacy in New Mexico.
Evidence
Them! (1954); Cousins and Schell; Russell (2001).
Against
Containment and deterrence, which treat nuclear apocalypse as a future event to be “endlessly deterred”.

Them! (1954)

In the Hollywood film Them! (Douglas 1954), “Los Alamos weapons science and Cold War logics of ‘containment’ are both turned quite sensationally on their heads” p. 517. Instead of producing security through a deterrent, the nuclear complex is “the domestic source of proliferating radiation effects, creating an entirely new ecology of risk in the form of gigantic mutant carnivorous ants”. The ants come from the first atomic explosion, set off by Los Alamos scientists in New Mexico on 16 July 1945. The Trinity test appears not as a triumph of big science or the way the war was ended, but as “the source of an inverted natural order, in which the smallest of creatures can become a totalizing threat”. It demands “a total mobilization of the security state” to protect citizens from Los Alamos science. The film articulates “a new kind of nuclear fear in 1954, not one based on the apocalypse of nuclear war but on the everyday transformation of self and nature within an irradiated landscape” p. 517.

Masco's reading

The film is remembered for its McCarthy-era theatrics, with the ants as “a thinly veiled allegory for the communist ‘menace’”. But it “more subtly presents a devastating critique of U.S. nuclear policy at the very height of the Cold War”. “It argues that with the first nuclear explosion on July 16, 1945, Americans entered a postnuclear environment of their own invention. From this perspective, the nuclear apocalypse is not in the future—a thing to be endlessly deterred through nuclear weapons and international relations—it is already here, being played out in the unpredictable movement of radioactive materials moving through bodies and biosphere” pp. 517–518.

Insects and the species question

Them! belongs to a wider discourse in which nuclear critics used insects to engage “the philosophical status of the nuclear age” p. 518:

  • Norman Cousins' essay “Modern Man is Obsolete” (1945), written days after Hiroshima and Nagasaki, says only “men and ants” make war. It asks whether organised conflict is a biological imperative in both species, “highly organized but ultimately mindless beasts”.
  • Jonathan Schell (1982) pictures a postnuclear American “republic of insects and grass”: humans are too fragile to survive a nuclear war, and only insects that can adapt to radiation would win.
  • Both argue the bomb “demands social evolution”, and both use insects as “a critical mirror to humanity”.

These discourses question the “nature” of nature: the power of atomic energy and the “purity” of ecosystems against human “nature”. “Technoscience militarizes nature in these discourses, making social evolution and biological extinction the dual focal points of a new kind of modernity” (Russell 2001). The bomb “initiates a profound mutation” in self, nature and society p. 518.

Masco's thesis

The article explores “the production of nuclear natures in New Mexico, arguing that one of the most profound legacies of the Manhattan Project has been to put in motion changes in specific social and biological ecologies that are highly mutable”. To understand them he offers “a theoretization of ‘mutation’”. For an anthropology of science its value is its attention “to multigenerational reproduction and the quality of biosocial transformations over time—marked alternatively as injury, improvement, or noise” p. 518.

The argument in three parts p. 518
  1. Nuclear injuries produced by Cold War testing, especially radiation experiments on living beings.
  2. Post–Cold War efforts to reinscript radioactive environments as ecological “improvements”.
  3. How the concept of a “nuclear worker” has expanded to link humans and wildlife “within a shared genetic experiment in northern New Mexico, producing a highly ambiguous and charged social space”.

Attention to “the specific terms informing a ‘mutant ecology’” explores the links “between natures, politics, and futures while recognizing the specificity of place within a nonetheless global nuclear biosphere” pp. 518–519.

Radioactive Mutants

Bravo and Them! in 1954 · the film as crime story · background radiation as a norm · collective and individual risk · mutation vs hybridity

Claim
The bomb's traces saturate the biosphere, so risk is at once collective and individual, uncertain and multigenerational. Mutation, not hybridity, is the right way to think about it.
Evidence
Castle Bravo; Them!'s plot; background radiation figures; Makhijani and Schwartz's seven exposed classes; GAO (2000); Petryna on Chernobyl; Semipalatinsk mutation studies.
Against
Hybridity (Latour, Haraway, Rabinow), read through Robert Young's point that a hybrid is infertile.

Bravo, 1954

Them! opened in June 1954, three months after Los Alamos scientists set off the largest thermonuclear explosion of the Cold War at Bikini Atoll in the Marshall Islands p. 519:

  • Bravo detonated at 2.5 times its expected force, with a 15-megaton yield.
  • Its fallout contaminated 50 thousand square miles of the Pacific with “serious to lethal levels of radioactivity” (Weisgall).
  • Those exposed included 223 indigenous residents of Rongerik, Rongelap, Ailinginae and Utirik atolls, and the 23-member crew of the Japanese fishing boat The Lucky Dragon.
  • As Americans flocked to Them!, one of the year's most successful films, the news followed their radiation sickness, “educating many Americans, for the first time, to the biological effects of radioactive fallout” fn 1. The US had heavily censored images of the bombs' effects on Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Fear of Bravo's contamination caused panic around the Pacific, and officials in full protective gear took Geiger counters to US fish markets to test the food supply.

For many, this “graphic documentation of the ecological costs of nuclear testing” turned the nuclear program “from an exclusively military project to a global environmental threat”. After Bravo, Americans saw the bomb also “as a chemical and biological hazard at home”. Each test raised “the global burden of radioactive elements in the biosphere, overturning the ‘national security’ logics supporting the nuclear arsenal by introducing the possibility of cellular mutations in plants, animals, and people on a planetary scale” p. 519.

The film as crime story

Them! was “the first U.S. popular culture text to depict the bomb not as a military weapon but as an ecological threat”. It dates from before the arsenal became a “normalized (all but invisible)” part of life, and it is “the urtext for an ongoing fascination with mutation in American popular culture” p. 519 (on atomic popular culture, fn 2). It won an Academy Award for its one special effect, the giant ants. Though it “might appear today as a form of atomic kitsch”, it is “played straight”, a “compelling textual effort to assess the ‘newness’ of the atomic age”.

  • Police find a young girl wandering the desert alone in her bathrobe, too traumatized to speak: “the image of a postnuclear survivor”. Her parents are among a series of bizarre murders.
  • The clues: buildings destroyed from the inside out, traces of sugar and formic acid, no motive. The FBI is as baffled as the police and cannot make “the imaginative leap required to see mutant nature as the cause”.
  • Frustrated by a lack of fingerprints, police and FBI agents “stare without recognition at a strange impression found in the earth”. Its plaster cast is the footprint of a giant ant, “a criminal signature literally too large for the police to comprehend”. One officer: “lots of evidence, loaded with clues, but nothing adds up” p. 520.
  • The crimes are “unnatural” by prenuclear standards, so “the first problem of the nuclear age” is “linking perception and imagination in a world operating by new laws of possibility”.
  • Scientists, soldiers and the FBI must contain the ants before they reproduce, “introducing a new kind of nuclear proliferation”.
Masco's reading

Them! “both deploys and ridicules the military logics of containment”: if the ants are a crime, who is responsible? It demonizes them as an external Other while knowing the US security state made them. “The terrible joke embedded in the title of the film suggests that the agents of destruction are foreign born rather than domestic—‘theirs’ rather than ‘ours’”. Dispersed nuclear materials create “a new kind of nature—mutant, wild, and uncontainable by the state”. Humans create this mutant ecology and are part of it, “producing a future that is as unpredictable at the level of genetic stability as it is at the level of international relations” p. 520.

Background radiation

Background radiation is the baseline federal authorities treat as inherent in the environment. It is the yearly dose the average American receives from all sources, and the standard against which industrial exposures are measured p. 520.

  • Today's rate is 360 millirems per year: 300 from “naturally” occurring sources (cosmic rays, radon, the earth's surface, potassium-40 in our bodies) and 60 from the cumulative effects of industry, meaning nuclear medicine, nuclear power and weapons testing (Wolfson 1991).
  • So the “background” for all radiation studies “is a mix of naturally occurring and industrial effects”. Los Alamos's trace elements leave “an atomic signature found in people, plants, animals, soils, and waterways”. The Manhattan Project “inaugurated a subtle but total transformation of the biosphere”.
Masco's view

“The very idea of a background radiation standard is to establish a norm, a new definition of the ‘natural’ in which the past effects of the nuclear complex are embedded as a fundamental aspect of the ecosystem.” The bomb must be studied at the level of the nation-state, “the local ecosystem, the organism, and ultimately, the cell” pp. 520–521.

Collective and individual risk

The background rate is an average and applies to no one in particular fn 3. Radiation standards assume “a kind of universal national subject”, which northern New Mexico's diversity of land use and health care challenges. Real risk depends on specific exposures. Arjun Makhijani and Stephen Schwartz identify seven classes of people at risk from US nuclear production p. 521:

(1) Workers in uranium mines and mills and in nuclear weapons design, production and testing facilities; (2) armed-forces personnel who participated in atmospheric weapons testing; (3) people living near nuclear weapons sites; (4) human experiment subjects; (5) armed forces personnel and other workers who were exposed during the deployment, transportation and other handling and maintenance of weapons within the Department of Defense; (6) residents of Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945; and (7) the world inhabitants for centuries to come.

Makhijani and Schwartz 1998:396 · p. 521

The differences between these groups show “both the generality and specificity of the nuclear age”: exposures are “simultaneously collective (involving everyone on the planet) and highly individualized”. No two are the same, since location, occupation, events such as Chernobyl, physiology and ecosystem all combine. Yet “if we were able to track back in time and space, following the trajectory of the various chemicals and nuclear materials now in each of our bodies, one subset of these industrial signatures would lead back to Los Alamos”. America's nuclear project has produced “new kinds of risk societies, unified not by national affiliation, but by exposure levels, health effects, and nuclear fear” p. 521 (on the “risk society”, Beck; fn 4).

Partial knowledge

For most people the risk is theoretical, not known. Studies of Hiroshima, Nagasaki, the Marshall Islands, Chernobyl and nuclear workers explain high doses well, but “the effects of low-level radiation remain a subject of intense scientific debate” p. 521. fn 5 The US General Accounting Office concluded in 2000 that standards “lack a conclusively verified scientific basis, according to a consensus of recognized scientists”. Adriana Petryna (2002) calls this “partial knowledge”, which makes the nuclear challenge “as much the regulation of uncertainty as the documentation of biological effects”. Radiation illness has “a displacement in time (sometimes occurring decades after exposure) and a potential to be genetically transferred across generations” p. 521.

“The subtle but totalizing scope of the nuclear transformation of nature”, with plutonium, strontium, cesium and other elements dispersed into the biosphere, challenges the idea of a “nuclear test”, which at Los Alamos meant a detonation: “how does one define or limit the scope of the nuclear laboratory when its trace elements can be found literally everywhere on the planet?” While Los Alamos perfected weapons for a “closed world” of command and control (Edwards 1996), testing turned “the earth into a vast laboratory of nuclear effects that maintain an unpredictable claim on a deep future” p. 522. fn 6 Industrial pollutants are global too, and there has been no pristine nature since farming began. But the nuclear economy is new because its effects are both worldwide and “nationalized through a discourse of state security”, so each effect speaks to national belonging or alienation. The Comaroffs call the way nature is used here “as alibi, as a fertile allegory for rendering some people and objects strange”.

Why mutation, not hybridity

For Bruno Latour, Donna Haraway and Paul Rabinow, hybridity reveals technoscientific objects as fusions of nature and culture fn 7. Latour argues that science “purifies” nature as external to human intervention; others use the cyborg. But for radioactive natures, hybridity's “focus on parental elements and temporal orientation toward the present” limits our ability to see “multigenerational reproduction” p. 522. Robert Young (1995) recalls the biological definition. A species can reproduce; a hybrid is the infertile offspring of two species, so hybridity is “a form of generational stasis”. The bomb's world is planetary in scale, local in its effects, multigenerational and changeable, rooted “as much in ambiguity or latency as in material fact”. “The 24,000-year half-life of plutonium, for example, presents a multimillennial colonization of the future” p. 522.

So Masco proposes “extending our theorization of the complexity of nature–culture forms via the concept of mutation”. A mutation occurs “when the ionization of an atom changes the genetic coding of a cell, producing a new reproductive outcome”. Over time it has three possible outcomes p. 522:

  1. evolution, “an enhancing of the organism through a new adaptation to the environment”;
  2. injury, such as cancer or deformity;
  3. genetic noise, changes that neither improve nor injure but can still affect future generations.

Mutation codes time both past and future. It assumes change without prejudging its scale or kind, and it is reproduced across generations, “a specific kind of break with the past that reinvents the future”. It points beyond the networks that built the bomb to “the wide-ranging and long-term social and environmental effects of the production complex itself” p. 522.

  • Post–Cold War studies at Semipalatinsk found elevated mutation rates in the children of people exposed to fallout in the 1950s (Dubrova et al.) pp. 522–523.
  • Petryna's study of Chernobyl shows exposed people pursuing a “biological citizenship”, with daily life centred on negotiating risk, access to health care and radiation trauma p. 523.
Masco's view

“Although the Cold War American nuclear project has not yet produced giant ants”, it has put new material and ideological elements into citizens' bodies and the nation's social body “that continue to proliferate, promising unpredictable outcomes”. “The Manhattan Project remains an unending experiment”: nuclear war is still possible, and the biosphere and social orders are still being transformed. Each US citizen negotiates the traces of Los Alamos science, “making each of us real or potential mutants”. The rest of the article asks how “the pursuit of security through military technoscience has raised questions about the structural integrity of plants, animals, and people”. “The nuclear saturates both environments and social imaginations in New Mexico”, and the ethnographic challenge is “to realize a deep sense of this mutating future” in specific places p. 523.

Nuclear Test Subjects

Fallout for everyone · national amnesia · the Crossroads fish and the Chernobyl bull · Operation Plumbbob's pigs · the animal body as prototype

“Any person living in the contiguous United States since 1951 has been exposed to radioactive fallout, and all organs and tissues of the body have received some radiation exposure.”U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the National Cancer Institute · epigraph, p. 523

“Radiation can make cells lose their memory, and loss of memory seems to be one of the cultural effects of the bombs too, for Americans forgot that bomb after bomb was being exploded here.”Rebecca Solnit, Savage Dreams · epigraph, p. 523

Claim
Aboveground testing was a biomedical experiment on a species scale. The injured animal body studied in the 1950s has become, after the Cold War, a celebrated “survival”.
Evidence
The 2001 fallout report; Bradley's radio-autographs of Bikini fish; the Chernobyl bull Uran; the declassified DOD film of Operation Plumbbob.
Against
The “national amnesia” that forgot the tests, and any separation of experiment from real-world effect.

Fallout and forgetting

The Cold War arsenal “seems to have distributed risk to human populations on a species scale” p. 523. The 2001 joint report of the Department of Health and Human Services, the CDC and the National Cancer Institute says all Americans carry traces of US testing. But, as Rebecca Solnit reminds us, this national exposure “became an exercise in national amnesia”, as citizens “seemed to forget or repress the implications of living within a national nuclear complex”.

  • Much of this goes back to the “cultural repercussions” of the move to underground testing in 1963, “both a public health initiative and a means of making nuclear testing more covert in a world of competing states” p. 524. Without the mushroom cloud, mutation became “a generalized concern in American culture, rather than an explicit critique of the nuclear security state”.
  • After the Cold War, new environmental laws, declassification and mounting costs brought renewed awareness (fn 8). The new visibility “does not take the shape of a mushroom cloud but is, instead, measured in terms of toxicity levels, cancer rates for certain populations, and in the ecological challenges of Cold War nuclear production sites”.
  • The 2001 report estimates that aboveground testing in the continental US (1951–61) produced 11,000 cancer deaths and between 11,300 and 212,000 thyroid cancers p. 524.

As “participants in this radioactive ecology”, how should we evaluate “the dialectics of survival and sacrifice that remain at the center of the nuclear state”, and what has changed since the Soviet Union's end? p. 524

A fish: Operation Crossroads (1946)

Two illustrations from opposite ends of the Cold War: “the first involves a fish; the second, a bull—one an image of radioactive death, the other an image of radioactive life” p. 524. Operation Crossroads (1946) was designed to sink the remnants of the Japanese navy and display American nuclear power to the world (Figure 1). For scientists it was a chance to study the biological effects of America's fourth and fifth nuclear explosions, after Trinity, Hiroshima and Nagasaki, on test animals and on the Marshall Islands. In his memoir No Place to Hide (1948), David Bradley describes a new sign of the nuclear age, the “radio-autograph”:

Radio-autographs are made by taking a small fish, slicing it longitudinally down the middle, drying it in a blast of warm air, and then placing the fish, cut side down, on a photographic plate. After a suitable time the radioactivity present in the tissues of the fish will have exposed the adjacent film, which, when developed, will then outline the fish in tones which are proportional to the radioactivity present. . . . Almost all seagoing fish recently caught around the atoll of Bikini have been radioactive. Thus the disease is passed on from species to species like an epizootic. The only factors which tend to limit the disease, as distinguished from infectious diseases are the half-lives of the material involved, and the degree of dilution and dissemination of the fission products.

Bradley 1948:125–126 · p. 524

The radio-autograph is “a new sign of modernity, one made visible simply by placing the biological being on photographic film”. The creature “produces its own negative image, drawn in the mirrored form of contaminated organs and orifices” p. 525. Bradley treats radiation as a disease moving through the food chain, limited only by half-lives: “a form of incipient death, moving through the biosphere as contamination or infectious disease”. The Bikini ecosystem still lives with iodine-131's eight-day half-life, strontium-90's 28.5-year half-life, and plutonium's 24,500-year half-life p. 525. Marshall Islanders and US military personnel are still negotiating their entry into this ecology (Weisgall). With Manhattan Project personnel and the survivors of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, they form “the first distinct classes of a new global nuclear ‘risk society’” (Beck).

A bull: Uran at Chernobyl

If the Crossroads fish offer “an image of a nuclear ecology of damaged organs, a corrupted food chain, and death”, another image from the post–Cold War period “privileges survival”. Chernobyl is now for many “the primary reference for radiation exposure” pp. 525–526. After the 1986 meltdown and fire in one of its four reactors, a 30-kilometer “zone of exclusion” was created and the reactor was encased in a concrete “sarcophagus”. By the late 1990s 3.5 million Ukrainians were legally recognized as “sufferers” of what the Soviet state first called a “controlled biomedical crisis” (Petryna).

In the 1990s a bull was found grazing in the zone's contaminated grass. Its apparent health gave workers and neighbours “a source of commentary and pleasure”. Radioecologists named it Uran, after uranium, for the radionuclides in its body, and bred it to study genetic effects. Unlike the Crossroads fish, the cattle Uran sired were valued “not as signs of damaged nature but as ‘survivals,’ illustrations of a natural adaptation to a radioactive environment” p. 526.

Masco's view

The cases show a new “species” knowledge. “If the Cold War logics of deterrence sought to preserve the stability of the present through nuclear weapons, the power of the radioactive, yet thriving, bull is that it is neither pure nor hybrid but alive and potentially mutant.” Life in a radioactive zone “cannot be narrated within a discourse of purity—of either the nation-state or the state of nature”. The dialectic of survival and sacrifice now includes “an ecological dynamic that is both external to, and preceding, the possibility of nuclear war”. “Recognizing radioactive natures redefines the question of nuclear security, linking the survival of the nuclear-powered state to the integrity of both social and cellular reproduction over time” p. 526.

No line between experiment and world

Los Alamos knew radiation's biological effects from the start, so Crossroads was “an early step in an ongoing biological as well as military experiment”. The goal “was not to stop the exposure rates” but to mitigate the bomb's ecological effects during an arms race. In fact, “there was no separation between the experiment and its real world effects during the aboveground-testing regime of 1945–63” p. 526.

The pig: Operation Plumbbob (1957)

Operation Plumbbob was a series of 30 detonations at the Nevada Proving Grounds in 1957. It advanced weapons science and tested blast effects on planes, helicopters, blimps and tanks, on bunkers, foxholes and ammunition depots, and on land mines and missile guidance. A large part studied environmental and biological effects pp. 526–527. The DOD's 31-minute film Operation Plumbbob: Military Effects Studies was declassified in 1997. Under “Biomedical Testing”, the narrator says that a “major portion of the experiment was devoted to testing the effects of nuclear weapons on a large biological specimen: the pig” p. 527.

  • Shot Wilson: 135 pigs in individual aluminum containers in a grid (Figure 2). The bomb exploded with greater-than-expected force (10 kilotons). Only two of the 135 pigs lived more than 30 days, “making the experiment, by its own terms, a failure” p. 527. fn 9 Plumbbob also tested fallout control: Shot Rainier was the first contained US underground detonation, enabling the underground test regime of 1963–92. But each shot's immediate aim “was to increase U.S. nuclear war fighting capabilities”.
  • Shot Priscilla, a Los Alamos project on reducing fallout, also studied “radiation, thermal, and mechanical injury effects” on living beings. Seventy-eight pigs were shaved (to simulate human skin), painted or wrapped in fabric, and set in elevated boxes (Figure 3) to test uniform fabrics, flash-burn creams and thermal shielding.
  • 710 pigs in open pens and open-faced boxes behind sheets of glass: some were irradiated, others given shrapnel wounds “of the kind soldiers might experience in a nuclear strike”. The irradiated and wounded pigs were then used to field-test surgical techniques p. 527.
  • Priscilla too was a biomedical failure: a nearly 100-percent fatality rate from “mechanical injury to the organism” and “massive radiation” p. 528.

The film shows the blast wave hitting a pen of animals in slow motion, then scientists collecting the injured and dead. The narration calls the pig “an instrument”, and a close-up shows a preprinted chart of a pig's body for marking injuries (Figure 4). The chart's “industrial logic of production and control” is “at odds with the chaos” of technicians in white anticontamination suits trying to round up wounded and dying animals p. 528. “The effects of the nuclear blast on the instrument-body of the pig” are shown “with the same efficiency” as those on machinery.

Masco's view

Concluding that the nearly 100-percent death rate among the 845 pigs can “be applied to man”, Plumbbob's biomedical project “seems successful only in constructing the traumatized organism as an institutional project. Nuclear trauma is not avoided here—indeed, it is instrumentally and methodically pursued—in an effort to test the fragility of human and animal bodies” p. 528.

The animal body as prototype

“Each aboveground nuclear test was a biomedical experiment that explicitly sought to mitigate the effects of the bomb by methodically applying its force to plants, animals, and ultimately, people” p. 529. Pigs, dogs, sheep, cows, monkeys and mice were used to test radiation's effects on skin, lungs, eyes, blood and genes, in the millisecond of the blast and over the years as mutations appeared. “The protected body of the Cold Warrior, increasingly rendered as cyborg in the cockpit of planes and other military machines, was thus prefigured by the vaporized, mutilated, and traumatized animal body.” The pig's instrumentalization marks “the enormous stakes of the Cold War nuclear arms race” and illustrates “a larger production and deployment of radioactive natures since 1945”. Soldiers and citizens were part of the same regime, “exponentially expanding the frame of the nuclear experiment from the confines of the Nevada Test Site to the global biosphere”, since “all organs and tissues of the body have received some radiation exposure” (2001:2) p. 529.

fn 10 Plumbbob also tried to “harden” the human body. Volunteers tested a high-speed electromechanical shutter on goggles against flashblindness, meant to protect Air Force pilots. Worried that troops would panic at their first sight of a blast, the DOD exposed 100 soldiers to shot Galileo and then timed drills such as assembling and disassembling rifles (see Week 6).

Masco's turn

“The radioactive body once studied for its injury is now celebrated as a Cold War survival and embedded within a new discourse of ecological purity” p. 529.

Radioactive Natures: Life in the Wildlife/Sacrifice Zone

The geography of sacrifice · Los Alamos's wildlife preserve · Savannah River, INEEL, Hanford · tumbleweeds and fruit flies · the exception that proves the rule

Claim
After the Cold War the DOE rebranded parts of its contaminated sites as wildlife preserves, making biodiversity the security object of the nuclear state. But radioactive nature crosses the fences, and the preserves depend on the sacrifice they hide.
Evidence
Bill Richardson's 1999 announcements; DOE cleanup estimates; Associated Press and other reports on Savannah River, INEEL and Hanford.
Against
The DOE's discourse of “preservation”, “purity” and untouched nature.

The geography of sacrifice

At the end of the Cold War the complex covered over 3,300 square miles, with 13 major institutions and dozens of smaller sites p. 530. They were mostly in isolated, rural, nonindustrial places, “a complex form of domestic development”: Oak Ridge (Tennessee), Hanford (Washington) and Los Alamos (New Mexico), all 1943, then Aiken (South Carolina), Amarillo (Texas), Idaho Falls (Idaho), Rocky Flats (Colorado) and the Nevada Test Site. Materials were made, weapons built and tested, and waste stored there, “fusing local ecologies and local communities with the American nuclear project”.

  • “The internal logics of nuclear development required deliberate acts of territorial devastation, producing an archipelago of contaminated sites”, from South Carolina to Nevada, Kentucky to Washington, Alaska to the Marshall Islands.
  • Valerie Kuletz calls it a “geography of sacrifice”. Restoring the “remediable” sites is estimated at $216–$400 billion, likely more than the arsenal itself cost p. 530.
  • “Nuclear security has required complex new forms of internal cannibalism”, as both citizens' bodies and the state's territory absorbed new nuclear signatures after 1943.

Los Alamos: “the reunification of land and community”

After the Cold War the complex performed a “territorial reinscription”. On 30 October 1999 Secretary of Energy Bill Richardson announced a 1,000-acre wildlife preserve within the 43-square-mile Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL). The White Rock Canyon Preserve was “a unique ecosystem”, home to “bald eagles, peregrine falcons, southwestern flycatchers, 300 other species of mammals, birds, reptiles, and amphibians, as well as 900 species of plants” p. 530. Richardson:

How fitting that we are here today at Los Alamos, the place that witnessed the dawn of the atomic age. . . . In places of rare environmental resources, we have a special responsibility to the states and communities that have supported and hosted America's long effort to win the Cold War—and we owe it to future generations to protect these precious places so that they can enjoy nature's plenty just as we do. Los Alamos's White Rock Canyon is such a place, an able bearer of New Mexico's legacy of enchantment. After today, it will be more so as we celebrate the reunification of land and community.

Richardson 1999 · p. 530

A “wildlife preserve” claims purity, nature kept in a “state of nature”. What can that mean at a nuclear site? Richardson spoke after a decade of intense environmental politics. The post–Cold War period began in New Mexico with a moratorium on nuclear tests and the designation of 2,200 contaminated sites within LANL, needing over $3.3 billion of cleanup p. 531. Community groups demanded health studies and monitoring of water, soil and air. The preserve recognises the investments of Pueblo and Nuevomexicano communities in the land fn 11. Pueblo Nations hold cultural and some legal claims to laboratory land, and much of the workforce descends from Spanish colonizers with over 400 years in the region. But “preservation” can recognise them “only … by ignoring the long standing practices of environmental ruin” p. 531.

Masco's view

Instead of Bikini fish or Nevada pigs, the DOE now preserves the flycatcher and the peregrine falcon, trying “to expand retroactively its Cold War mission from nuclear deterrence to environmental protection”. That forwards “a deep structural contradiction”. Nuclear production has made “the biosphere itself a postnuclear formation”, so the “nature” of wildlife has changed. “If exposure is now a general condition—a question of degree rather than kind—then what does it mean to promote such images of survival in the midst of contamination?” p. 531

A new state territoriality

This recuperation of “nature” “articulates a new form of state territoriality”. The DOE has turned over 175,800 acres of nuclear sites into wildlife preserves “by legislative fiat”. They were carved from security buffer zones, fenced off at mid-century and cut off from people during the Cold War, “among the most heavily fortified wilderness areas in the world” p. 531. By presenting them as untouched for 50 years, the DOE redefines what fortification is for, “replacing nuclear weapons systems with biodiversity as the security object of the nuclear state”. “This suturing together of wildlife preserve and national sacrifice zone has become an expansive post–Cold War project.”

Savannah River and INEEL

At Savannah River the DOE “failed to mention” that “the unusually healthy alligators and rather large bass fish found at the Savannah River Site are also unusually radioactive”, carrying cesium-137, “a byproduct of nuclear material production on the site”, home to five reactors. The site “maintains a massive environmental problem in the form of 34 million gallons of high-level radioactive waste, a multimillennial challenge to the future”, yet it was rescripted as a reserve preserved for “future generations” p. 532.

Inaugurating the Idaho reserve, Richardson said the Department of the Interior estimated that 98 percent of intact sagebrush steppe had been destroyed or altered since European settlement. Because INEEL “has been a largely protected and secure facility for 50 years”, it still had unimpacted habitat, “one of the last vestiges of this important system” p. 532. With 52 reactors and 580,000 gallons of high-level waste, INEEL is “redefining the definition of ‘protected’ and ‘secure’—as well as ‘impact’ and ‘risk’—for distant future generations”. The animals' new status is “primarily a bureaucratic one”; it ignores how animals, ecosystems and radionuclides move between reserve and production site.

Hanford: tumbleweeds and fruit flies

“The hard insight informing these new wildlife preserves is that isolation from human traffic provides an enormous ecological benefit: Human contact is more immediately toxic for many ecosystems than are radioactive materials” p. 532. But the dual structure assumes nuclear materials stay put and borders can be patrolled for millennia. “This logic is trumped most convincingly” at Hanford:

  • mulberry trees show rising strontium-90 over the last decade;
  • the Russian thistle sends roots 20 feet down, sucks up strontium-90 and cesium, and breaks off as a windblown “radioactive tumbleweed”. Hanford spends millions a year on it, with crews “armed with pitchforks” patrolling in trucks “to wrangle the radioactive weeds” pp. 532–533;
  • in 1998 fruit flies landed in liquid radioactive material and spread contamination for weeks, requiring a $2.5 million cleanup. It dramatized “this inability to enforce the distinction between wilderness and wasteland” p. 533.
Masco's view

“Radioactive tumbleweeds, contaminated fruit flies, and toxic alligators—these are all survivals of the Cold War nuclear project as well as new forms of nuclear nature.” Next to each preserve are sites “not just minimally radioactive according to federal standards” but so hazardous they must be fenced and watched for, “in some cases, literally tens of thousands of years”: “Cold War survivals of another kind”. The complex “could not have produced 70,000 nuclear weapons from 1943–92 without favoring industrial production over environmental concerns”. Just as background radiation normalises testing, the wildlife zones offer “an image of a nature created through nuclear politics and radioactive practices”. “The wildlife preserve is thus an exception that proves the rule within the nuclear complex.” “Despite the new bureaucratic recognition of the ferruginous hawk, the pygmy rabbit, and the larkspur”, the line between “normal, abnormal, and pathological” is being redrawn. “The experimental projects that produced and now maintain the bomb have collectively turned the entire biosphere into an experimental zone—one in which we all live” p. 533.

Environmental Sentinels, or the Militarization of the Honey Bee

Escobar's regimes of nature · long-term stewardship · Area G's thousand-year plan · the tritium bee · DARPA's insects · people as monitors

Claim
Long-term stewardship turns contamination into permanent surveillance, and surveillance itself makes new natures. Bees, cyborg insects and whole human communities become “environmental sentinels”, often without consent.
Evidence
The DOE's stewardship mission; the Area G Performance Assessment (Hollis 1997); LANL honeybee studies; DARPA's Controlled Biosystems program; Pueblo monitoring; the tissue-sampling lawsuit.
Against
The assessment's assumption that nature and the state will be stable for a thousand years.

Escobar's three natures

Arturo Escobar proposes “a prismatic view of nature” that weaves together “the biophysical, the cultural, and the technoeconomic” p. 533. He distinguishes three regimes:

  • “organic nature”, an ecological view of human and natural links;
  • “capitalist nature”, nature objectified, managed and extracted;
  • “technonature”, biocultural technologies such as the new genetics that reinvent society and nature.

In this tripartite scheme, understandings of nature are “always positional and hybrid, requiring an ethnographic framing of physical context, cultural logic, and global–local forms”. The value of Escobar's approach is “its openness to new configurations of nature and society and its attention to local detail”; his political ecology is “in motion, subject to continuing historical transformation but still capable of recognizing multiplicity”. Mutation is “a logic of generational reproduction” that keeps Escobar's multiplicity and acknowledges environmental effects. Pursued this way, political ecology focuses on the multigenerational outcomes of fusions of nature and culture, and demands “a critical stance toward each instantiation of the mutant form—as evolution, as degeneration, or as noise” p. 534.

Long-term stewardship

The preserves are radioactive natures “paradoxically marked for federal conservation as valued ‘survivals’”. “These newly recognized zones of biodiversity are not untouched by the nuclear state”, and their deep future, “as a natural and socially valued space”, is “an open question”. The DOE admits that as many as 109 sites are too contaminated to remediate effectively. What to do with them over decades, centuries and millennia is now framed as “long-term stewardship” p. 534. The DOE's definition:

The Long-Term Stewardship Program will maintain and continuously improve protection of public health, safety, and the environment at a site or portion of a site assigned to DOE for such purposes. This mission includes providing sustained human and environmental well-being through the mitigation of residual risks and the conservation of the site's natural, ecological, and cultural resources. Mission activities will include vigilantly maintaining “post-cleanup” controls on residual hazards; sustaining and maintaining engineered controls, infrastructure, and institutional controls; seeking to avoid or minimize the creation of additional “post-cleanup” long-term stewardship liabilities during current and future site operations; enabling the best land use and resource conservation within the constraints of current and future contamination; and periodic re-evaluation of priorities and strategies in response to changes in knowledge, science, technology, site conditions, or regional setting. …

DOE Long-Term Stewardship Program mission statement (2003) · p. 534 · fn 13

The program treats the Cold War's legacy “as a bureaucratic, as well as technoscientific, problem”. “Promising an increasingly intimate interaction with contaminated sites”, it hopes to deploy “as yet undeveloped technologies” pp. 534–535. Through constant surveillance it “naturalizes the environmental problems of the Cold War” around managing contaminated land “for an indefinite future”.

Masco's view

“This is a utopian program that imagines perfect management of Cold War nuclear waste and contaminated sites for millennia—despite the prior 50 years of environmental neglect” p. 535.

Governmentality, inverted

For Foucault, governmentality is the state policing its population to improve its health and well-being. In stewardship, “the logic of national security is inverted; the threat of foreign arsenals and armies are replaced by an internal discourse of contamination and territorial colonization” p. 535. Governing means protecting citizens from the security state's own industrial effects, “redrawing the lines between policing and welfare”. But “environmental well-being” is undefined. The DOE cannot restore a prenuclear state, so “clean-up” means meeting regulatory standards that depend on expected land use. The program hopes for ecological stasis until science improves, but the untreatable sites show “the Cold War still has a powerful claim on a deep future”. With budgets projected only to 2070, the DOE estimates $100 million a year just to maintain the 109 sites p. 535.

Area G

Stewardship sites create stewardship communities, “entirely new ecosocial orders” p. 535. Area G has been LANL's main nuclear waste site since 1957: a 100-acre facility on Mesita del Buey, a finger-like mesa of the Pajarito Plateau.

  • It holds low-level waste, mostly contaminated objects, and significant quantities of plutonium-239 and uranium-238 in pits and shafts.
  • Inventories have been carefully documented since 1988; few records exist for 1957–71 and poor ones for 1971–88.
  • That matters because White Rock (population 6,800) lies just east and San Ildefonso Pueblo land just north. Pueblo members “collect plants and hunt game in the shadow of Area G”, and keep shrines and sacred sites there pp. 535–536.
  • A laboratory “performance assessment” says Area G will be full by 2044, “initiating a new kind of territorial project” p. 536:

Active institutional control will continue for a period of 100 years (between 2047 and 2146). During institutional control, site access will be controlled, environmental monitoring will be performed, and closure cap integrity will be maintained. After the institutional-control period, it is assumed the site will be maintained by the DOE or its equivalent for as-yet undefined industrial uses. This industrial-use period is assumed to prevail for the 900 years remaining in the compliance period (between 2147 and 3046).

Hollis 1997:10 · p. 536

Evaluating risks to future populations under “a variety of intrusion scenarios”, the report confirms that the Manhattan Project “inaugurated a new ecological regime on the Pajarito Plateau”, now bound up with plutonium's 24,000-year half-life, yet Area G evaluates risk only on a 1,000-year frame p. 536. The assessment says: “The ability to contain radioactivity locally depends largely on nature, while the ability to prevent intrusion depends solely on man.” It assumes “current natural conditions will prevail” and “a government entity will maintain the site and control access to it” for 1,000 years (Hollis 1997:16).

Masco's view

“Both nature and the state are, for the sake of the study, assumed to be stable entities across the next millennium, even as the evidence of the last 50 years shows a dramatic change in both.” Subtler changes are already giving the plateau “a new state of nature, more mutant than stable”. Plumes of tritium and residues of high explosives are already leaking from Area G, showing the plateau's geology is more permeable than assumed. Tritium in wells around LANL raises concerns about transport “through the geological tuft” (sic) and the regional aquifer. “Indeed, surveillance itself has become the basis for new kinds of nature” p. 536.

The tritium bee

The Italian honeybee (Apis mellifera) forages widely and returns to a fixed hive, so it is “a natural environmental surveyor” p. 536. Los Alamos scientists showed it is particularly sensitive to tritium, used in nuclear weapons “to enhance the size of the explosion” and “notoriously difficult to contain”. Used as a monitor since the late 1970s, bees have recorded rising tritium at Area G through the 1990s (Fresquez et al. 1997) p. 537.

Masco's view

Pueblo communities treat mesa tops as culturally important places, with shrines and sacred sites. Pueblo cosmology works “not to deploy nature as a technoscientific object, but to integrate Pueblo members into the local ecology” (Ortiz 1969). In Eastern Pueblo cosmologies the bee is “both a symbol and an agent for life itself”, and pollen figures in ceremonies of purification and renewal. The Manhattan Project replaced this regime with the technoscientific deployment of nature, first the bomb, then the bee as monitor. The bee's value is “no longer as a life-giving entity but as a toxic being, marking the transformation of the plateau from a wild space of nature to a new kind of mutant ecology” p. 537.

DARPA's controlled biological systems

“Within this ecology, the bee becomes an agent of the national security state.” LANL's environmental surveillance links to the Pentagon's research on “controlled biological systems”. The Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) describes the program on its homepage:

The principal objective of the program is to utilize biological organisms for real-time collection of information in the environment. Applications of interest include controlling the distribution of biological systems for real-time monitoring of individual or populations of organisms (e.g., swarms, hives, dens, schools) to seek out and collect information in the environment (air, land, or water) about agents of harm including chemical or biological weapons and unexploded ordnance. . . . To accomplish this objective, the program will seek to monitor and utilize the sensory signals (e.g., chemical, visual, thermal, acoustic, other) and sensorimotor behavior employed by biological organisms to forage and reproduce in their environment.

DARPA, “Controlled Biosystems” · p. 537 · fn 18

Its research takes “three novel deployments of nature”:

  1. vivisystems: insects (bees and moths) and other animals as “environmental sentinels”, tracking chemical weapons, explosives and radioactive materials;
  2. hybrid biosystems: cyborg bugs and animals for surveillance, which “integrate living and nonliving components for novel device applications”;
  3. biomimetics: machines that mimic living beings, especially insects.

Insects plus nanotechnology promise a remote-controlled cyborg for surveillance “in the literal form of the fly on the wall”. The concept is “an instrumentalization of life using state-of-the-art technology, making it a genealogical descendent of the biological testing programs conducted during aboveground nuclear testing”. The 1950s pig was also a “real-time collection of information” through organisms, but about the exploding bomb rather than production's effects pp. 537–538. Post–Cold War Los Alamos has both vivisystems, “environmental sentinels in the form of tritium-sniffing bees”, and “the biomimetic project of engineering robots with insect-like abilities”: Figure 5 shows a mechanical dragonfly and butterfly designed at LANL to explore the social uses of “mechanical” life forms (Shroyer 1998) p. 538.

People as radiation monitors

“Whereas specific animal forms are being deployed—and reinvented—to shape environmental politics”, “a more subtle aspect of the Manhattan Project has been to transform regional human populations into radiation monitors” p. 538.

  • Activists spent much of the 1990s pushing for environmental impact studies and regulation, “helping to produce a cross-cultural regional dialog” about the laboratory. LANL scientists, Pueblo representatives and the Bureau of Indian Affairs each began testing air, water, soil, plants and animals, both to define risk to Pueblo citizens and to check LANL's science fn 20. Within a year of the 1992 agreements with four Pueblo Nations, LANL's testing jumped: in 1993 it collected 11,500 samples and ran 215,000 tests, up about 40% and 70%.
  • The Pueblos of Jemez, Cochiti, Santa Clara and San Ildefonso train young people as environmental scientists to take over monitoring the laboratory.
  • By the late 1990s LANL scientists, Los Alamos residents, Native Americans, Nuevomexicanos and antinuclear activists all called themselves “environmentalist”, with “deeply felt, if asymmetrical, investments” but radically different understandings of the plateau pp. 538–539.

Unwitting test subjects

New Mexicans were also “unwitting environmental test subjects throughout the Cold War” at two levels: as workers monitored for exposure, and as residents who “(often unwittingly)” took part in the Los Alamos Tissue Analysis Program, begun in the 1950s to track exposures through tissue samples p. 539. In the late 1990s the relatives of 407 people whose tissue was taken at autopsy brought a class action; the multimillion-dollar settlement acknowledged that families had not given informed consent. Their bodies had been placed “in the role of ‘environmental sentinel’”, like the bees. The medical knowledge remains partial: a fourfold excess of thyroid cancer found in Los Alamos in the 1990s might simply reflect intense screening (Athas 1996). But illnesses across the region are attributed to the laboratory, “another aspect of the nuclear reinvention of nature” p. 539.

Claim
A worker's videotape of Area G shows the mutant ecology whole: dangerous labour, racial division, buried evidence, fear of mutation turned into play, and ordinary life beside the waste.
Evidence
The videotape and its owner's commentary; interviews with Los Alamos workers who believe their health was damaged.

The videotape

Interviewing Los Alamos workers who believed their health was damaged on the job, Masco was “told repeatedly” about a videotape of hazardous work at Area G, a possible piece of legal evidence that could show outsiders practices “usually shielded by gates, security, and the power of the nation-state” p. 539. A former Area G worker who did not trust the cumulative radiation-badge readings in his medical file invited Masco to watch it at home. “As I watched, I was confronted with a complex textual record of mutation.”

  1. The official record. LANL staff made the tape to document consolidating space for accumulating waste pp. 539–540.
  2. The accident. “The banality of worker job descriptions is soon ruptured” when a tractor accidentally punctures a partly buried barrel of nuclear waste, and the tape shifts from formal statements to the crew playing to the camera.
  3. The racial split. “The multiracial workforce splits along racial lines, as the white program managers don anticontamination gear to test the drum for radionuclides while the Nuevomexicano and Pueblo workers remain in normal work clothes.” The digging and moving of barrels happens “underneath the deep blue New Mexican sky with a ferocious wind that completely covers workers in dust from the site”. Masco's host says the dust may have contaminated them, and that the radiation monitors at Area G could easily be switched off so exposures went unrecorded p. 540.
  4. Silkwood. Midway, the host says he knows what happened to Karen Silkwood, the Kerr-McGee whistleblower who died mysteriously in a car crash in 1974. Her organs were sent to Los Alamos for the tissue registry and then lost. He says they went into a laboratory refrigerator that failed and was dumped at Area G, “packed full of the damaged organs of U.S. nuclear workers”. Area G becomes “quite literally, a grave, a site where the human evidence of radiation exposures is buried as industrial waste”.
  5. The mirror. Twenty minutes in, in the Area G offices, the camera operator finds the camera's mirror function. For the next 20 minutes he entertains his co-workers by giving them a third eye, merging their foreheads into giant mutant forms, or giving them tails, laughing hysterically. The tape shifts “to a literal discourse of mutation”. The workers Masco spoke to focused on the first half, but the tape records “a surreal form of nuclear play that shows workers not as potential mutants but as present ones—linked by tails, misshaped heads, and multiple eyes” p. 540.
  6. The accordion. The tape cuts to a garage in the northern Rio Grande Valley, where a Nuevomexicano relative of the camera operator stands stiffly, without emotion, playing ranchero music on an accordion. This “eruption of the nonnuclear everyday” is a reminder of the many cultural worlds linked “both formally and informally” to Los Alamos pp. 540–541.

The tape documents the burying of waste on the plateau, “permanently transforming the ecology of that space”. It shows whole communities “now devoted simply to monitoring and working with the nuclear waste produced by the U.S. national security regime”, and “the fears of mutation that permeate workers' psyches” p. 541. These forces are “highly mobile”: the Manhattan Project's regional effects cannot be understood without seeing how “material realities fuse with sociocultural logics and nuclear fear”.

Masco's final paragraph

“A political ecology of the bomb that investigates the interaction between regimes of nature reveals the American nuclear project to have been ecologically transformative and multigenerationally productive: It has reinvented the biosphere as a nuclear space; transformed entire populations of plants, animals, insects, and people into ‘environmental sentinels’; and embedded the logics of mutation with both ecologies and cosmologies.” “The giant cinematic ants of 1954 have, in other words, been replaced now by far more subtle and serious forms of life defined by the ambiguities and dangers of inhabiting specific radioactive spaces within mutant ecologies that now present an ever evolving biosocial, political, and ethnographic terrain” p. 541.

Creager in ten lines

  1. After 1945 the US government became “the manufacturer of a basic laboratory and clinical tool”: reactor-made radioisotopes p. 653.
  2. The idea came from Manhattan Project scientists at an uncertain Clinton Laboratories (“Dogpatch”), with cancer research as the first justification pp. 656–657.
  3. Isotopes Branch (Oak Ridge, Feb 1946, Aebersold). Prices were “out-of-pocket”, excluding the reactor's cost pp. 658–660.
  4. First sale, 2 August 1946, the day after the Atomic Energy Act: 1 mCi of carbon-14 for $367 to a St. Louis cancer hospital p. 662.
  5. Demand was weak in 1946 (306 requests, 125 shipments): “The mindset of scarcity rapidly turned into a culture of promotion” p. 662.
  6. Two classes: tracers (research) and radiation sources (therapy). Diagnostics blurred them and proved “even more revolutionary” than cancer therapy pp. 653–654.
  7. P-32 and I-131 dominated. I-131 cured hyperthyroidism (Mayo 80%) and was the only true fission by-product; the cancer cure stayed a “mirage” pp. 663–669.
  8. C-14 had no clinical use but drove biochemistry: Calvin's photosynthesis cycle, “a new mode of perception” pp. 669–677.
  9. A “founder effect” favoured cyclotron-linked users. The AEC “commodified what had been largely a gift relationship”, creating a subsidised “supply-driven economy” p. 675.
  10. Political, not only military: proof the AEC was “truly a civilian agency”; its dream “that atoms could cure as well as kill” pp. 651, 679.

Traps

  • By-product or not? Most AEC isotopes were made by bombarding targets in the pile; only iodine-131 was a fission by-product (C pp. 653, 669).
  • Two different dollar figures for the same claim. Creager quotes the AEC: 1,000 cyclotrons would cost “well over a hundred million dollars” (p. 674). Krige (Week 4) gives “well over $1 million” for the same comparison Comparison not in Creager.
  • 1951 or 1952? Creager calls the microscope quote “an AEC report from 1951”; fn 108 cites AEC 1952.
  • Which isotopes led? P-32 and I-131 were the most shipped (two-thirds of domestic shipments); C-14 was third. By 1948, P-32, C-14 and I-131 made up two-thirds of reported uses (C).
  • Research first, on paper. The 1946 catalog ranked research above therapy, but medical uses got more priority under the AEC (C p. 661).
  • Colour, not protein. Radiation turned Sonora 64 amber; the protein gain was claimed, toned down, and not atomic (H p. 37).
  • Who moved where. In 1964 Silow went from the FAO to the IAEA and Fried the other way on paper, but the division stayed in Vienna under Fried. Silow returned to the FAO in 1968 (H).
  • Two $1.5 millions. Silow's claim of IAEA misspending, 1962–66 (H p. 31); Canada's potato irradiation (H p. 33).
  • Silow's page counts. 26 (Aug 1966), “30” (Lindquist), 49 (Mar 1967), 38 (Dec 1970).
  • Spellings. Hamblin writes Sigurbjörnsson and Sigurbjörnssen. Creager writes Solley (text) and Soley (fn 73). Jacobs writes Englehardt and Engelhardt, and Furtwangler and Furtwanger. Not in the readings The standard spellings are Tom Engelhardt and Judith Merril.
  • Which film is which. Duck and Cover: FCDA and Archer Films, Bert, Paul, Patty, Tony. Atomic Alert: Encyclopedia Britannica Films, Ted and Susie, the block warden (J).
  • Prompted vs open. Schwebel prompted students about war; Escalona asked an open question (J p. 37).
  • Plutonium's half-life. Masco gives 24,000 years (pp. 522, 536) and 24,500 years (p. 525).

For INT354: the course lens Synthesis, not in the readings

Whose order, whose justice?
Each reading describes an order built by the US nuclear state or its international offshoots, and shows who paid for it. Creager's order is the AEC's. Isotopes proved it a civilian agency, signalled “the beneficence of the U.S. to the free world”, and made the federal government the supplier of biomedicine's tools. Justice sits at the edges: the “founder effect” kept access with cyclotron-rich institutions, foreign shipments were controversial, P-32 did not prolong patients' lives, and secret experiments sought a “lowest permissible dose” (fn 113). Hamblin's order is the UN's. The IAEA, “the servant of the Member States”, shaped poor countries towards “a particular vision of modernity”. Its injustice, in Silow's charge, is that they became “an experimental zone”, paying with their own money for techniques rich countries dropped, while the one dissenter was silenced in ways even IAEA staff called “not very ethical”. Bull (Week 1) notes that aid is attacked as “a means of perpetuating domination” and flows to governments, not people; the IAEA's reply to Silow was that governments had asked. Jacobs's order is civil defense's promise of adult control and “the rapid restoration of social order”. Its injustice is generational: children made responsible for their own survival and tagged for identification, facing a war “of their parents' generation's making”. Masco's order is territorial: sacrifice zones, then preserves and stewardship. Its costs fell on Marshall Islanders, soldiers, every American exposed to 1951–61 fallout, Pueblo and Nuevomexicano workers, families whose relatives' tissue was taken without consent, animals used as “instruments”, and future generations.
Which actors?
C: the AEC and Manhattan Project, Congress, scientists and physicians, industry (Monsanto, Abbott, Tracerlab, New England Nuclear), the American Cancer Society and National Academy. Patients appear only as subjects. H: UN agencies (IAEA, FAO, the ILO Tribunal), their directors and scientists, Global South governments (India, Turkey, Tanzania, Thailand, Pakistan, Argentina, Iran), US agencies (USDA, AEC, PSAC, FDA, Army, JCAE), companies and foundations. Farmers and consumers appear only indirectly: Indian buyers who disliked red wheat, Turkey's “public apprehension”. J: children at the centre, then parents, teachers, the FCDA and film companies, psychologists, science-fiction writers, SDS. Jacobs does not discuss race, class or region among his children Observation not in Jacobs. M: the nuclear complex (Los Alamos, DOE, DOD, DARPA); exposed people (Marshallese, the Lucky Dragon crew, Chernobyl “sufferers”, Pueblo and Nuevomexicano communities, workers, Silkwood); activists; and non-human actors (pigs, fish, the bull Uran, bees, alligators, tumbleweeds, fruit flies).
What makes something “nuclear”?
For Creager, isotopes were “nuclear” because the AEC's reactors made them and its publicity called them atomic by-products. Most were not fission products, and relabelling by firms like New England Nuclear later “obscured the site of origin”. Their nuclearity was a political asset, stressed or hidden as needed. For Hamblin, “modern” meant “atomic”: “the term ‘atomic energy’ began to assume almost magical powers”, and an irradiated wheat became an atomic success even though its gains were not atomic. For Jacobs, the nuclear is the flash that makes every schoolyard ground zero, and a future cancelled in advance. For Masco, nuclearity is ecological and genetic: background radiation is everywhere, and a pig, a bee, a tumbleweed, a wildlife preserve or a worker's organs can all be nuclear. The “nuclear test” has no edges. Compare Hecht (Week 4): nuclearity is “a technopolitical spectrum that shifts in time and space”, and political leaders treat the IAEA as “the ultimate arbiter of global nuclearity”. Its 1959 questionnaire on regional training centres, which Hecht calls a “barometer of nuclearity”, asked about “radiochemical and radioisotope laboratories”.

Links to other INT354 readings

Bull, “Order vs. Justice in International Society” (Wk 1)
The “so-called Third World” seeks justice “even at the price of disorder”. Aid is “assailed … by the doctrine that aid is essentially a means of perpetuating domination and exploitation”, and donors and international organizations transfer resources “not directly to these individuals but to the governments”. Set this beside Hamblin: the IAEA said “technical assistance is requested by Governments”, while Silow said the poorest countries were being misled.
Bensaude-Vincent, Boudia and Sato, “Introduction: shaping the nuclear order” (Wk 2)
“A complementary logic of pacification was at work in the promotion of nuclear applications in medicine and energy.” The US “shared knowledge and isotopes to help improve relationships with other nations” (citing Creager's 2015 book Life Atomic). The IAEA “simultaneously promotes and seeks to control these technologies”, and its promotion “resembles other postwar programs for economic development in the Global South”. This is Creager and Hamblin in two sentences.
Kraft, “Dissenting Scientists in Early Cold War Britain” (Wk 2)
The 1955 Geneva conference would showcase “the ‘flagship’ peaceful nuclear technologies: civic energy and the medical isotope”, and Britain's radioisotope program was widely publicised while Cockcroft “portrayed bright visions of the atomic future”. Kraft's 2006 article on British radioisotopes is the one Creager cites (fns 14, 23), and Creager thanks her. Cockcroft reappears in Hamblin as an IAEA adviser and Consultant Group chair.
Todeschini, “Illegitimate Sufferers” (Wk 3)
The ABCC, “dispatched by the U.S. government in 1946”, turned hibakusha “from victims to guinea pigs”, and official responses “denied the victims both understanding and legitimacy”. Compare Masco's test subjects, the tissue samples taken without consent, and Chernobyl's legally recognised “sufferers”. Creager notes the AEC funded the ABCC (fn 11).
Goldstein and Stawkowski, “James V. Neel and Yuri E. Dubrova” (Wk 3)
The Neel–Dubrova debate: Dubrova's 1996 Chernobyl study and his 2002 study of Kazakh families exposed to Soviet test fallout, which found fallout “doubled the germline mutation rate”, against the “hegemonic” Hiroshima–Nagasaki data. Masco cites both Dubrova studies as evidence of elevated mutation, taking the dissenting side. The article also confirms that the AEC funded ABCC genetics from 1946 to 1997, matching Creager.
Lindee, “Survivors and scientists” (Wk 3)
Both Creager (fn 11) and Masco cite Lindee's 1994 book Suffering Made Real on the ABCC. Her Week 3 article follows its successor, the Radiation Effects Research Foundation, from 1975 to 2014.
Higuchi, Political Fallout, ch. 1 (Wk 3)
The three nuclear powers defined off-site contamination as harmless by stressing “its smallness in quantity compared with the maximum permissible dose and natural background levels”. Their monitoring “did not follow the movement of the settled radionuclides through the food chain and into the human body”, and “the Castle Bravo test conducted in 1954 drastically raised public awareness”. Compare Masco's background radiation as a manufactured norm, Bradley's radioactive fish, and Bravo.
Eisenhower, “Atoms for Peace” speech (Wk 4)
The agency would “apply atomic energy to the needs of agriculture, medicine and other peaceful activities. A special purpose would be to provide abundant electrical energy in the power-starved areas of the world.” Creager's medicine and Hamblin's agriculture are the two halves; Hamblin shows agriculture became the practical offer when power was “a distant possibility”.
Krige, “Atoms for Peace, Scientific Internationalism, and Scientific Intelligence” (Wk 4)
Its section “The Benign Atom in the 1940s: The Radioisotope Program” covers Truman's announcement at a cancer congress in St. Louis (September 1947), exports limited to 28 isotopes of 19 elements, Strauss blocking phosphorus-32 for Helsinki, and Oppenheimer's case for “doing the decent thing”. It cites Creager's 2002 article, and Creager's fn 7 says her study supports Krige.
Roehrlich, “Atoms for Peace” (Inspectors for Peace, ch. 2) and “The Cold War, the developing world” (Wk 4)
The atom promised power “too cheap to meter”, and “would revolutionize medicine and turn deserts into green fields”. The IAEA's creation “played a role in development aid as part of the Cold War competition”. The first bilateral Atoms for Peace agreement was with Turkey, later home of Hamblin's Iskenderun irradiator, and Strauss insisted on Sterling Cole as director-general.
Hecht, “Negotiating Global Nuclearities” and “Nuclear Ontologies” (Wk 4)
In 1959 the IAEA weighed a conference on radioisotopes against one on small and medium power reactors; South Africa pushed the reactors, because anything that raised “the increased consumption of atomic fuel” served its interest. Its delegate, Donald Sole, held that “scientific prestige is unfortunately not built up on programs of technical assistance”. An IAEA questionnaire that Sole helped calibrate, Hecht's “barometer of nuclearity”, counted “radiochemical and radioisotope laboratories”. Read with Hamblin on prestige against development.
Masco, “Life Underground” and “Survival Is Your Business” (Wk 6)
Duck and cover is “the ultimate Cold War posture”, practised by “every American schoolchild … for the forty years of official Cold War” (Life Underground, p. 25). School campaigns were “designed to deploy children to educate their parents” (Survival, p. 374). Survival's fn 16 on troops timed at rifle drills after a blast is Mutant Ecologies' fn 10 (shot Galileo), and Survival's editor's note (p. 393) cites “Mutant Ecologies”.

Retrieval practice

Self-test

Answer out loud or on paper before revealing. If you miss one, reread that section of the summary.

Q1 · Why isotopesCreagerWhy did the AEC value isotope distribution “quite out of proportion to the financial questions involved”?

Smyth (1950): asked about the peacetime uses of atomic energy, the AEC could answer “Isotopes”. They showed it was “more than just a weapons organization”, at home and in foreign relations. Creager: the Commissioners saw isotopes as “a crucial demonstration that the AEC was truly a civilian agency”, and by 1948, with nuclear power hopes fading, it “had little else to hold out” (pp. 650–651).

Q2 · Side result?CreagerWas the isotope program just a by-product of weapons production?

No: “a deliberate commitment, not a mere side result”. Most isotopes were not fission by-products but made by bombardment in the pile. The program made the government “the manufacturer of a basic laboratory and clinical tool” (pp. 652–653).

Q3 · Two classesCreagerDistinguish tracers from radiation sources, and explain how diagnostics blurred the line.

Tracers: tiny amounts following molecules through metabolism (research). Sources: irradiating tissue in place of X-rays or radium (therapy), needing over a thousand times more isotope. Diagnostics used tracer amounts and methods in medicine and proved “even more revolutionary” than cancer therapy (pp. 653–654).

Q4 · ClintonCreagerWhy were Clinton Laboratories' scientists eager to become isotope suppliers?

Their future looked “hopeless”. The 1943 reactor was only a pilot for Hanford and no longer needed for bombs; they had lost their academic contractor (Chicago, July 1945) to Monsanto; and new national labs needed academic backing. A Radioisotope Committee proposed distribution “for general and cancer research” (pp. 656–657).

Q5 · PricingCreagerWhat did scientists win in the argument with Groves over pricing?

Groves wanted the reactor's construction cost built into prices. Scientists warned “you won't sell a microcurie” of carbon-14. Prices were set at “out-of-pocket” cost, “exclusive of pile costs, plant rentals”, so buyers did not pay for the wartime plant (pp. 659–660).

Q6 · Human useCreagerWhat did the Subcommittee on Human Application do, and how did it understand ethics?

From 28 June 1946 it could veto any use of isotopes involving humans, medical or industrial (sulfur-35 in coal mines, yttrium-90 in gasoline for crash tests). Expecting scarcity, it saw ethics “principally in terms of allocation”, which became moot once supply exceeded demand (p. 660).

Q7 · First saleCreagerDescribe the first sale of a reactor isotope.

2 August 1946, the day after Truman signed the Atomic Energy Act. It was staged “in front of the Clinton pile” with full press coverage. Eugene Wigner handed a millicurie of carbon-14, sold for $367, to Cowdry and Simpson of the Barnard Free Skin and Cancer Hospital in St. Louis, Monsanto's home city (p. 662; Fig. 1).

Q8 · PromotionCreagerWhy was 1946 demand low, and what did the AEC do?

306 requests, 125 shipments, under $30,000 in sales. The reasons: lack of trained personnel, lack of detection devices, and research plans not ready. “The mindset of scarcity rapidly turned into a culture of promotion” (p. 662).

Q9 · Iodine-131CreagerWhy was iodine-131 special among the AEC's isotopes?

It was the undeniable clinical success: the standard hyperthyroidism treatment by 1948 (Mayo 80%) and widely used in diagnosis. It was also nearly the only biomedical isotope that was a fission by-product, so the only isotope to fulfil the public's wish that bomb materials could also cure disease (pp. 668–669).

Q10 · CancerCreagerWhat became of the promise that isotopes would cure cancer?

It was not substantiated. P-32 did not prolong leukemia patients' lives “more than a few months” (Rhoads), and few thyroid tumours respond to I-131. Journalists and officials kept up “the mirage” into the 1950s while real uses grew in diagnostics (pp. 654, 666, 668–669).

Q11 · HutchinsonCreagerHow did isotopes change ecology, according to Creager?

Hutchinson traced P-32 through Linsley Pond to study the “metabolism” of a community, since “the community is an organism”. Isotopes made Tansley's 1935 “ecosystem” concrete, but tracing metabolic pathways “kept the organismal conception in play” (p. 667).

Q12 · CalvinCreagerHow did Calvin's group use carbon-14?

They grew Chlorella in a “lollipop” apparatus, fed it ¹⁴CO₂ for seconds to minutes, and separated its contents by two-dimensional paper chromatography read on X-ray film. They found CO₂ becomes phosphoglyceric acid, then sugars: the Calvin cycle. Dyson called the technique “completely revolutionary” (pp. 671–674).

Q13 · Founder effectCreagerWhat are the “founder effect” and the move from gift to commodity?

Scientists already using cyclotron isotopes ordered most from Oak Ridge, so access was not “democratized”. Where cyclotron physicists had shared isotopes locally, the AEC “commodified what had been largely a gift relationship”: “not a true market” but “a supply-driven economy, subsidized by the government to fulfill political as well as scientific agendas” (p. 675).

Q14 · HistoriographyCreagerWhy does Creager call for a reassessment of the AEC?

Histories of physics read the AEC through military patronage and complicity (Forman vs Kevles), which also fits its secret human experiments. But that frame “fails to capture the full range of the AEC's initiatives in biology and medicine, many of which were driven by political concerns rather than military objectives”. The bomb's changes were “initially quantitative, not qualitative” (pp. 678–679).

Q15 · QuestionsHamblinWhat three questions frame Hamblin's article?

Why did so many path-breaking efforts to apply atomic energy to agriculture happen in developing countries? Did the UN encourage atomic agriculture “at the expense of other methods”? Why did the UN “fall so fully in line” with a vision of modernization that reflected US foreign policy? (p. 26)

Q16 · PhilosophiesHamblinWhat were the two competing philosophies of UN development?

Rational planners wanted UN policy guidelines so science and technology served economic development. Others put “science and technology first”, backing local scientists and projects such as atomic agriculture. Silow's charge was that the Joint Division refused an overriding development policy (pp. 26–27).

Q17 · VictoriesHamblinWhat were the IAEA's three victories within the UN?

(1) Dominance over all UN work on atomic energy in agriculture. (2) Total control of the Joint Division by “excluding, suppressing, and containing” Silow. (3) Brushing aside the main critique. The result was a culture that “virtually disallowed skepticism”, giving “the appearance of unanimity and thus of international endorsement” (p. 27).

Q18 · Why agricultureHamblinWhy did agriculture matter so much to the IAEA?

It was “the most sensible way to introduce economically relevant atomic energy” where power reactors were unrealistic. Cole said power was “a distant possibility in most countries”, and agriculture let the IAEA “direct our attack on hunger and disease”. Dropping it would undermine a part of Atoms for Peace “that specifically targeted the developing world” (pp. 28–29, 42).

Q19 · KarlsruheHamblinWhat happened at Karlsruhe in 1960, and what did the FAO's Julén warn?

A joint FAO–IAEA symposium on radiation and seeds, with Swedish barley and mustard and improved Italian wheat; Sparrow called it the “end of an era”. Julén warned that perhaps one mutation in a thousand is useful, and that irradiation should not replace classical breeding (pp. 28–29).

Q20 · Joint DivisionHamblinWhy was the 1964 Joint Division “a grave error” for the FAO?

It stayed in Vienna near Seibersdorf. Fried, now an FAO employee on paper, became director and Silow deputy, “the appearance but not the reality of FAO dominance”. IAEA staff saw the welcome party as “a celebration of our victory”. The FAO's independent voice “would never recover” (p. 30).

Q21 · AppraisalHamblinWhat did Silow's 1966 appraisal claim?

The FAO was being “seriously misled”, and the IAEA had misspent $1.5 million in 1962–66, about half a million from developing countries. Developing countries had asked not to be pushed into unproven techniques, yet the division used “the developing world as an experimental zone for ideas that industrialized countries were not willing to adopt” (p. 31).

Q22 · ConsultantsHamblinWho sat on the Consultant Group, and what did it conclude?

Cockcroft (UK), Lindquist (USA), Gustafsson (Sweden) and Dakshinamurti (India). Its 1966 report fully endorsed the IAEA's plans, citing the world protein crisis, and called Silow's criticisms “unjustified”. Lindquist: “we all feel sorry for the man” (p. 32).

Q23 · White elephantsHamblinGive two of Silow's “white elephants”, with figures.

Tanzania's $4 million plant to sterilize beef, due in 1969 before any US plant. Turkey's Iskenderun grain irradiator, costing five to seven times other methods and handling under 1% of the grain. Canada had already spent $1.5 million on potato irradiation and given up (pp. 33–34).

Q24 · RefugeHamblinWhat did Silow say about Wantage and Seligman, and who later half-agreed?

Britain cut grain irradiation at Wantage. Its supervisor, Seligman, became IAEA deputy director-general and started Seibersdorf, and the project was “reconstituted in virtually identical form here on the international level”. The official historian Fischer later wrote that Seligman and Salvetti “sought scientific refuge in the IAEA” (p. 34).

Q25 · SuppressionHamblinHow did the IAEA answer Silow's 1967 paper based on PSAC's report?

It belittled a PSAC drafter as a Campbell Soup employee, backed the US Army's claim that irradiated food was wholesome, and cited Seaborg and the JCAE. Its 25-page rebuttal praised the USSR on the strength of one paper by Rogachev, and said the IAEA was “the servant of the Member States”. Eklund refused to circulate the paper: “a decisive step to quash dissent” (pp. 35–36).

Q26 · Sharbati SonoraHamblinWhat did radiation do to the Mexican wheat in India, and what did it not do?

Swaminathan's team irradiated Sonora 64, which Indian buyers found “unpalatable” for its reddish colour, and found an amber mutant, renamed Sharbati Sonora. It gave huge yields and the IAEA its success story. But “the protein ‘miracle’ had nothing to do with atomic energy”, and the protein claims were later toned down (p. 37).

Q27 · AresvikHamblinWho was Aresvik, and how was his critique handled?

A Norwegian agricultural economist whose draft book made Silow's critique: developing countries think they need “atomic reactors and institutes for irradiation genetics”, and should not fund “white elephants” that rich countries can afford. Fried “picked nits” and called leaving the research to rich countries “spoon-feeding”. The exchange was hidden from Silow, and Aresvik “seems to have been cowed” (pp. 39–40).

Q28 · ErasureHamblinHow do the IAEA's histories treat Silow, and what is Hamblin's final verdict?

Fischer mentions only “an unhappy and unwilling FAO official”. Sigurbjörnsson omits his name, calls him “our adversary”, and admits tactics that were “not very ethical”. Verdict: the consensus was created by identifying the controversy “with one troublesome individual”. Silow handled it “ineptly”; his enemies handled it “more successfully but perhaps wrongly”; “Development was only a secondary objective at the IAEA” (pp. 41–42).

Q29 · MessagesJacobsWhat did the civil defense materials mean to teach, and what did they actually teach?

Intended: “Survival, therefore, was a choice”, and authorities were in control and would quickly restore order. Implicit, “the exact opposite”: nuclear war was imminent, adults would not be there, the government was unreliable, so children would have to save the world themselves (pp. 26–27).

Q30 · GapJacobsWhat gap in scholarship does Jacobs fill?

Much work examines FCDA efforts aimed at families and at children in schools, “but almost none on efforts aimed at children as individuals” (fn 1: Henriksen, McEnaney, Oakes, Weart, Rose, Brown).

Q31 · CampJacobsHow is Duck and Cover remembered, and what does Jacobs insist?

As “a classic of comedy and camp”, thanks to clips in The Atomic Café (1982). But “in its original release, the film was deadly serious”, part of the response to the Soviet bomb, meant to teach children to survive “by themselves, without adult assistance” (p. 28).

Q32 · BertJacobsWhat is “skewed” about the Bert the Turtle cartoon?

Bert, the figure children should identify with, is drawn “almost as an old man”, with a bow tie, wrinkled neck, civil-defense helmet and an old man's voice. The dynamite-dangling monkey is “a classically mischievous kid”, and the explosion “would seem to annihilate the monkey” (p. 28; fn 14).

Q33 · The drillJacobsGive two contradictions Jacobs finds in the film's drill.

Children are told they are safe crouching by a wall a minute after hearing the bomb can “smash in buildings”. They cover their faces against flying glass while the camera shows their backs exposed (p. 30).

Q34 · On your ownJacobsWhat is the film's “powerfully mixed assurance”?

“Older people will help us like they always do. But there might not be any grown-ups around when the bomb explodes … Then, you're on your own.” It promises survival while teaching that adults will be gone: “a tale of a dangerous present and a dismal future” (p. 31).

Q35 · Dog tagsJacobsWhy were children given dog tags, and what did the tags signify?

From 1951, over 2.5 million went to schoolchildren in San Francisco, Seattle, Philadelphia and New York, chosen over tattooing, marked clothes and fingerprints, to identify children, and their bodies, after an attack. They told children “they were soldiers in the Cold War”, mobilized around “the idea of the children's own deaths” (p. 32).

Q36 · Two filmsJacobsHow does Atomic Alert differ from Duck and Cover?

Atomic Alert (Encyclopedia Britannica Films, 1951) enlists children as partners on a national defense “team” (“You are on that team”), with Ted and Susie asking “What is your job?” and a block warden arriving to say “You've done a good job”. Duck and Cover stresses possible separation from adults. Both stress self-reliance (pp. 32–33).

Q37 · SurveysJacobsCompare Schwebel's and Escalona's surveys and how Jacobs weighs them.

Schwebel interviewed thousands of NY and PA students after the 1961 and 1962 crises with three questions about war; they split on whether it would come but nearly all cared. Escalona asked 311 children aged 10–17 an open question about the world in ten years; 70% (219) raised war unprompted, mostly pessimistically. Schwebel “put the notion of war into the heads” of students, while Escalona let them set their own priorities (pp. 36–37).

Q38 · The BlobJacobsHow does The Blob show the generational shift?

Earlier 1950s films show passive children. In 1958 Steve and Jane are disbelieved by the police sergeant, so they sound horns and the air-raid siren to “wake this town up”, discover that cold defeats the Blob, and save the town. “Duck and Cover had come true—they were on their own” (pp. 38–40).

Q39 · New LeftJacobsHow does Jacobs connect atomic childhood to the New Left?

Raised to expect catastrophe and taught they alone could save themselves, the Boomers founded and led the New Left. SDS's Port Huron Statement (1962) cites “the presence of the Bomb” and says “we may be the last generation in the experiment with living”. They “refused to live in the basement of fear and fatalism”, and protested Vietnam (p. 40).

Q40 · Them!MascoWhat does Masco read in Them! (1954)?

Beneath its anticommunist allegory, a “devastating critique of U.S. nuclear policy”. With Trinity, Americans “entered a postnuclear environment of their own invention”, and the apocalypse is “already here”. The title's joke is that the agents of destruction seem “‘theirs’ rather than ‘ours’”. It is the “urtext” of American mutation culture (pp. 517–520).

Q41 · MutationMascoDefine mutation, give its three outcomes, and say why Masco prefers it to hybridity.

A mutation occurs “when the ionization of an atom changes the genetic coding of a cell, producing a new reproductive outcome”. Outcomes: evolution (improvement), injury, or genetic noise. Hybridity looks to parents and the present, and a hybrid is infertile, “a form of generational stasis”. Mutation tracks change across generations (p. 522).

Q42 · BackgroundMascoWhat is background radiation, and why does Masco call it a norm?

The average yearly dose used as the baseline for all exposure standards: 360 millirems, 300 natural and 60 from industry, including nuclear medicine, power and weapons testing. Setting such a standard establishes “a new definition of the ‘natural’” with the nuclear complex's past effects built in (p. 520).

Q43 · Seven classesMascoName four of Makhijani and Schwartz's seven exposed classes.

Uranium and weapons workers; troops at atmospheric tests; people near weapons sites; human experiment subjects; military weapons handlers; residents of Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945; “the world inhabitants for centuries to come” (p. 521).

Q44 · BravoMascoWhat happened at Bravo, and why does it matter for Them!?

In March 1954, three months before the film, the largest Cold War thermonuclear test ran at 2.5 times its expected yield (15 Mt). It contaminated 50,000 square miles and exposed 223 Marshallese and the 23-man Lucky Dragon crew. News of their sickness taught Americans about fallout, turning the bomb “from an exclusively military project to a global environmental threat” (p. 519).

Q45 · Fish and bullMascoContrast the Crossroads fish with the Chernobyl bull.

Bradley's radio-autographs of radioactive Bikini fish (1946) show “radioactive death”, contamination moving through the food chain. The bull Uran, grazing in the Chernobyl zone in the 1990s and bred by radioecologists, was valued as a “survival”, adapted to radiation: “neither pure nor hybrid but alive and potentially mutant” (pp. 524–526).

Q46 · PlumbbobMascoWhat happened to the pigs at Operation Plumbbob, and what is Masco's verdict?

At Shot Wilson, only 2 of 135 caged pigs lived 30 days. At Shot Priscilla, 78 shaved pigs tested fabrics and burn creams, and 710 were irradiated or given shrapnel wounds, then used to practise surgery. Nearly all 845 died, a result said to “be applied to man”. Verdict: “Nuclear trauma is not avoided here—indeed, it is instrumentally and methodically pursued” (pp. 527–528).

Q47 · PreservesMascoWhat contradiction lies in the DOE's wildlife preserves? Give one example.

They claim “purity” on sacrificed, contaminated land, “replacing nuclear weapons systems with biodiversity as the security object of the nuclear state”. Savannah River's reserve boasted healthy alligators and bass that are “unusually radioactive”, next to 34 million gallons of high-level waste (pp. 530–532).

Q48 · HanfordMascoHow does Hanford expose the preserve logic?

The preserves assume contamination stays behind fences for millennia. At Hanford, mulberries take up strontium-90, and Russian thistle roots 20 feet deep, then blows away as radioactive tumbleweed, chased by pitchfork crews. In 1998 fruit flies spread contamination, costing $2.5 million to clean up (pp. 532–533).

Q49 · StewardshipMascoWhat is long-term stewardship, and why does Masco call it utopian?

The DOE's plan to manage 109 sites too contaminated to clean, through surveillance and technologies not yet invented, at $100 million a year with budgets only to 2070. It inverts governmentality, protecting citizens from the state's own waste. It is “utopian” because it imagines perfect management for millennia “despite the prior 50 years of environmental neglect” (pp. 534–535).

Q50 · Area GMascoGive Area G's timeline and the assessment's two key assumptions.

Waste site since 1957, with few records to 1971 and poor ones to 1988; full by 2044; institutional control 2047–2146; “industrial use” 2147–3046; risk judged over 1,000 years against plutonium's 24,000. It assumes “current natural conditions will prevail” and “a government entity will maintain the site” for a millennium, though tritium is already leaking (p. 536).

Q51 · BeesMascoHow has the honeybee been “militarized”?

Since the late 1970s LANL has used bees, which are sensitive to tritium, to monitor Area G. In Pueblo cosmology the bee is “a symbol and an agent for life itself”; now it is “a toxic being”. DARPA's controlled biological systems extend this with vivisystems, cyborg hybrid biosystems and biomimetic robots such as LANL's mechanical dragonfly (pp. 536–538).

Q52 · VideotapeMascoWhat does the Area G videotape show?

A tractor punctures a waste barrel. White managers suit up while Nuevomexicano and Pueblo workers stay in work clothes in the dust. The worker describes Silkwood's lost organs dumped at Area G. Then come twenty minutes of mirror effects giving workers tails and third eyes, “not as potential mutants but as present ones”, and an accordion in a garage at the end (pp. 539–541).

Q53 · PromotersCreager · HamblinHow do Creager and Hamblin each show a “peaceful atom” serving its promoter?

Creager: isotopes had real scientific and medical value, but the AEC promoted them because they proved it “truly a civilian agency” and it “could say so much” about them. Hamblin: the IAEA promoted atomic agriculture because promotion was its “raison d'être”, making development “only a secondary objective”. Creager is more positive about the results; Hamblin is sharper about the harm to developing countries.

Q54 · KrigeCreager · Wk 4How does Krige's radioisotope section (Week 4) relate to Creager, and which number differs?

Krige covers the foreign side: Truman's 1947 St. Louis announcement, exports limited to 28 isotopes, Strauss blocking P-32 for Helsinki. He cites Creager's 2002 article, and Creager's fn 7 supports Krige's view of science as foreign policy. For the 1,000-cyclotron comparison, Krige writes “well over $1 million”; Creager quotes the AEC's “well over a hundred million dollars”.

Q55 · DubrovaMasco · Wk 3How does the Neel–Dubrova debate (Goldstein and Stawkowski, Week 3) bear on Masco?

Masco cites Dubrova's studies of Chernobyl (1996) and of Kazakh families exposed to test fallout (2002) as evidence of elevated mutation in exposed people's children. Goldstein and Stawkowski show these findings challenged the “hegemonic” Hiroshima–Nagasaki data defended by Neel. Masco takes the dissenting side.

Q56 · Duck and coverJacobs · Wk 6What does Week 6's Masco add to Jacobs on duck and cover?

Masco calls the drill “the ultimate Cold War posture”, practised by every schoolchild “for the forty years of official Cold War” (Life Underground, p. 25). School campaigns aimed “to deploy children to educate their parents” (Survival, p. 374). Jacobs adds the children's side: the drill taught them adults could not protect them.
