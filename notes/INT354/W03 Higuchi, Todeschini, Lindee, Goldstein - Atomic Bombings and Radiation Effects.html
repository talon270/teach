1

Higuchi · 2020 · “A State of Emergency”

From 1945 to 1953 the United States, the Soviet Union and Britain all treated fallout outside their test sites as an “emergency”. They guarded the public only against large, quick doses (evacuation at 75 R, 25 R or 200 R) and measured only near the site during a test. Meanwhile each secretly tracked fallout round the world as a harmless tracer: to catch the enemy's tests and, for the US (Project Gabriel, Project Sunshine), to model a world nuclear war. This “compartmentalized production of knowledge” deepened ignorance of low-dose harm to ranchers, Kazakh villagers and Aboriginal Australians. The powers' “definitional power” cracked after Castle Bravo in 1954.

2

Todeschini · 1999 · “Illegitimate Sufferers”

US and Japanese scientists and officials made biomedicine “a hegemonic force” in defining hibakusha, and victims' own experience was “repressed and denied”. Her evidence is women survivors' testimony (Hayashi Kyōko, the Osaka and Yamashita groups) and her interviews of 1991–93. She traces occupation denial and the Press Code; pollution beliefs that made women “doubly dangerous”; the ABCC's no-treatment policy (“guinea pigs”) and its denial of genetic effects; and relief laws that medicalised suffering, coined “A-bomb neurosis” and turned the bomb into “an event without actors”, so the state never had to pay compensation.

3

Lindee · 2016 · “Survivors and scientists”

The RERF, the ABCC's successor from 1975, is the “gold standard” of radiation risk and is also distrusted. She makes three claims. Dosimetry is “a fundamentally humanistic exercise”: it reenacts the chaos through survivor interviews, “phantom” Japanese houses bombed in Nevada, and redrawn maps. The RERF made citizenship a scientific credential, the reverse of Petryna's biological citizenship. And military and civilian science cannot be separated: by the 1970s nuclear power justified the survivor study, and after Fukushima, Japan's “third atomic bombing”, survivors turned against nuclear energy.

4

Goldstein & Stawkowski · 2015 · Neel vs Dubrova

The Hiroshima–Nagasaki data stay “hegemonic” on the genetic effects of low doses partly because Lysenkoism had stigmatised Soviet genetics. James Neel, founder of the ABCC genetics study, found no demonstrable effect in survivors' children and defended that finding for life. He attacked Dubinin and Altukhov (1979) and then Yuri Dubrova, whose 1996 Nature study found doubled minisatellite mutation rates in Chernobyl children. They disagreed on four issues: junk DNA, mice, the doubling dose and control groups. The elevation of US data and the denigration of Soviet data “co-configured” today's knowledge, and the Japan data still decide courtroom cases.

How the four readings connect

Read this before the summaries

1 · Secrecy makes ignorance

All four show that what was not measured or shared shaped “the facts”. Higuchi: fallout knowledge split into public reassurance and secret intelligence, which “systematically sustained and deepened ignorance” Higuchi p. 18. Todeschini: the US Press Code (to 1952) kept hibakusha from learning about their exposure, and Japanese doctors' data went to the US as “top secret” Todeschini p. 71. Lindee: the bombs' radiation output was “a state secret even for a state-supported scientific project”, and the Kyoto team's measurements were confiscated and never found Lindee p. 194. Goldstein and Stawkowski: critics of the Hiroshima data “took much longer to emerge within the framework of secrecy” G&S p. 73.

2 · One institution, three verdicts

The ABCC/RERF sits at the centre of three readings. Todeschini (1999) writes as its critic: it examined but never treated, denied genetic effects and made victims feel like “guinea pigs”. Lindee (2016) writes its institutional history: a “shotgun” wedding of the NAS and AEC, the 1975 rebirth as the RERF, and its rise as a global adviser from Chernobyl to Fukushima. Goldstein and Stawkowski (2015) study its geneticist James Neel. Todeschini and Goldstein both lean on Lindee's earlier book Suffering Made Real (1994), especially on how the genetics study chose what “counted” as a mutation. Higuchi adds a link of his own: Project Sunshine got human bones from Japan “through its ties with the survey of the atomic bomb survivors” Higuchi p. 30.

3 · The fight over low doses

Each reading turns on doses too small to see. In 1953 the USAEC said the doubling dose was “1,600 times higher” than lifetime fallout exposure near Nevada Higuchi p. 25. Neel held the doubling dose at about 2.0 Sv and called it conservative G&S p. 88. Lindee notes that the linear no-threshold model rests “not in robust data, but rather in logic” Lindee p. 186. Todeschini: “permissible” doses are “much debated” p. 82. Victims (hibakusha, downwinders, Chernobyl families) count harm more widely than the experts do.

4 · Bombs and reactors cannot be kept apart

Lindee's central point: the industry tried to separate nuclear war from nuclear power, but the RERF's own data (the basis of worker standards) and survivor activism after Fukushima joined them. Goldstein and Stawkowski show the Japan data used to defend Sellafield and Hanford, and Atoms for Peace (1953) creating a need to show that radiation risks were “acceptable”. Todeschini ends with nuclear waste at La Hague as an “eternal impurity”. Higuchi shows test fallout serving as a model of nuclear war (Project Sunshine).

5 · Whose order, whose justice?

The nuclear order was bought with other people's bodies: ranchers near Trinity, St. George downwinders, the Kazakh steppe, the Anangu and Yankunytjatjara under the “black mist” (Higuchi); hibakusha told to “endure” as part of a “general sacrifice” (Todeschini); Minamisoma joining “a different kind of ‘nuclear club’” defined by contamination (Lindee); Hanford downwinders whose eyeless sheep did not count in court (Goldstein and Stawkowski). Order (deterrence, energy, reconstruction) repeatedly outranked justice (treatment, compensation, recognition), which is Bull's week-1 dilemma in concrete form Link from the course lens.

6 · Links to other weeks

Week 4: Goldstein and Stawkowski name Eisenhower's 1953 Atoms for Peace as the moment the US needed radiation risks to look “acceptable” p. 76. Higuchi's fallout intelligence networks sit beside Krige's thesis that internationalism and intelligence were “two sides of the same coin”. Lindee's “nuclear club” of contamination and Goldstein's contest over what “counts” as a mutation both echo Hecht's nuclearity, where being “nuclear” is decided, not given. Reading 1 · History · Stanford University Press, 2020 · Chapter 1 · pp. 16–40

A State of Emergency The Origins of Radiation Protection in Nuclear Weapons Testing, 1945–1953. Toshihiro Higuchi, Political Fallout: Nuclear Weapons Testing and the Making of a Global Environmental Crisis

Chicago citationHiguchi, Toshihiro. “A State of Emergency: The Origins of Radiation Protection in Nuclear Weapons Testing, 1945–1953.” In Political Fallout: Nuclear Weapons Testing and the Making of a Global Environmental Crisis, 16–40. Stanford, CA: Stanford University Press, 2020.

The chapter in one breath

Between Trinity (1945) and 1953 the US, the USSR and Britain all said, as a statement of fact, that fallout far from their test sites was harmless. Higuchi asks how they “knew”. Each built radiation protection on the same principle. Pressed for weapons and afraid of one bad accident, they defined off-site exposure as an “emergency”, acted only against large doses delivered fast, and monitored only near the site during the test. The same logic made them value distant fallout as a harmless, useful tracer. They tracked it secretly round the world to detect each other's tests, and the US also used it to estimate the world-wide effects of the nuclear war it was ready to fight (Project Gabriel, then Project Sunshine). “This compartmentalized production of knowledge systematically sustained and deepened ignorance about the widespread and long-term effects of radioactive debris beyond the test-site regions.” The places called empty were not: ranches near Trinity, Kazakh and settler villages round Semipalatinsk, pearl divers and Aboriginal people near Monte Bello, Emu and Maralinga.

Opening: the fogged X-ray film Unheaded introduction

In fall 1945 Eastman Kodak inspectors in Rochester, New York, found spots on X-ray film. Natural radiation (granite, radium water) sometimes did that, but the culprit was cerium-141, a fission product “previously believed not to exist naturally”. Dust from the secret Trinity test in New Mexico had blown east, been rained out over the Midwest into the Wabash River, and been used by a paper mill in Vincennes, Indiana, to make the strawboard that packed the film p. 16. The fogged radiographs “captured a historic moment in the Anthropocene”: one human event left “enduring environmental footprints” on parts of or the whole world almost at once.

Tests and yields “drastically increased” (Figure 2, a map of the major atmospheric test sites to 1963: Nevada, New Mexico, Bikini, Enewetak, Johnston Island, Christmas Island, Semipalatinsk, Novaya Zemlya, Monte Bello, Emu, Maralinga) pp. 17–18. Reports of abnormal radioactivity far away were always dismissed. US officials: distant radioactivity “definitely is not dangerous to humans, animals, or plants”. Churchill, on Britain's first test at Monte Bello: care was taken “to wait for southerly winds so as to avoid the possibility of any significant concentration of radio-active particles spreading over the Australian mainland”. The Soviets rarely spoke of tests and never of safety p. 17.

Higuchi's question and thesis

The denials were no surprise given national security, but “their denials always took the form of a factual statement, not a political opinion. How, then, did they ‘know’ that the nuclear debris of their weapons tests posed no danger whatsoever?” p. 17

The three programmes differed in detail but their “organizing principles were strikingly similar”. Afraid of an accident and pressed for speed, all three defined the exposure situation as an “emergency”, concentrating on “the well-known dangers of instantaneous exposure to large amounts of radiation”. The same logic led them to track global fallout secretly as “a supposedly harmless and useful tracer” for security: to uncover foreign tests and, for the US, to estimate the effects of nuclear war against the USSR. “This compartmentalized production of knowledge systematically sustained and deepened ignorance about the widespread and long-term effects of radioactive debris beyond the test-site regions.” pp. 17–18

§1pp. 18–31

United States

Trinity: “What about the radioactivity?” · pp. 18–21

In April 1945 the Manhattan Project asked physical chemists Joseph O. Hirschfelder and John L. Magee to list hazards to people near the test site. Hirschfelder suddenly realised something was missing and ran to Magee shouting “What about the radioactivity?” They searched industrial smoke, airplane trails and chemical warfare for clues, but a nuclear blast “defied a simple extrapolation”. A dry run on 7 May (100 tons of dynamite with fission products on a tower) threw material so high that little fell near ground zero. They warned of “a definite danger of dust … falling on towns near Trinity and necessitating their evacuation” pp. 18–19.

Context: Einstein's letter to Roosevelt (August 1939) started US atomic research as a hedge against Germany. Britain's project, “Tube Alloys”, was suspended in 1943 and Britain joined the Manhattan Project “as a junior partner”. Germany surrendered the day after the mock test; Truman turned to Japan and hoped a demonstration would make the Soviets “more reasonable”. Henry Stimson: “The bomb as a merely probable weapon had seemed a weak reed on which to rely, but the bomb as a colossal reality was very different.” A successful test was “crucial for a diplomatic success” p. 19.

Kenneth T. Bainbridge, test manager, chose a remote corner of the Great Basin near Los Alamos. “Isolation, however, was an illusion.” Settlements within ten miles were vacated, but early-1950s maps show 63 ranches, 3 camps and 14 small towns within thirty miles. Stafford Warren, chief medical adviser, saw human traces from the air: “there were a lot of people living in the desert that weren't on the map” p. 19.

Two decisions “set the pattern for U.S. off-site fallout policy for years to come” p. 20:

  1. No protective action short of clearly injurious levels. Inside the site the project used the 0.1 R daily limit of the nongovernmental Advisory Committee on X-ray and Radium Protection (ACXRP). For civilians it set a trigger for mass evacuation of a projected 75 R in two weeks of external gamma, about fifty times the daily limit. Civilians were assumed at risk only if a plume blew straight at them. Louis H. Hempelmann and James F. Nolan (Los Alamos Health Group) told Bainbridge the worst predicted case, 68 R in two weeks, “would probably not even cause radiation sickness”; “Fatalities probably would not result unless ten or more times this dose were delivered.”
  2. Narrow monitoring. Bainbridge hurriedly built “then the world's largest radiation surveillance network”: posts to 55 miles, vehicles to 150 miles downwind, aircraft tracking the plume. But ground measurements stayed near the site and only during the operation, and mostly measured external radiation from a passing plume, “leaving out the whole question of internal exposure through the inhalation and ingestion of radioactive materials”. High reference levels plus narrow monitoring “combined to systematically foster ignorance”.

16 July 1945, 5:29:45 a.m.: part of the cloud went toward people. Towns on highway 380 reported 3.3 R per hour, enough in the worst case for 90 percent of the evacuation benchmark; the rate soon fell. The plume passed over ranches in a canyon about twenty miles northeast; residents probably got up to 47 R in two weeks. Seeing no illness, officials assumed no measurable impact. Hempelmann later: “a few people were probably overexposed, but they couldn't prove it and we couldn't prove it. So we just assumed we got away with it.” p. 21

Fallout as intelligence · pp. 21–22

Unconcern about low doses did not mean ignorance of spread: the emergency mindset made nuclear dust valuable for detecting foreign explosions, with the Soviet Union already the imagined enemy. Magee and Anthony L. Turkevich guessed fine particles might circle the globe, inspired by the 1883 Krakatoa eruption. Turkevich: “many of us had read the [British] Royal Society report on the Krakatoa volcanic explosion”. Army Air Force planes with Geiger counters found slightly raised radioactivity on the West Coast about three weeks after Trinity; a report said dust sampling could uncover explosions “almost anywhere with proper meteorological conditions” pp. 21–22.

As relations with Moscow worsened, the gap between health monitoring and intelligence monitoring widened. At Operation Sandstone (Enewetak, 1948, a year after Truman's aid to Greece and Turkey), safety officers checked only inside the “Danger Area” and a few atolls, while the military secretly sampled dust at 26 US sites and 41 overseas, with planes flying from the Arctic Circle to the Equator and from Manila to Tripoli. The US Air Force's Atomic Energy Detection System ringed Soviet airspace just before the first Soviet test (August 1949). Its alert level, first 100 then 50 counts per minute, was so near background that there were 111 false alarms before it caught the Soviet test, a stark contrast to the high public exposure limits p. 22.

Nevada, Kodak and HASL · pp. 22–24

The “myth of isolation” collapsed again when the US Atomic Energy Commission (USAEC) opened the Nevada test ground in 1951. Caution rose: the benchmark fell from 75 R in two weeks to 25 R in thirty days, and monitoring reached a 200-mile radius. But the philosophy stayed. After the Soviet test Truman ordered a crash H-bomb programme; the Korean War (June 1950) made superiority urgent. Thomas L. Shipman (Los Alamos Health Division), noting “the somewhat delicate public-relations aspect”, argued that “no drastic action to disturb the public should be taken unless it is clearly felt that such action is essential to protect local residents from almost certain damage” pp. 22–23.

The first public-relations problem was not panic but interference with X-ray film and Geiger counters nationwide, the same sensitivity that made long-range detection possible. It hurt uranium prospecting and low-level research. When Nevada tests began in January 1951, Kodak's Rochester air filters caught particles, and Kodak's president wrote to the USAEC that the company might sue the federal government p. 23.

The troubleshooter: Merril Eisenbud, director of the Health and Safety Laboratory (HASL), New York. Born 1915, eldest son of a Russian-Jewish immigrant physician; an industrial safety engineer at Liberty Mutual who moved to occupational disease (“the risks of many occupational diseases depended on how much of a particular substance was inhaled”). Known as a “dustologist”, recruited by the USAEC in 1947 for what became HASL, “an extremely useful trouble-shooting arm of the commission” pp. 23–24. He planned routine continent-wide dust collection to warn film makers. The Nevada safety office refused help: “there can be no significant fall-out which could possibly produce a health hazard of any sort” beyond its 200-mile zone. So he improvised with USAEC sites, universities, the Weather Bureau and Canada; by 1952 about 100 stations used gummed paper round the clock. “The trouble of the photographic industry, not any concern about public health, prompted the U.S. government to install the nation's first permanent radiological surveillance across North America.” p. 24

Fallout reaches the public · pp. 24–26

  • February 1951: radioactivity in snow over the Eastern US and Canada.
  • Shot Easy, 7 May 1952: cloud over Salt Lake City, no more than 10 milliR per hour.
  • Shot Harry, 19 May 1953: wind shift toward St. George, Utah, cumulative exposures reportedly up to 4.2 R.
  • An earlier plume met a storm and deposited radioactivity from Chicago to Quebec City. Herbert M. Clark (Rensselaer Polytechnic, Troy NY) estimated 55 milliR in ten weeks, more than twice natural background p. 25.

The USAEC now had to justify its safety claims publicly, on two grounds p. 25:

  1. Maximum permissible dose. It used the recommendations of the National Committee on Radiation Protection (NCRP), the ACXRP's successor, for work inside the site, combining the weekly 0.3 R over a whole test series (ten weeks, later thirteen). January 1953 public material: “[n]one of the measurements of fall-out radioactivity outside the Nevada Proving Ground has exceeded the recommended maximum of 3 roentgens per ten weeks.” An occupational standard was applied to the public.
  2. Detectability. The USAEC admitted there might be no safe dose for genetic damage, but said the doubling dose (dose to double the natural mutation rate) was “1,600 times higher than the lifetime exposure level of 50 milliroentgens noted in communities surrounding the test site”. Too small to cause a detectable rise in mutations.
Dissent · Lyle B. Borst, May 1953

Physicist at the University of Utah, Manhattan Project veteran, first director of Brookhaven (to 1951). He wrote to USAEC chairman Gordon Dean: levels were below the permissible dose, but he challenged “the philosophy implied—that as long as radiation levels do not exceed calculated tolerance values, the situation is safe”. Fallout reached pregnant women and children, not healthy adult workers; every measure should minimise off-site exposure p. 26.

Borst was “alone in dissent”. Most scientists agreed with the USAEC; Clark compared his 55 milliR with an “accepted safe level” of 3,000 milliR without noting the different exposure situations. John C. Bugher (USAEC Division of Biology and Medicine): “Atomic tests are conducted in the interests of national welfare, a circumstance which certainly warrants deviation from normal laboratory practices”. Norris E. Bradbury (Los Alamos director), complaining to a University of Utah vice president: “an atomic war would be fantastically worse—and that perhaps the AEC's efforts at Nevada make the likelihood of such a war considerably less!” p. 26

Project Gabriel and Project Sunshine · pp. 26–31

“Indeed, the logic of national security further drove bifurcation”: while denying danger, the USAEC began a secret worldwide radiostrontium survey in 1953. Historian Jacob Darwin Hamblin has shown the US and NATO studying the catastrophic potential of nuclear war, including a world so contaminated that most survivors might die of delayed effects pp. 26–27.

  • Project Gabriel (1949), physicist Nicholas M. Smith Jr.: the biological limits of nuclear war. His model centred on strontium-90, “highly carcinogenic”, which mimics calcium and builds up in bones and teeth. With no measurements, uncertainty was huge. November 1951: the probable limit was about 100,000 standard (20 kt) bombs, but the true number might be “100 [times] too low—or 10 times too high”. The USAEC General Advisory Committee (GAC) asked RAND (systems analysis) to check; RAND hired Willard F. Libby p. 27.
  • Libby: born 1908, a farmer's son north of San Francisco, 6 ft 3 in, “Wild Bill”; Berkeley; showed “an unusual knack as a toolmaker” for measuring weak radiation and claimed to build the first US Geiger counter as a senior student, later tuning it to detect rare earths; worked out the natural tritium cycle (1946); invented radiocarbon dating (Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 1960) p. 27. He crossed disciplines, saw the world as one system (“the entire ocean mass, the entire biosphere, the entire atmosphere, as though it were in my test tube”), and had wood collected worldwide through museums. His political conservatism stood out: he backed the H-bomb when most Chicago colleagues, Fermi included, opposed it. Since Gabriel was meant to find a threshold below which Washington could justify using nuclear weapons, Libby's pronuclear view “guaranteed that his review would not be a political embarrassment for RAND and the nuclear security state” p. 28.
  • July 1953, a three-day expert conference. Past Gabriel studies lacked a “test”. Libby's idea: the world was already contaminated. RAND: “Today we are afforded the opportunity of doing a radioactive-tracer chemistry experiment on a world-wide scale.” Almost 10 kg of Sr-90 released in under a decade was probably detectable everywhere. When few cared about fallout as a health issue, Libby found it “a supposedly harmless tool of research to predict the worldwide impacts of a nuclear war” p. 28.
  • Global design: “anything less than a worldwide assay … will be unsatisfactory”. It mirrored a “twisted logic”, the US as caretaker of the human race because it could wipe out countless bystanders. Libby: “it wasn't whether you killed Americans; it is whether you kill people, and I think that we must assay the world, not our own backyard”; “[A]fter all aren't we really concerned about whether the negros [sic] in South Africa are radioactive?” Higuchi names this nuclear paternalism p. 29.

Project Sunshine rode on US global reach. Hard power: Lyle E. Alexander (US Department of Agriculture) toured Western Europe, the Middle East and North Africa in 1954 and 1955, taking soil from embassy and consulate yards; in France the embassy restaurant found a one-year-old lamb and an attaché's wife bought milk. From 1956 the Naval Research Laboratory measured surface air along the 80th meridian west, eventually 22 stations from Greenland to Antarctica; Navy ships sampled Pacific seawater p. 29. Soft power: the USAEC used the Rockefeller Foundation's mission hospitals in India, South America and South Africa to get human bones; the Armed Forces Institute of Pathology got bones from Japan through the atomic bomb survivor survey. J. Laurence Kulp (Columbia geochemist, chief bone analyst) asked an Austrian missionary doctor in South India for monthly shipments of bones for “this urgent humanitarian scientific study”. By April 1956: 26 individuals in 19 countries p. 30.

It was not rigorous. September 1953: Libby tended to “rush into the sampling”. Well funded in the name of security, it became profitable: “The slogan ‘This is important for Sunshine’ has replaced ‘Open Sesame’ for people who want funds and authorization to study everything from Northern Lights to penguin feathers, with ocean bottoms in between.” Eisenbud (May 1955) called high accuracy “useless” given nuclear war's imponderables and costs; a reporter: the philosophical evaluation “proved, as always, so troublesome that we cheerfully abandoned it in favor of the customary intuition” pp. 30–31. These gaps would “loom large” when Sunshine was later repurposed as “the most important research to convince the world of the safety of nuclear weapons testing” p. 31.

§2pp. 31–35

Soviet Union

April 1947 instructions: an uninhabited region with no farmland within 125 miles, far from borders but near railways. The Semipalatinsk Test Site, south of the Irtysh River in today's northeastern Kazakhstan, seemed to fit: “Along the way there were neither houses nor trees … Even birds here were fairly rare.” “The Kazakh steppe, however, was anything but a void.” Russian, German, Ukrainian and Caucasian peasants had settled since late tsarist times; Kazakh nomads were forced into collective farms in the early 1930s; factories grew in the war. A thousand families were removed, but both banks of the Irtysh held villages and camps; a 1958 survey found 22 population centres downwind, including the industrial cities of Semipalatinsk and Ust'-Kamenogorsk p. 31.

The Soviet programme began in 1942 but stayed modest. At Potsdam (July 1945) Stalin feigned ignorance when Truman told him of the test, but concluded the Americans meant to intimidate him. After Hiroshima and Nagasaki he told Igor V. Kurchatov: “Hiroshima has shaken the whole world. The balance has been disrupted. Give us a bomb—it will turn a great danger away from us.” The project ran full steam under secret police chief Lavrentii Beria pp. 31–32.

The USSR did not formally join the International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP) until 1956 but followed it. The Third Chief Directorate of the Health Ministry (1947) used the 0.1 R daily limit, plus an allowance up to 100 R for urgent tasks. That preparedness did not reach beyond the site. At the first test, RDS-1 (August 1949), off-site surveillance was “almost nonexistent”: a few mobile units northeast of the site and between Semipalatinsk and Site “M” (today's Kurchatov, forty miles north), and aircraft tracking clouds only to just under two hundred miles p. 32.

Kurchatov ordered an investigation when high readings came in. Monitors found a band of contamination 750 miles long and 187.5 miles wide. Worst hit was the village of Dolon, 75 miles away: an estimated 200 R external exposure p. 32. Officials cared only about acute injury. Avetik I. Burnazian (director of the Third Chief Directorate) visited a hospital, found no overexposure and, at Kurchatov's insistence, telegraphed Moscow that the test hurt no one. Beria to Stalin: “radioactive fallout settling farther than 400 km does not represent a serious danger for people and animals.” p. 33

What alarmed the Kremlin was that the US had caught the debris. On 23 September 1949 Truman announced “evidence” of a Soviet explosion. Belatedly seeing fallout's intelligence value, Moscow set up a long-range detection system: Isaak K. Kikoin (Kurchatov's LIPAN laboratory) led the task group; David A. Frank-Kamenetskii (LIPAN) and Andrei D. Sakharov (VNIIEF) designed sampling; Boris V. Kurchatov, Igor's younger brother, led radiochemical analysis with the Radium Institute in Leningrad p. 33. As in the US, intelligence dwarfed safety: cotton-wrapped tablets at 120 stations run by the State Meteorological Service and Agriculture Ministry; four military intelligence (GRU) outposts in the Far East at Ussuriisk, Sakhalin, Kamchatka and Dalian (moved to eastern Turkmenistan when Soviet forces left Manchuria in May 1955). In January 1954 Yulii B. Khariton activated the net before the US Operation Castle: 20 balloons from six stations on the western borders and flights between Odessa and Leningrad and between Beijing and Guangdong. The debris helped Soviet scientists learn “some of the key design features of a multimegaton thermonuclear weapon.” pp. 33–34

Unlike the US, the USSR kept testing inside its continent, relying on “erratic weather”. In 1951 planners forecast fallout for each shot so as not to add debris to areas already hit. “The trade-off was a familiar one: choosing relatively low but widespread contamination over the well-known dangers of acute exposure.” p. 34

RDS-6, the first Soviet fusion-boosted bomb. In May 1953 scientists realised it would scatter lethal fallout. After consulting a Russian translation of a 1950 US civil defense manual, they called for evacuating everyone where external exposure might exceed 200 R, just below the manual's level for injury and possible death. The military agreed but warned of casualties in a rushed evacuation. Marshal Aleksandr Vasilevskii reassured Sakharov: “Army maneuvers always result in casualties—twenty or thirty deaths can be considered normal. And your tests are far more vital for the country, and its defense.” Sakharov: “Catching a glimpse of myself in a mirror, I was struck by the change—I looked old and gray.” pp. 34–35

The evacuation was unfinished when RDS-6 fired. Physicians found no radiation sickness, and evacuees were sent back after two weeks while external rates were still 15–37 milliR per hour, about a thousand times normal. Sakharov later said the incident opened his eyes to the danger to humanity, but the scientists, “still operating under the notion of emergency”, went back to prepare more tests p. 35.

§3pp. 35–39

Great Britain

Last of the three, Britain “presumably enjoyed the benefit of general knowledge about radioactive fallout”. William Penny [Penney], head of the British delegation to the Manhattan Project, watched Trinity and the 1946 Pacific tests; W. G. Marley, chief radiation safety officer at the Atomic Energy Research Establishment (AERE), Harwell, saw Trinity as an explosives specialist. Cooperation ended when Congress banned sharing weapons information in 1946 Not in Higuchi (the McMahon Act). Attlee's Labour government wanted a deterrent and Great Power status. Ernest Bevin: Britain “could not afford to acquiesce in an American monopoly of this new development.” A secret cabinet decision (January 1947) put the programme under the Ministry of Supply, with Penny as scientific director pp. 35–36.

Britain first asked to use US sites in the Marshall Islands, hoping to revive the partnership; Truman refused for fear of Congress after the Klaus Fuchs spy arrest (February 1950). The reach of the colonies and Commonwealth found alternatives: the Monte Bello Islands, fifty miles off northwest Australia, for Operation Hurricane; then Operation Totem (October 1953) on the claypan of Emu, South Australia; and after surveying more than a dozen places “from Shetland to Somaliland, the Seychelles to the Falklands”, a permanent site, Maralinga, between Emu and the transcontinental railway p. 36.

The sites were half a world from Britain and far from Australia's southeast cities. Monte Bello was an uninhabited archipelago of limestone rock; Emu and Maralinga were red sand dotted with small oases. But “this barren landscape … masked the historical presence of mobile people”: pearl divers brought from Japan, the Philippines and other Asian countries, some of whom stayed after oyster exhaustion, the Depression and Japanese wartime attacks ruined pearling, and visited Monte Bello seasonally; and the Aboriginal Anangu, whose north–south trekking route (for hunting dingoes, delivering goods, visiting relatives) crossed the range even after a Christian mission closed in 1952 pp. 36–37.

Australian Prime Minister Robert Menzies, an Anglophile keen on British help for Australia's own atomic programme, asked for “some categorical and authoritative statement” that “the effects will be innocuous”. Penny feared technical detail would let Australians dispute the risk or send observers, so Britain gave “a public reassurance without disclosing its technical basis”. The joint statement of 18 February 1952 promised “no danger whatsoever from radioactivity to the health of people or animals in the Commonwealth” p. 37.

Penny's staff at Aldermaston also treated off-site exposure as an emergency. For Hurricane: an integrated 2 R, “reasonable for other people as what was probably a once-for-all exposure”. For Totem, essentially US Nevada criteria: fire only if inhabited areas stayed at the “zero-risk level” (3 R external gamma over ten weeks; not absolutely safe but “no measurable effect on the body”) or the “slight risk level” (up to 25 R; “some slight temporary sickness to a small number of people who have a low threshold sensitivity to radiation”) pp. 37–38.

These were for planning only. After Hurricane the test director cancelled a detailed survey when coastal flights found nothing; part of the cloud came back over the continent. On 10 October, a week after the test, radioactivity turned up in Melbourne rainwater, about two thousand miles away, and photographic production there shut down. Harry Messel (School of Physics, University of Sydney) put a monitor on his roof during Totem: “I do not believe that there will be any danger to the present population, but you should not go around exploding atomic bombs, however far away, without taking measurements.” p. 38

Unknown to Messel, Britain was secretly monitoring global fallout for intelligence, under the January 1948 modus vivendi with the US (sharing in non-weapon fields): rainwater measurements from 1951 at Harwell and Milford Haven (AERE with the Ministry of Agriculture); RAF stations in Scotland, Northern Ireland and Gibraltar, with filter-equipped bombers flying between them. By contrast, public-health measurements in Australia were “sporadic and haphazard”. Asked in Parliament about city monitoring, Minister of Supply Oliver Howard Beale repeated: “every precaution has been taken …” pp. 38–39.

Nobody foresaw the danger to the Aborigines, whom segregation policy made impossible to locate. After the first Totem shot a “black mist” reportedly rolled into the Yankunytjatjara community of Wallatina, about one hundred miles from Emu, leaving residents acutely sick. Only in 1985 did a Royal Commission conclude some Aboriginal people were likely exposed, with temporary illness. With no exposure records or medical exams, Britain kept its safety claims and moved on toward an “independent” deterrent p. 39.

§4pp. 39–40

Conclusion

Higuchi's conclusion

Central to the powers' “self-regulation of global radioactive contamination” was “the shared notion of emergency”. Designed to stop high doses delivered quickly, the system made small distant fallout look harmless, even “advantageous”, for detecting tests and modelling nuclear war. When North American readings forced the US to respond, officials stressed smallness compared with the permissible dose and background. “In this way, the three nuclear rivals jointly established the dominant definition of radioactive contamination outside the test sites as a harmless phenomenon.” pp. 39–40

The scientific basis had “numerous unknowns”: safety monitoring was confined to the region and the test period; the HASL network measured air but “did not follow the movement of the settled radionuclides through the food chain and into the human body”; military detection data were confidential and ignored debris once it settled; Sunshine was only “a quick check of the crude model”. “The fundamental instability and fragility of definitional power held by the three nuclear powers became instantly clear when the Castle Bravo test conducted in 1954 drastically raised public awareness” p. 40.

The essay in one breath

The bombs of 6 and 9 August 1945 “created” a new group of people, the hibakusha, who live with the “unexploded bomb” of delayed radiation effects and fear for their children. Their illness was shaped by culture (Shintō and Buddhist ideas of pollution), by gender and by politics (occupation, reconstruction, the Cold War). “Biomedicine,” deployed by both Japanese and American scientists and governments, “emerged as a hegemonic force in shaping definitions of hibakusha”, and victims' experiences, needs and moral questions “were repressed and denied”. She traces this in four stages: early denial and censorship; the ABCC's examine-but-never-treat research, which made victims “guinea pigs” and denied genetic effects; relief laws (from 1957) that medicalised suffering and labelled fear “A-bomb neurosis”; and a state that used medicine to avoid compensation and war responsibility, turning the bomb into “an event without actors”. The women's testimonies insist on the bomb as it really is: “a weapon of mass destruction”.

Opening: “a new group of human beings” Unheaded introduction · epigraph

“The bomb is not a matter of survival, it is a matter of living.”

Yamamoto Mitsuko, A-bomb victim (epigraph) · p. 67

The bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki (6 and 9 August 1945) destroyed two communities and also “created” a new group of human beings, hibakusha, “A-bombed persons” fn 1: those in the cities at the time, those contaminated by fallout in the following days, and those exposed in utero. About 350,000 are officially registered, including foreigners, especially Koreans; the term (written with different characters) now covers radiation victims worldwide, so she does not italicise it. Hibakusha share traumatic memories and above all “a common identity as the ‘radiation-exposed’”, living with the threat of delayed effects fn 2 (cancers, leukemia and blood disorders, cataracts; “divergent conditions” such as keloids, anemia, liver, endocrine and skin disease, nervous-system damage and general weakness). The feeling of carrying an “unexploded bomb” inside their bodies has not abated, and despite scientific denial of genetic effects, fears extend to their children p. 67.

Psychological effects may be partly shared across cultures fn 3 (Vyner on US “atomic veterans”), but culture shaped the illness. “Radiation” came to be seen as a polluting, defiling substance, part of highly developed Japanese beliefs about purity and pollution rooted in Shintō and Buddhism. Hibakusha were suspected of “transmitting” the impurity of death through genes or bodily “contagion”, which drove discrimination in marriage and work, like attitudes to the Burakumin fn 4 (tied to trades of blood and death: butchery, tanning, corpse disposal) or to AIDS patients pp. 67–68. Radiation pollution is unique because it is man-made, still “mysterious”, and grew up with the occupation, Japan's path to prosperity and the Cold War, so it carries “highly political meanings” p. 68.

Todeschini's thesis

She examines how scientists, doctors and officials responded to A-bomb victims and “radiation illness”, and the effects of scientific and legal discourse. “‘Biomedicine,’ deployed by both the Japanese and American scientific and political community, emerged as a hegemonic force in shaping definitions of hibakusha; in the process, victims' own experiences and needs, as well as the social and moral questions they raised by their very existence, were repressed and denied.” The process “epitomizes other approaches to massive, man-made suffering” fn 5 (the Dædalus “Social Suffering” issue, 1996) and continues through “second-generation victims”, who “tell us that no bombs are needed to create and perpetuate the dilemmas posed by radiation” pp. 68–69.

§1pp. 69–70

Hibakusha Narratives: A Voice of Their Own

“The objectifying logic of scientific and legal discourses is best brought out by victims' personal accounts.” Her sources: hibakusha narratives, especially women's testimonies, from published autobiographies, grassroots publications, oral testimony and her own interviews in Hiroshima and other cities, 1991–1993. Most relevant: Hayashi Kyōko, a prominent author and Nagasaki survivor living in Tokyo, and two women's groups formed in the late 1960s, the Osaka Association of Female A-bomb Victims and the Yamashita Group in Hiroshima fn 6 (both founded 1967; the Osaka group by three women, now a leading self-help, advocacy and counseling group; the Yamashita group's booklets, Asa [Morning], 1967–85) p. 69.

The women were adolescents in 1945. Unlike most testimonies, theirs focus on “what came later”: lost schooling and careers, discrimination in marriage and work, fear of bearing children, disrupted families, poverty. They reveal Japanese society's treatment of minorities and the social and cultural construction of hibakusha experience through gender, class, local ideas of the body, health and sickness, and local politics. Their anger at scientists, doctors and officials shows that “hibakusha and radiation illnesses are contested categories”, with scientific, biomedical and judicial discourse at odds with victims' own interpretations pp. 69–70.

§2pp. 70–75

“An Illegal Disease”: Emerging Interpretations

Denial and censorship · pp. 70–72

The most terrifying effect was acute radiation illness, which killed survivors who looked unhurt. Hayashi (14) and Takagi Shizuko (17, later a cofounder of the Osaka group) had no serious external injuries but were within 2 km of the hypocenter and suffered diarrhea, purpura, high fever and hair loss. Authorities did little. On 14 August the Japanese government called them “new type atomic bombs” with no explanation. Yet Japanese survey teams entered Hiroshima as early as 6 August and autopsies began on 10 August fn 7, so officials were well informed p. 70.

Until the national relief law of 1957 the state did nothing to help or inform victims, who relied on inexperienced local doctors. This contrasted with Japanese scientists' early interest: “although a national narrative attributes the scientific appropriation of hibakusha solely to ‘Americans,’ Japanese scientists demonstrated just as much interest”, constrained by censorship pp. 70–71.

The US began investigating on 15 August. It first denied radiation effects, calling radiation deaths “Japanese propaganda” fn 8 (Burchett), treating it as “just another” bomb. A Japanese government eager not to anger the occupier, and stressing its “rebirth” from militarism, accepted this. When effects could no longer be denied, the US imposed the Press Code on bomb information judged disruptive to “public peace and morals”, in force until the San Francisco Treaty (1952). Studies continued under GHQ; data from Japanese doctors were labelled “top secret” and sent to the United States fn 9 p. 71.

Censorship's overall effect is debated fn 10 (Braw; Rubin), but it kept hibakusha from learning about their exposure and sharing experiences. Literary accounts were censored; Ōta Yōko's pioneering account (written late 1945, City of Corpses, 1948 fn 11), with graphic radiation illness and criticism of both governments, was partly censored and delayed. The Code also blocked medical information among overwhelmed local doctors. Hayashi: “For the local doctors, we hibakusha were the ‘unknown’ (michi no mono). They didn't know how to treat us, and often they couldn't say much more than ‘please eat good food.’” fn 12 Another woman was told her white blood corpuscles had “abnormally increased”; neither she nor the doctor knew what it meant pp. 71–72.

Reproduction, blood and pollution · pp. 72–75

Reproductive effects caused the greatest agony. Babies exposed in the womb were stillborn, had major abnormalities (especially microcephaly) or growth disorders fn 13 (in one study of women within 2,000 m, nearly 25% of fetuses were stillborn, 26% died at or soon after birth, 25% had mental retardation); miscarriage and premature birth were frequent fn 14. Hayashi saw her pubescent body as a “shrunken, worm-eaten apple”, eaten from inside by radiation: “Nagasaki's barren landscape reflected my own self.” Girls dreaded menarche, linking it to the hemorrhaging of radiation illness, and heard rumours of “monstrous” babies disposed of by their mothers: “We were afraid we'd give birth to abnormal children. It was such a contradiction: More than most people, we were aware of the value and weight of human life … yet we were terrified that we should nip unborn life in the bud.” fn 15 fn 16 p. 72

Radiation as the “pollution of death” weighed most on women, whose reproductive capacity and blood are considered ritually polluted in Japan and elsewhere fn 17 (Namihira, Ohnuki-Tierney, Lock). Female hibakusha “combined” the impurity of death (radiation) and of blood (reproduction), so were seen as “doubly dangerous”; and women, not men, are blamed for sterility or abnormal offspring fn 18 (Héritier) pp. 72–73. One account compared Nagasaki girls to “outcasts” who “never stop bleeding” and whom “nobody wants to marry” fn 19. Burn scars, keloids, were taken as “evidence” of contamination fn 20 (the keloid-scarred “A-bomb maidens” of Amami Island, shunned for fear of airborne infection). In films and novels leukemia, “the prime symbol for ‘contaminated blood,’” is a “female” disease, as hysteria was in nineteenth-century Europe fn 21. Marriage discrimination followed; several Osaka members could not marry, and informants' daughters face the same today p. 73.

Women internalised it: narratives dwell on “impure blood”, menstrual ailments and childbirth fears; Hayashi repeatedly asks her son to “forgive her” for “having marked him with my stain”. Medical social workers' unpublished figures: “blood disorders” are the most cited “subjective symptom” of women applying for aid fn 22 (1993: a third of female applicants versus 10% of male) pp. 73–74. Female vulnerability was also real: high ovarian, uterine and breast cancer rates fn 23, clear only two decades on. Hayashi: “radiation was particularly bent on destroying women's reproductive organs” fn 24 p. 74.

Scientists were intensely curious fn 25 (spermatogenesis, menstruation, pregnancy; all at least temporarily disturbed). Japanese researchers questioned Hayashi's class in embarrassing detail: “Are your menstrual periods heavy? … Did you get your first period after you were exposed to radiation?” fn 26 They concluded fertility was not lastingly affected (except at very high doses) and abnormal births were limited to in-utero exposure fn 27.

Todeschini's view

Those studies were unavailable to hibakusha, and “it makes little sense to contrast this ‘scientific truth’ with victims' own interpretations; the very fact that their reproductive functions attracted scientific scrutiny sent the implicit message that there was a problem” p. 74.

Radiation was imagined as a “poison”, a substance that could be passed on but also expelled: folk remedies (herbal and moxa treatments) based on Shintō purging; mothers and families as a “symbolic counterpoint to the ‘coldness’ displayed by scientists”; bleeding, vomiting, bathing and sweating to flush it out; even burns. One woman: “I believe even now that the radiation in my body was expelled through blood, pus, and sweat.” pp. 74–75

Todeschini's reading

These beliefs arose just as officials minimised radiation illness, and persist against scientific argument. They give meaning to the mystery of radiation, “‘cutting it down’ to the manageable, human size of impurities and poisonous substances”. For her informants they became “an embodied memory of suffering and loss, and a form of resistance against official responses that denied the victims both understanding and legitimacy” p. 75.

Not in TodeschiniThe heading's phrase “an illegal disease” is not explained in the text; it matches the essay's title, suffering that official science and law would not recognise as legitimate.

§3pp. 75–83

Hibakusha and “Science”: From Victims to Guinea Pigs

The Atomic Bomb Casualty Commission (ABCC), sent by the US in 1946 to research “long-term radiation effects on survivors” (cancers, leukemia, sterility, genetic changes), and its successor, the Radiation Effects Research Foundation (RERF), “occupy a central place” p. 75. Their data are the most important and are held reliable; given censorship the ABCC had a “virtual monopoly” on delayed-effect studies for nearly a decade and shaped hibakusha medical law; since 1975 the RERF also performs routine exams. Its links to both governments and scientific elites fn 28 (ABCC: presidential directive, National Academy of Sciences, Armed Forces and AEC funds; RERF: US Department of Energy, NAS, Japanese Ministry of Health and Welfare) make critical scrutiny important p. 76.

In 1950 the ABCC built an impressive institute on a hill overlooking Hiroshima. Victims hoped for treatment; instead it only “investigated” and as policy refused treatment, calling that local doctors' job. Treatment would have looked like “atonement” and an admission of guilt, delegitimising the bombing, which was unacceptable to Washington fn 29 (Lindee, Suffering Made Real) p. 76.

The no-treatment policy became “the primary bone of contention”. Only “examined”, victims felt used as “guinea pigs”, or worse, for preparing future nuclear wars, a suspicion fed by AEC funding. “Aggressive” diagnostics (blood, urine, X-rays, postmortems) deepened the sense that bodies were “misappropriated and objectified”; hence accusations of a “sinister, even diabolical” institution pp. 76–77. The RERF brochure insists its research is “for peaceful purposes” fn 30, but the reputation stuck. Matsuzaka Yoshimasa: citizens still ask “Who knows what's really going on there?”; he appealed to it to “come down the mountain” (yama kara kudari yo) fn 31 p. 77.

“They were always polite and friendly, asking me about ‘how I was feeling,’ etc., and giving me coffee and sandwiches. But they never told me about the results. Young and married as I was, I had to undress and put on a white cloth, and they'd examine me thoroughly. I had problems with my uterus and they wanted to keep examining it. And they took countless X-rays, despite the fact that I'd received so much radiation anyway! … They just told me that I was extremely anemic and gave me some sort of medicine, but it made me terribly sick and I stopped taking it. Perhaps they were testing the medicine, too? It gradually dawned on me, why as a victim, do I help the aggressor? … Indeed, I was stupid.”

Yamashita group member (her friend: “I always had this feeling of great loneliness when I went there. I was scared of that place …”) · p. 77

Humiliating exams recur in women's accounts; women raised prewar to be chaste and to fear Americans as “devils” were traumatised. A Hiroshima man, after sake, confessed “unspeakable hate” for the US scientists who examined his mother nude. The theme shows science becoming “gendered” and conflated with race: women's violation read as “penetration” of Japanese culture by the foreign “other” pp. 77–78.

Data and minimisation. The closed ABCC guarded its materials, followed occupation policy in minimising effects, and “reassured” hibakusha their complaints were not radiation-related; several scientists said it underplayed acute and delayed effects fn 32 (Nakagawa; Sugihara). A mother took her microcephalic son (irradiated in utero) and was told it was “malnutrition”, then given money instead of advice. The ABCC tended to blame poverty that supposedly predated the bombs; officials from the elite looked “down” on victims and blamed protest on “ignorance” fn 33 p. 78.

Genetics. The ABCC consistently denied genetic mutations, creating an “optimistic” image that the RERF perpetuates and that advertises the bomb's “harmlessness” pp. 78–79. Building on Susan Lindee: the genetics study was shaped “as much by Cold War politics and public concerns about radiation as it was by ‘pure science’”. Mutations were defined selectively fn 34 (only those “threatening for the future survival of the species”: sex ratio, birth weight, malformation, stillbirth, neonatal death; excluded reduced fertility, early spontaneous abortion, minor malformations). A 1953 report discounted stillbirth effects and minimised “sex ratio effects” to “avoid misinterpretation by the popular press”. After interviewing ABCC director Robert Holmes, U.S. News and World Report (1955) ran “Thousands of Babies, No A-bomb Effects.” p. 79

Critics add weak methods and possible recessive mutations in later generations fn 35 (Barnaby). In 1992 the RERF's American chief of research admitted an “overly crude test methodology” might explain finding “absolutely nothing” in the second generation, and said: “It all depends on your research methodology, and on the manner in which you interpret the facts; science is nothing else but a public consensus.” Todeschini calls this “surprisingly accurate and candid” p. 79.

Reassurance meant little to hibakusha alarmed by 1950s leukemia fn 36 (mortality peaked 1950–54, over thirty times higher at high doses; still seven times higher in 1965–71). Well-publicised deaths of hibakusha children devastated parents who felt guilty for giving birth. Two Osaka women's loss of adolescent children to leukemia led to founding the group, whose brochure says: “Even one case of hereditary effects of radioactive matter constitutes an unforgivable sin against justice and humanity.” p. 80

The “Lucky Dragon” incident (1954), a Japanese fishing boat contaminated by fallout, one crewman dead of acute radiation illness, revived fear. Hayashi grabbed her one-year-old son's wrist: “The weak pulsations … seemed too feeble to support a life” fn 37. Her words point to ch'i (ki), vital energy in East Asian medicine, whose loss or blockage causes illness; her ki, her son's and other victims' are connected. So is the mother–child tie (Takie Lebra's “mammalian symbiosis”; the child as bunshin, “split part” of the mother) fn 38 pp. 80–81. Hibakusha use “hereditary” and “disease” widely, including experiential, cultural and social aspects; ABCC scientists, with their “inappropriately named ‘patients,’” declared anyone without “objective evidence” healthy p. 81.

An insider · unpublished ABCC report, 1955 (fn 39: George M. Naglehurst)

A former ABCC doctor realised “none of us working here really knew any of the patients.” Without permission he held “lengthy conversations” with hibakusha, found most “live in fear” and are treated by family as “‘invalid’ (sic) and ‘useless’”, named it “Atomic Bomb Stress”, said the ABCC was “completely unaware” and that “nothing comparable … exists in the history of man”. He said it was necessary to “delve deeply into people's histories” and asked for six more months and funds; refused, the project ended, and individual ABCC members were pressured to “abstain” from studying victims' experiences p. 81.

Little has changed: the US chief of research, in Hiroshima over five years, admitted he had “never once talked to hibakusha personally” p. 82. Lindee: “(M)aking the suffering at Hiroshima and Nagasaki disappear … was … a long-term act of scientific work” fn 40 (also Gusterson, Nuclear Rites).

Hayashi, 1980: “Are we women living witnesses to the inhumanity of the bomb, or living proofs of its harmlessness? Will we be used as ‘medicine’ against the nuclear allergy?” fn 41 Recent reassurances: “unexpected longevity of hibakusha”; higher-risk studies dismissed as “advocacy documents”; RERF (1991): cancer death risk “only [sic] 2.5 percent higher than normal”; a former NAS statistics director in 1995, the fiftieth anniversary, “not coincidentally”: “only [sic] about 1,500 people have died from radiation-caused cancers in fifty years” fn 42; a 1992 Science article saying the bomb's radiation was “less effective in producing cancer than has been assumed” and its output “needs recalculating” fn 43, as if the “experiment” had to be rerun. They omit that uncertainty persists and that today's weapons make claims of “innocuousness” “painfully obsolete” pp. 82–83.

Informants dismiss such reports as “one more expression of scientific perversion”. Hayashi: “Our wounds have been inflicted deliberately, by calculation.… And because of these calculations, we and our children continue to suffer.” fn 44 Testimony remains “the ‘power of the word’” in its primal sense: making humanness and “re-membering” bodies that are or may be “dis-membered” p. 83.

“Problem Patients”: The Dilemmas of Medicalization

From the 1957 relief law the state paid for care and specialised institutions grew. But the “hibakusha health-care complex” inherited the ABCC-RERF's narrow biomedical focus on “objective symptoms”. In medical anthropology's terms, doctors treated “disease” (bodily symptoms tied to the bomb) and neglected “illness” (lived experience) p. 83.

There is no specific pathology of radiation illness: a radiation cancer looks like any other, so doctors infer cause by comparing with non-hibakusha averages. Ōta Yōko (1955): hibakusha are “incurable” (fuchi) like the terminally ill, yet many “are not even sick” and cannot “marshal” the data doctors need fn 45. Encounters are tense: patients feel minimised, doctors see “difficult” patients, “problem patients” pp. 83–84.

The relief laws (free checkups, treatment, allowances for designated illnesses) pushed victims and well-meaning doctors to turn “subjective complaints” into verifiable symptoms, perhaps leading victims to overemphasise complaints to qualify. Bodily complaints “can also be interpreted as a form of resistance against normative biomedical definitions of illness” fn 46 (Lock). The laws gave doctors power over eligibility, adding a material stake. An Osaka member seeking an allowance was told: “There are many people, older than you, who suffer from more severe illnesses—we can't give you special treatment just because you're a hibakusha … So I backed off” pp. 84–85. Women especially hesitate to challenge doctors, addressed as sensei (master): “We'd been raised to consider doctors as eminent people” p. 85.

Biannual checkups were welcomed but made some expect serious illness, ironic as scientists said effects were “not as bad” as feared. For second-generation hibakusha the contradiction was starkest: in 1973 the Health and Welfare Ministry began routine checkups while claiming “no genetic effects” and calling them “purely a research activity” with no medical justification p. 85.

Anxieties were blamed on a “neurotic” obsession. Hiroshima physicians coined “A-bomb neurosis” (genbaku-noirōze), likened to hypochondriasis, phobias, sometimes psychosis. Robert Lifton described “a lifelong preoccupation with ‘A-bomb disease’—with blood counts and bodily complaints, particularly that of weakness”, part of an “atomic bomb mythology which attributes all death to radiation effects”; hibakusha “resist clarification” fn 47 pp. 85–86. Many Japanese physicians share this; similar “pathological behavior” was reported in US atomic veterans fn 48 (Vyner's “Radiation Response Syndrome”) p. 86.

The “neurotic” charge created a vicious circle: victims felt they must give suffering a biological basis. Ōta: “I know my body better than any medical equipment.… The term ‘A-bomb neurosis’ is just a sly label for something doctors can't figure out.” Her severe anemia was blamed on her “unhealthy” writer's lifestyle, “writing at night and sleeping during the day”; she came to envy women with keloids, who had “something to show for it” fn 49 p. 86.

Todeschini's critique of psychologization

Beneath the labels lurks the assumption that fears are irrational and should be forgotten fn 50 (the Kleinmans on professional transformations of suffering). “Neurosis” is “highly questionable”: ordinary neuroses spring from intrapsychic conflict, whereas hibakusha anxieties are rooted in “devastatingly ‘real’ experiences”. Focusing on the individual cuts suffering loose from its social and political context: “the psychiatric idiom is not necessarily more humane than the biomedical/scientific discourse used by ABCC researchers” pp. 86–87.

No therapy was offered; consultations stayed “purely medical”. Mental illness carried stigma as “moral weakness”, handled in the family or not at all; only extreme cases went to institutions giving drugs, not therapy, in a system with overcrowding, involuntary long stays, no rehabilitation and patient abuse fn 51 p. 87. The Hiroshima A-bomb hospital gave only treatment and exams; hibakusha without “a real illness” had no one to talk to. Counseling by medical social workers began only in the 1980s; the hospital's sole social worker, also serving non-hibakusha, was overwhelmed and had no specialist psychiatrists pp. 87–88.

The luxurious old people's home drew criticism that hibakusha were being “bought off”; a Yamashita member called it a showcase (“See how well we are treating hibakusha!”); because it lies far from the city, the Osaka group likened it to obasute, “abandoning old women” fn 53 (the mountain-village custom fictionalised in Ballads of Narayama) p. 88. A hibakusha mother wanted to enter with her microcephalic, in-utero-exposed daughter as “one unit” (hito-kumi); the city's rules forbade it. Sharing a room mattered more to her than any equipment pp. 88–89.

§5pp. 89–94

Expelling Politics: Biomedicine as a “Solution”

Medicine's usefulness to politics is as clear in the Japanese state as in the ABCC. From 1945 to 1957 the government denied responsibility; occupation policy suited its view of the war as a “great folly,” brought to a merciful end by the bombs. As reform gave way to reconstruction, hibakusha were left behind in Japan's plan to become a superpower p. 89.

By the early 1950s the cities were rebuilt and hibakusha a minority among vigorous newcomers. Expensive memorials honoured the dead while the living struggled. Many hibakusha, disproportionately women, worked as day labourers fn 54, some building Hiroshima's Peace Park, the “haut-lieu de mémoire” and epitome of “sanitized, ahistorical memory” fn 55 (Yoneyama) p. 89. Observers in the mid-1950s found rebuilt Hiroshima “more beautiful” than before; hibakusha bodies, showing the bombs still killed in “peace and prosperity”, threatened the “ideology of renewal”. Americans saw the city's “rebirth” as “yet another sign of the bomb's harmlessness, even usefulness” p. 90.

After the mid-1950s, with the Cold War and the 1954 Lucky Dragon, the government took an overtly “antinuclear” stance as the “sole A-bombed country”. Little of this nationalist discourse acknowledged hibakusha; the absence of aid had become a “national embarrassment”. For the relief laws it relied uncritically on existing biomedical studies; the first national survey of victims came only in 1965, and was largely statistical p. 90.

Allowances are graded by illness category, plus a “nursing allowance”, a “health-management allowance” and even a “funeral allowance”: a way of “paying off” hibakusha. The Ministry claims needs are met in an “antinuclear spirit” fn 56, and many Japanese think hibakusha get “special treatment” pp. 90–91. In reality red tape blocks many; the most generous allowance (about $1,000 a month during treatment) goes to under 1 percent; most get the health-management allowance, about $300. Most allowances are income-tested, so hibakusha are treated like despised welfare recipients (under 1 percent of Japanese are on welfare), asking for “charity” (omegumi). The law requires them to be both “sick” and “deficient” to fit “institutionalized hibakushahood”; many eligible informants refuse to apply p. 91.

Todeschini's central claim

The medical and bureaucratic laws were “a convenient solution to a moral and political dilemma”: they let the government “respond” while avoiding reflection on its responsibility for the war fn 57 (Buruma). “By transforming hibakushas' suffering into ‘just another disease,’ and thus the bomb into an event without actors, a medical approach serves to suppress the questions that are integral to a historically and politically informed understanding of the A-bomb experience.” It also sustains the false belief that radiation can be treated or “cured” pp. 91–92.

Compensation. The laws deflect from state compensation based on collective responsibility. Hibakusha groups have long sought a “genuine relief law”: state compensation plus a public commitment to eliminate nuclear weapons fn 58 (Hidankyō). The government refuses: “hibakusha should not be given special treatment.” The US says the San Francisco Treaty settled civilian claims. Japan pays no damages to war victims but gives former military personnel generous compensation and pensions that dwarf hibakusha benefits. A revealing 1980 Health and Welfare Ministry report: military personnel are “rewarded” for service; civilian victims, hibakusha included, must “endure” fn 59 (Ishida) p. 92.

Here medicine is the deus ex machina: the report conceded hibakusha suffer “special medical conditions” from “radiation damage” that justify “relief measures” (engo-taisaku) fn 60. Once medical and economic needs are “taken care of”, the difference from other war victims is cancelled and all fold into “collective war sacrifice”: “the population should accept these losses, in the name of the ‘general sacrifice’ (ippan no gisei) that all citizens must pay …” fn 61 pp. 92–93.

This legalism “reveals the hypocrisy” of the “antinuclear spirit” and matches the American discourse that celebrates the bombs. In 1982 Nevada fallout victims who sued were rejected because the government is “immune to damage claims” when “doing research for the benefit of the public” fn 62. Chernobyl survivors and victims of Russian and French testing met the same abdication: “the discourses on the bomb and radiation transcend national boundaries” p. 93.

For the Osaka group, compensation is urgent as hibakusha age. Media disinterest is striking: the Murayama cabinet debated compensation in 1994 almost unnoticed fn 63, unlike the global coverage of Holocaust restitution, which shows how ambiguous Hiroshima's “place in memory” is beside Auschwitz's fn 64 (Minear; Brossat) p. 93. “We cannot seem to decide whether the A-bombings were ‘beneficial’ or crimes against humanity”, a question suppressed by “science” and “law”. Hayashi and the Osaka and Yamashita women lead back to the bomb “as it really is: a weapon of mass destruction, designed to injure and destroy human bodies, instantaneously and over time” pp. 93–94.

§6pp. 94–95

Coda: “Dustbins for Eternity”?

“I wonder whether there is not a way to wipe out hibakusha (zetsumetsu no hōhō). In view of the hereditary risks of ‘A-bomb disease,’ we should think about applying the Eugenics law, and the city should initiate policies to prevent hibakusha from bearing children. This would also be better from the point of view of government finances.”

A municipal assemblyman, Diet session on “medical relief for second-generation hibakusha”, Tokyo, 1978 · fn 65 (Mainichi shinbun, 2 July 1978) · p. 94

The remark caused a stir but epitomises the essay's two themes: the use of “science” to “wipe out” people and of “medical laws” to contain human and financial consequences. It is “simply an honest expression of the mixture of denial, thinly veiled hostility, pragmatism, and hypocrisy” that marked reactions “on both sides of the Pacific” p. 94.

Todeschini's last word

In the politician's eugenic “fantasy of elimination” echoes humanity's ancient dread of the “impure”, projected on irradiated bodies: our wish that radiation's forces can be “domesticated” and this “modern impurity” expelled by technical means, in both military and civil uses. The atom is here to stay; radioactive substances outlive our concept of time and can only be buried as waste, hoping “the monster might never emerge in our lifetime” pp. 94–95. Françoise Zonabend, studying the La Hague waste plant, calls radioactivity “a waste that will never be eliminated … une souillure éternelle” (an eternal impurity) fn 66. “Hibakusha have also become the wastebins of our collective imagination; they and their children carry ‘radioactive pollution,’ and the specter of wholesale devastation that it conjures, on behalf of all of us.” p. 95

The article in one breath

The Radiation Effects Research Foundation (RERF), called the Atomic Bomb Casualty Commission (ABCC) from 1947 to 1975, has tracked the bomb survivors for almost seventy years. It is the “gold standard” of radiation risk, advises on Chernobyl and now studies Fukushima's cleanup workers. It is also distrusted for neglecting low doses, black rain and internal exposure. Lindee calls the bombings “the first techno-scientific disaster” and draws three lessons from the institution. (1) Dosimetry, the after-the-fact estimate of each survivor's dose, “is a fundamentally humanistic exercise”: a decades-long reenactment through interviews, phantom houses bombed in Nevada, and redrawn maps. (2) The RERF made citizenship a credential: its leaders had to be Japanese or American, so experts drew “on their own political status to make claims about biology”. (3) The line between military and civilian science does not hold. By the 1970s nuclear power justified the study, and the RERF's data (the basis of worker standards) and survivors' activism drew bombs and reactors together, especially after Fukushima, “Japan's ‘third atomic bombing’”.

Opening: Fukushima, trust, and three claims Abstract and unheaded introduction

Fukushima (from March 2011) raised global awareness of nuclear risk, undermined trust as government and electric company leaders “misled the public”, disrupted more than 2 million lives, permanently evacuated some areas, and produced new citizen science: a Twitter-born campaign mapping soil radiation and do-it-yourself cleanups in Koriyama n. 1 (Safecast; Collins's “default expertise”, the citizen sense that experts may be wrong; the group 3a run by contractor Suzuki Yohei; four Citizen-Scientist symposia; Citizen Science Initiative Japan). Radioactive water still flows into the Pacific daily n. 2 (one scientist found the leak probably significant, contrary to TEPCO's daily reassurances), and scraped soil sits in plastic bags in lots, fields and roadsides p. 185.

The disaster was called a “third atomic bombing” of Japan. Hiroshima survivor Keijiro Matsushima, lecturing young diplomats at the Peace Memorial Museum (summer 2011): “It seemed as though the Japanese people were experiencing the third atomic bombing, after Hiroshima and Nagasaki.” Survivors had long campaigned against weapons but were “silent on nuclear energy”; Fukushima changed that. Masahito Hirose to the New York Times (August 2011): “Is it Japan's fate to repeatedly serve as a warning to the world about the dangers of radiation? I wish we had found the courage to speak out earlier against nuclear power” p. 185.

Nuclear issues pose “classic problems of trust, authority, and expertise” in STS (Lakoff, Macfarlane, Hecht, Nelkin). Following Ulrich Beck's Risk Society, they are “scientific at both ends”: made by elite physics, chemistry and engineering at the front end; tallied at the back end by “messy, slow epidemiology”, psychological and social work, even political science and field biology. Lindee's subject is “that messy scientific work at the chaotic end”, made together by experts and survivors p. 185.

Why the RERF is trusted (the “gold standard”): it documented leukemia, solid tumours, cardiovascular disease and more; it has a large sample, men and women of all ages, an unselected population (not only workers), relatively high exposures, extensive dose calculation and comprehensive follow-up. Its staff have been consulted after every radiation accident since the 1970s, planned Chernobyl's long-term studies, and in 2014 were appointed to study the most exposed Fukushima workers p. 186.

Why it is distrusted: inattention to low-dose risk. The linear, non-threshold hypothesis (even tiny exposures do harm, though too rarely to find) guides limits but is “a precautionary way of understanding radiation risk, grounded not in robust data, but rather in logic”. Critics fault neglect of residual radiation on early entrants, black rain and internal exposure. Survivor-physicist Shoji Sawada (Nagoya; Gensuikyo) charges a “cover-up of the effects of internal exposure by residual radiation”; a December 2014 RERF paper answered the black-rain charge; Alice Stewart (1982) judged ABCC-RERF work a poor guide to low-dose worker risk (Figure 1: the ABCC/RERF buildings on Hijiyama Hill) pp. 186–187.

“The RERF is, therefore, both trusted and not trusted, both believed and disbelieved.” Researchers lament that the public fears radiation more than equal risks n. 3 (a “taken-for-granted fact” among such scientists, rarely published); critics say scientists are “in the pocket” of industry. Olga Kuchinskaya: “radiation is not directly perceptible to the unaided human senses … formal representations become doubly important”. Radiation exists for survivors only through scientific inscription (articles, reports, dose-response curves n. 4), which may drive public fear pp. 186–187.

Lindee's claims

Engaging the 2013 Berkeley STS Forum on Fukushima n. 5, she characterises the atomic bombings as “the first techno-scientific disaster — produced by elite physicists”. Three propositions p. 187:

1. Dosimetry “is a fundamentally humanistic exercise critical to all risk assessment”. Disaster epidemiology must reconstruct a short sequence “in excruciating detail”, calculating each survivor's risk, so it is “both imaginative and historical”; STS ideas of situated knowledge and citizen science apply pp. 187–188.

2. Citizenship. Disasters apportion blame (Vaughan); Laetita Atlani-Dualt [Laëtitia Atlani-Duault] calls them “collective constructs” of the operational, discursive and symbolic. Expert claims are trusted or not by the scientist's national identity (Boudia), and institutions can perform this by requiring nationalities, “as at the RERF”. Attention to citizenship-based authority matters for disaster planning p. 188.

3. “The mongrel nature of militarized and civilian sciences”. Bombs justified the study; by the 1970s nuclear power “became crucial to continued public funding”. “Industrial interests in Japan and the United States sought to draw a sharp line between the risks of nuclear war and the risks of nuclear power, but the work of the RERF … and the activism of atomic bomb survivors have drawn these two issues together.” The RERF is “a critical node in a complex global network of scientific institutions that adjudicate radiation risk” p. 188.

Coalitions form: Fukushima's people have joined Marshall Islanders, Chernobyl residents and bomb survivors; together with their scientists they “capture nuclear history as it plays out in the vulnerabilities of the human body” p. 188.

§1pp. 188–190

Creating the ABCC

Both Japanese and Allied experts saw at once the importance of studying survivors. Press reports of “mysterious symptoms” prompted an Allied survey under “tragic conditions” as the Occupation began; its 1946 “Joint Commission” report made the case for a long-term study. Japanese scientists had already begun collecting data pp. 188–189.

Truman approved; the US National Academy of Sciences (NAS) would organise it and the Atomic Energy Commission (AEC), “widely mistrusted”, would fund it. The “‘shotgun’ wedding of the NAS and the AEC” made a “vexed alliance” of different cultures; NAS scientists often challenged AEC policy. Japanese scientists were sidelined: until 1952 censorship banned Japanese publication on the bombs, so “people all over the world debated the morality and impact of the bombings, while public discussion was banned in the country directly affected”. A formal alliance with the new Japanese National Institutes of Health (JNIH) in March 1948 made the project “joint”, a contested term. Even after censorship Japanese access was limited as the US “tried to control public perceptions of radiation risk”. Japan paid only about 10 percent of the budget until 1975, and scientific leadership always came from the US p. 189.

A key triumph: a survivor-status question in the 1950 Japanese census. “Hibakusha” was an undesirable category; survivors faced job and marriage discrimination as supposed poor workers and bad marital partners (feared genetic effects, “though genetic effects were never found”). Self-identification rose gradually, and the census created a core population for research p. 189.

In 1955 the Francis Committee, chaired by virologist-epidemiologist Thomas Francis Jr. (University of Michigan; influenza work, flu vaccines), set a structure that endures: a fixed “Master Sample” based on the 1950 census pp. 189–190.

§2pp. 190–192

‘ABCC is dead. Long live ABCC’

Early 1969: ABCC director George Darling proposed making Japanese scientists more equal partners. The incentive was financial: 1963–73 the budget more than doubled while staff fell by 300 (1,063 in 1951; 980 in 1962; 680 in 1972). In 1972 the US paid ¥1.25 billion and Japan ¥74 million; the dollar was falling and deficits mounting n. 6 (NAS president Philip Handler's 1973 letters to James Neel) p. 190.

A 1969 Tokyo meeting began four years of talk n. 7 (US proposal January 1972; Japanese proposals November 1972 and March 1973). In late April 1973, with the AEC, NAS, Foreign Ministry and Health and Welfare, Japan affirmed an “equal participation principal [sic]” n. 8 but deferred money. “Reduction in Force” threats (most employees were Japanese) and domestic criticism of Tokyo's “evasive” replies helped n. 9 p. 190.

AEC chemist Spofford English n. 10 (AEC career until 1976; also a disarmament advocate at the 1955 London disarmament talks and 1959 Geneva test-ban conference) offered three options: two moved staff into the JNIH (likely unappealing); the third was a “new, autonomous, non-profit research institute, incorporated under Japanese law, funded and governed equally by Japan and the United States”. His argument “called up the specter of a nuclear energy accident” p. 191:

“Japan has one of the most rapidly expanding nuclear power industries in the world and Japan will need this information … to insure to their own Japanese people that the nuclear energy programs of Japan are safe.”

Spofford English, 1973 · n. 11 · p. 191

The programme “should be continued for 20–25 years”; it has had “an ever-receding sunset horizon since the 1950s, always about 20 years away”. The AEC also linked a $320 million sale of fuel for Japanese power stations to support for the ABCC n. 12: an “ironic trade” of energy technology for research to evaluate its risks p. 191.

April 1974, after Japan funded another year, Darling wrote to biophysicist Detlev Bronk (NAS president 1950–62): “ABCC is dead! Long live ABCC (by whatever name)!” n. 13 The name turned on whether to mention the bomb. Proposed first: “Institute for Radiological Studies”, “suitably non-military” n. 14 (Aaron Rosenthal to Handler, 17 April 1973: the AEC was “receptive to the proposed Charter for an independent, autonomous Institute”). The Japan Radiation Research Society and Japan Science Council opposed “RERF” for putting “the A-bomb under cover” n. 15. A diplomat told NAS president Philip Handler of “considerable consternation … It is felt to be too all-encompassing”: medical-irradiation researchers feared competition, and utilities “would like to remain at arms' length from all aspects of radiation effects … fearing a stronger association with nuclear power” n. 16 p. 191.

Ralph Nader (whose anti-nuclear group had about 200,000 supporters) planned a much-publicised stop in Hiroshima to link nuclear energy and survivors; the nuclear community's response was “to strengthen, rather than weaken, the association between radiation effects and bombs, and to disassociate nuclear power” n. 17 pp. 191–192.

Lindee's point

“But nuclear power was indeed the main justification for sustaining the costly research program.” By the 1970s survivors “had become surrogates not only for American civilians threatened with nuclear attack but also for workers, local residents, and anyone at risk from a nuclear power plant accident” p. 192.

Final agreement: December 1974. ABCC closing ceremony, March 1975, Hiroshima; RERF inaugurated 1 April 1975, jointly sponsored by the US and Japan p. 192.

§3pp. 192–193

Trustworthiness via passport: The citizenship credential

At the ABCC most employees were Japanese but control was wholly American; until 1959 many publications were only in English. Some Washington overseers feared Japanese scientists would “see more risk” because the victims were Japanese; some Japanese critics believed AEC-funded US scientists “routinely minimized radiation risks” to reassure the public about weapons and energy p. 192.

That mistrust was written into the RERF's rules n. 18 n. 19:

  • 1974 draft: Chairman and Vice Chairman from different countries.
  • Articles of incorporation: an “equal number of Councilors having Japanese nationality and US nationality”; Executive Councilor and Vice Executive Councilor of either nationality but never the same at the same time; likewise Chairman and Vice Chairman.
  • No leader from a third country; positions to alternate “for every term of office”.
  • Even the 1995 Blue Ribbon Committee allocated seats by Japanese and US citizenship, with third-country members chosen alternately by each side n. 20. In the 1990s, sensing “the United States has run out of candidates for leadership positions”, it suggested “internationalizing” the pool n. 21; the binational agreement did not allow it pp. 192–193.

Boudia notes that in 1955 some scientists demanded risk studies by scientists from countries without nuclear weapons: scientists understood “as nationally located” p. 193.

Lindee's concept · reversing Petryna

Adriana Petryna's “biological citizenship” describes how Chernobyl's affected used their health status to claim state support and resources. “In my account … the valences are reversed. … Petryna's citizens draw on their own biology to establish a particular political status. My experts draw on their own political status to make claims about biology.” What is remarkable is “the institutional, international scientific consensus that qualities of citizenship should be configured as credentials” p. 193.

The RERF became an international training centre as its emphasis moved “seamlessly” from nuclear war to nuclear energy, drawing scientists from the US, China, the USSR/Russia and Ukraine n. 22. After Chernobyl Reactor No. 4 exploded (1986) it hosted four Soviet scientists; in 1990 RERF chairman Itsuzo Shigematsu chaired the IAEA–WHO Advisory Committee on Chernobyl. Media reports of severe sickness and malformed infants “seemed inconsistent with the known doses”, and RERF data would “clarify the health status of people” n. 23. At Vienna's International Chernobyl Project meeting, with 570,000 people exposed, Shigematsu stressed dose estimation for every person p. 193:

“… it may be no exaggeration to say that more than half of all the efforts made in Hiroshima and Nagasaki during the past 45 years to follow up the late health effects of the atomic bomb survivors has been devoted to the dosimetry problem.”

Itsuzo Shigematsu, Vienna, 1991 · n. 24 (emphasis added by Lindee) · p. 193
§4pp. 194–198

Reenacting chaos

People who study disasters “spend a great deal of time reliving them”. At the moment of detonation, when “all other exposures were trivial”, the radiation released was not a “stable, measured, known” fact but “frequently recalculated estimations, grounded in technical and social uncertainties and shaped by Cold War secrecy” p. 194.

Secrecy · pp. 194–195

The AEC refused to tell ABCC scientists about the bombs' radiation. In 1950 Cornell physicist Robert R. Wilson estimated doses from published sources (chiefly Glasstone, 1950). More was known about the Nagasaki implosion design, tested at Alamogordo (16 July 1945) and Bikini (1946), but test estimates were withheld; the Hiroshima gun-type bomb was used once and never again, so its yield relies on extrapolation. “The bomb's radiation impact was a state secret even for a state-supported scientific project.” Early ABCC leukemia papers used distance from the hypocenter, not dose p. 194.

Key data “fell victim to post-war secrecy”. A Kyoto University team under physicist Sakae Shimizu collected photographic film from shops and sulfur from telephone-pole insulators, measured radiation directly, estimated burst height with home-built instruments and pre-war US Bureau of Standards calibration sources: “the very best, most immediate data”. The Occupation confiscated everything; its fate is still unknown. Oak Ridge health physicist John A. Auxier (1977): “It is likely that they are in some file … and that someday they will be found. In the meantime, the job … went on without them.” By 1955 Soviet tests “had made clear the absurdity of attempting to classify and keep secret every scrap of information” and serious dosimetry began pp. 194–195.

Survivors reenact · p. 195

From October 1952 Japanese investigators took detailed histories: location, clothing, what survivors heard, blast or heat, black rain, then the full story. Survivors took teams to where they had stood and “re-enacted the moment in physical and embodied terms”; draftsmen's drawings survive at the RERF. By end-1954: 2,000 histories in Nagasaki, 650 in Hiroshima. Everyone near the hypocenter who survived was inside a building, so the AEC's blueprints mattered, including the Mitsubishi Arsenal Complex in Nagasaki, where one-fourth of all survivors within 2,000 m had been. Shielding categories: “in the open”, “behind some building”, “in the open but totally shielded by terrain”, “shielded totally by a factory building”, and “fetal exposure” — shielded by a maternal body n. 25 p. 195.

Phantom houses in Nevada · pp. 195–197

In 1956 Oak Ridge's Health Physics Division set up Ichiban (“first”, connoting “best”), which staged reenactments of the bombings in the Nevada “dessert” [desert]. With the ABCC it specified a “typical” Japanese house for a surrogate bombing of a “phantom” Japanese community at the Nevada Test Site, measuring mud plaster, slate, wood siding and tatami. The ABCC sent bamboo, seaweed for stucco, roof tiles and oyster shells; US crews built the houses. In the 1957 Plumbob tests they were loaded with 120 sensors and bombed, yielding T57D, “tentative 1957 dosimetry”. Later, Johns-Manville cement–asbestos board was found equivalent in shielding and replaced the Japanese materials (Figure 2: the Plumbob Japanese house and analog houses) pp. 195–196.

Lindee's reading

“The Nevada Test Site was a theater in which radiation risk was enacted, with houses made from materials unrelated to life or death in Japan, ‘Japanese’ in only one parameter, shielding capacity. A Nagasaki type bomb then completed the performance.” pp. 195–196

Other programmes bombed American suburban “phantom” houses (ramblers, split-levels, brick homes with refrigerators and canned goods). They are uncanny in Joseph Masco's sense, “blurring the distinction between the animate and the inanimate”. The two-story wood-frame house on Yucca Flat in Operation Teapot had sofas, plates and a toilet. No people were there, but “the houses were occupied by techno-scientific ghosts — future victims of an imagined nuclear apocalypse, represented by sensors”. Avery Gordon: “how do we reckon with what modern history has rendered ghostly?” pp. 196–197

Doses that would not hold still · pp. 197–198

Units multiplied: from the rad to the Gray (1 Gy = 100 rad), plus sieverts, becquerels, rems and curies. Estimates kept shifting. In 1983 William J. Schull wrote that the genetics programme could nearly “demonstrate that man is not mouse-like in his genetic response to radiation … Unfortunately — just when I thought we were close to pinning this down, along comes the dosage reevaluation. We're in ‘hold’ until we have the new doses” n. 26. Kato et al. (1966): the problem “is still troublesome today, after 20 years … for the present we must be content with this range of uncertainty” p. 197.

In 2014 RERF statisticians Eric Grant and Harry Cullings rebuilt the 1945 maps. The old ones came from pre-bombing US Army aerial photographs distorted by camera tilt, handling, lenses and height; digital correction produced a “veridical map — of a detailed ghost city” (Figure 3: an aerial image of Hiroshima before the bombing). All 93,000 survivors changed position: their grid coordinates had been rounded, dropping the last two decimals and pushing people onto grid lines. “People did not move far, but they moved.” The team felt “more confident” n. 27 pp. 197–198.

Lindee's claim, restated

Dosimetry and mapping “require a suite of skills that are humanistic, sociological, quantitative, and technical. What happens over a single day can take decades to unravel and interpret. It must be technically and socially relived, through photos, maps, stories, measurements, archive hunts, and explosions.” p. 198

§5pp. 198–202

New maps of contamination

After the March 2011 tsunami the RERF consulted Fukushima Medical School at once; two RERF epidemiologists attended a preparatory meeting for the Fukushima Health Management Study, followed by a dose-estimation panel, enrolment, simpler questionnaires, response rates and biospecimen plans. In October 2014 the RERF won the contract to study the cleanup workers, still exposed to high doses; the reactor-dismantling roadmap runs four decades n. 28 n. 29 pp. 198–199.

In Hiroshima and Nagasaki the disaster “reanimated old wounds” and sparked an anti-nuclear-energy movement among survivors. “Fukushima-as-Hiroshima” became a trope. A survivor: “We may have underestimated the horrors that nuclear energy could bring about” n. 30. River lanterns at the 66th anniversary carried anti-nuclear-energy messages n. 31. Nihon Hidankyo (about 10,000 members) formally asked the government to eliminate civilian nuclear power p. 199.

“We let them fool us, even in this country that was the victim of the atomic bomb. They convinced us that nuclear power was different from nuclear bombs Fukushima showed us that they are not so different.”

Hirotami Yamada, Hidankyo Nagasaki chapter · n. 32 · p. 199

“Nuclear power and mankind cannot coexist. We survivors of the atomic bomb have said this all along. And yet, the use of nuclear power was camouflaged as ‘peaceful’ and continued to progress.”

Sumiteru Taniguchi, Nagasaki A-Bomb Survivors Council · n. 33 (also Toshiko Tanaka) · p. 199

Survivors demanded health care for those exposed at Fukushima, standing with “new victims” as their own needs had once been ignored p. 199.

Minamisoma City (formerly about 70,000 people, 14 km north of Daiichi) was zoned into five areas: “the difficult-to-return-to area; the not-permitted-to-live-area; the ready-to-be-lifted evacuated area; the specific-spot-recommended-for-evacuation area; and the no-restriction area” n. 34. Space is now defined by human absence, presence and future presence. Minamisoma is “in kinship with Hiroshima, Bikini, Hanford, Chelyabinsk, Semipalatinsk, Sellafield, and Chernobyl. James Acton (Carnegie): “not the worst nuclear accident ever but … the most complicated and the most dramatic”; Brookings: the most costly disaster in history n. 35 n. 36. Fukushima, Iwate and Miyagi were worst hit. The region lives in “slow violence” (Rob Nixon): environmental damage that unfolds slowly and “performs inequality” p. 200.

ICRP economist Jacques Lochard ran “Ethos in Belarus” and, with RERF chairman Niwa Ohtsura, “Ethos in Fukushima”, working household by household n. 39. Rethy Chhem (formerly IAEA Human Health) n. 40: “how do we tell a story that makes sense? … We lose the battle if we talk only numbers” p. 201.

Lindee's conclusion

Many scholars date a “radical break” to the early 1970s, when the planet became “a vast laboratory” n. 41 (Centemeri on Seveso, 1976). The RERF fits as a site where “the structural tensions of a risk society” unfold, providing reassurance and overseeing the “vast laboratory” of nuclear energy. “While I am inclined to see a more radical break in 1945, indeed something new happened for the ABCC as it became RERF” p. 201.

With Boudia, the task is “to untangle the jumbled threads” of US policy, Cold War relations, anti-nuclear mobilisation, demands for precaution and scientific interests. Her thread: an institution that evolved “from a weapons orientation to an economic orientation, from nuclear fear to nuclear hope”, promoting energy critical to Japan. “Citizenship, dosimetry, and the military–industrial–academic complex play a role in many modern disasters.” Knowledge of risk is “dispersed and dappled, like the radioactive materials contaminating prefectures in East Japan”; STS is “obligated to try” to clarify it pp. 201–202.

The article in one breath

After Fukushima, the press assumed that “what we know about Fukushima, we know only from what we understand about Hiroshima.” Why do the Hiroshima–Nagasaki data still dominate? The authors follow two geneticists from opposite sides of the Cold War. James V. Neel (1915–2000) founded the ABCC genetics study and found no demonstrable genetic effect in survivors' children, a finding he defended all his life. Yuri E. Introduction

After Fukushima (March 2011), New York Times journalist Denise Grady wrote that “scientists disagree about the effects of very low doses of the sort that may have occurred in Japan”, citing Evan B. Douple, associate chief of research at the RERF: “The [Hiroshima] survivors received their entire doses all at once to the full body, but exposure from the reactors may be gradual.” The article treated ABCC survivor data as central pp. 67–68. It shared the assumption “that what we know about Fukushima, we know only from what we understand about Hiroshima.” p. 68

Low-dose genetic effects have “vexed scientists since at least 1945” (Lindee 1994). Two opposite camps p. 68:

  • Underestimate: Richardson (2012), Wing et al. (1999), Walker (1994), earlier Gofman (1981) and Bertell (1985), who blamed conflicts of interest in radiation health and health physics.
  • Overestimate: a few scientists and associations, including Roger Clarke, President of the ICRP.

Through the biographies (generation, training, experience, methods) of two well-respected scientists “trained on opposite sides of the Cold War”, the authors explain “how — and with what potential pitfalls — we have come to our current privileging of certain knowledge”. Petryna argued Western scientists dismissed local post-Chernobyl studies as compromised by local politics and economics, worried about future accidents, and shared with Russian authorities an interest in denying pollution. This article studies not local medical science but large international population studies, which are “decidedly ‘not local’” pp. 68–69.

The authors' thesis

“We argue that the elevation of the U.S.-constructed Japan data and research and the relative denigration of the Soviet-constructed Chernobyl data and research in genetic scientific circles during and after the Cold War have co-configured our contemporary understanding of the genetic effects of low-level ionizing radiation exposure.” p. 69

Further, Lysenkoism, the anti-Mendelian genetics dominant in the USSR from the late 1920s to the early 1960s, had “a long-term effect on how American geneticists — including James Neel — perceived Soviet science”. Western commentators use the Lysenko affair as “a metaphor for the politicized and inept qualities of Soviet science”; it marked Neel's formative years and “may have affected Neel's approach to Yuri E. Dubrova” p. 69.

Plan: Neel's formation and ABCC research; Dubrova's formation and Chernobyl research; the disagreement; anthropological and historical analysis. Neel knew his data were imperfect but defended them for life; the aim is to show “the operative positioning of Soviet genetics as a tainted discipline” that informed Neel and kept his studies privileged pp. 69–70.

§2pp. 70–77

James Van Gundia Neel (1915–2000) and Population Genetics

A primary founder of US human genetics (memoir: Physician to the Gene Pool, 1994). Born Hamilton, Ohio, 1915; biology at the College of Wooster; Drosophila (fruit fly) lab of Warren P. Spencer; graduate study with Curt Stern (1902–1981), a German Jewish émigré of 1933 who trained a generation. Building on Hermann Muller's Nobel-winning (1927) work on radiation-induced mutations in Drosophila, Stern concluded there was no safe threshold, supporting what became the linear no-threshold (LNT) model: effect proportional to dose, “with no level being completely harmless”; one hypothesis is that cancer is directly proportional to dose p. 70.

Stern was seen as rigorous, as German scientists were; Neel, his first PhD student, built a reputation for methodological precision. Leaving flies for humans was hard p. 71:

“Human geneticists of the current generation cannot imagine how I agonized over the decision to turn my back on the hard genetics of Drosophila and enter the soft and tainted field of human genetics.… The irony that I was simultaneously fumbling with thoughts of bringing Drosophila-type rigor into genetic studies of that most intractable of all organisms, man, is not lost on me.”

Neel 1994, pp. 18–19 · quoted p. 71

He entered medical school in 1942. As “interim director” of the ABCC in 1947–48 he was charged with “the nature and extent of the genetic effects caused by the atomic bombs”. ABCC publications ran to hundreds, Neel on many, occasionally as sole author. The AEC funded the study and extensions from 1946 to 1997. In 1991 the NAS–NRC published The Children of Atomic Bomb Survivors: A Genetic Study (eds Neel and Schull), calling their work “the most extensive exercise in genetic epidemiology ever undertaken”. Its “Orientation” chapter still states the current position of the IAEA and UNSCEAR p. 71. Neel stressed the Japanese survivors are the only population exposed to both high and low doses and systematically studied; he omits the Marshall Islanders (not directly hit; data classified until 1994) p. 72.

Human genetics was full of “methodological and technological dynamism and thrill of discovery” in his career. Neel organised the original dataset with his staff and updated it for life, applying the latest statistics and genetic concepts to subsets and reconceptualising parts of the work. He accepted LNT but held that low-dose injury was so improbable it appeared statistically insignificant. ABCC work set standards for public health, worker safety and environmental litigation in both war and energy industries p. 72. No overt variation in mortality among children of exposed parents (Kato et al. 1966) p. 72:

“This failure to demonstrate significant changes in F1 mortality as a function of parental exposure of course cannot be construed as evidence that no genetic effects resulted.… Unless one is willing to argue that man differs from all other forms of life thus far studied, lethal and semilethal mutations … were induced. But the present study provides no evidence for their existence.”

Kato, Schull and Neel 1966, p. 365 · quoted pp. 72–73

So Neel knew mutations probably occurred but were not perceivable by available methods. Across decades of reanalysis “the summary findings never changed”: no serious mutations in the F1 generation. Survivors had high and low doses, unlike Manhattan Project and reactor workers' constant low exposure, yet the Japan line led to the view that harm to future generations “was small or not perceptible” p. 73.

Opponents emerged slowly under Cold War secrecy, in two clusters: post-Chernobyl Soviet and post-Soviet researchers, and US occupational epidemiologists studying nuclear workers. Wing, Richardson and Stewart (1999) list three kinds of A-bomb study (inherited genetic effects, fetal irradiation, the Life Span Study (LSS) of cancer), all from the original ABCC work, and ask why they kept authority when other studies suggested underestimated cancer risk from protracted low doses (Mancuso 1977; Beral 1988; Kneale and Stewart 1995; Morgenstern 1997) p. 73. Wing et al.: “standard-setting bodies continue to rely on the Life Span Study … as the primary epidemiological basis” and “faith in the internal and external validity” shaped worker studies p. 74.

  • Strength: large samples, between 75,991 (Wing 1999) and 86,611 (Ozasa 2012) LSS survivors.
  • Difference: some got high, whole-body gamma and neutron doses, unlike long, low exposure.
  • Most critically: the LSS began five years after the bombings and was treated as a “natural experiment” though it contained only survivors: the “healthy survivor” effect stressed by physician-epidemiologist Alice Stewart (1906–2002). Neither the LSS nor worker studies are representative. Yet longitudinal power made the A-bomb studies the “gold standard” p. 74.

Wing et al. (Stewart was a co-author) show that whenever research challenged the originals, the defence strategy was to compare it and find the challenger “lacking in some fundamental manner”. Wing et al.: A-bomb studies kept “a predominant role … despite their focus on an extreme exposure situation, evidence of selective survival, and unresolved questions about inaccuracy of dose estimates”, while occupational studies (low doses, individual dose badges, no reliance on interviews, no survival selection) “have been kept in the background” pp. 74–75.

The authors' added dimension

They agree “research delegitimation also takes place”, but add: “The historical stigmatization of Soviet science and genetics that had begun much earlier made Soviet-based scientific studies later challenging the A-bomb research more easily dismissible.” p. 75

Archival evidence. On 8 July 1959 Neel wrote to Theodosius Dobzhansky (“esteemed Ukrainian Drosophila geneticist”, critic of Lysenkoism) about the pirating of his textbook with Jack Schull. A Romanian wanted to translate it and mentioned a Russian translation, as of Stern's: “Needless to say, it came as a bit of a surprise to us to find that we might possibly in some small way contribute to Brother Lysenko's discomfiture.” This is “a partial basis” for the claim that Neel would view Soviet science and Chernobyl studies with skepticism p. 75.

The 1950s pressure. Wing et al.: the government worried about insurance claims, labour relations and public hostility to the nuclear industry; Walker calls it an anxious time. In 1953 Eisenhower's “Atoms for Peace” sought to placate opinion and redirect research to peaceful uses, unveiling nuclear power; “a vital component” was reassuring the public that radiation risks were “acceptable”. Pressure to report little genetic damage was real (Lindee 1994) and Neel “was not immune”, yet he insisted, “it was science, not politics, that prevailed” pp. 75–76. Wing et al.: the studies had the momentum of the half-century, amid “secrecy, deference to authority, and even disdain for workers”; Bertell: standards aimed at an “acceptable” trade-off, not protecting health. The authors add that the studies' “momentum” also “benefited from the decrepit state of Soviet science in the 1950s” p. 76.

By 1996 the Cold War was over and the nuclear industry established; Chernobyl had happened; Neel faced a challenge from the East. In a rare single-authored 1998 PNAS paper, “Genetic Studies at the Atomic Bomb Casualty Commission – Radiation Effects Research Foundation: 1946–1997”, he reaffirmed the ABCC studies and confronted Dubrova, whose populations (workers and civilians with varying acute doses then continuous low-level exposure) resembled worker studies and were likewise “deemed inferior to the LSS work” pp. 76–77.

Yuri E. Dubrova (1955–) and Population Genetics

Yuri Evgenjevitch Dubrova grew up in Kiev, studied biology at Kiev State University, and finished his PhD in 1982 at the Institute of General Genetics, Moscow, under Yuri Petrovich Altukhov (1936–2006; director 1992–2006). Altukhov, nearer Neel's generation, had already “exchanged intellectual fire” with Neel; he headed a new Laboratory of Population Genetics as Mendelian genetics was reborn after “the Stalinist silencing”. His work on mutations and environmental pollutants brought the clash p. 77.

In 1979 Nikolay Petrovich Dubinin (leading Soviet radiation geneticist, director of the Soviet Institute of Genetics, who argued congenital defects were rising from mutagenic pollutants, radiation included) and Altukhov published in PNAS: “Gene Mutations (de novo) Found in Electrophoretic Studies of Blood Protein of Infants with Anomalous Development” fn 1 (even today, whether pollutants raise germinal mutations that pass to offspring “remains a matter of debate”). Under a Soviet-American environmental cooperation programme, both sides sought ways to separate types of “genetic load” and link new mutations in offspring to environmental mutagens pp. 77–78.

The 1979 paper alarmed North American biologists (Coulthart 1984). Neel and Harvey W. Mohrenweiser replied in 1984 with the “pugnaciously titled” “Failure to Demonstrate Mutations Affecting the Protein Structure or Function in Children with Congenital Defects or Born Prematurely”: cause and effect were unproven, and the Soviets never described their population's full composition, a prerequisite of population-genetics rigour. Altukhov's Population Genetics: Diversity and Stability (1990) again tied pollution to inherited mutation: in irradiated fruit flies, induced mutations at enzyme loci were “approximately 10 times higher in larvae than it was in adult flies” p. 79.

Dubrova, a research fellow at the Institute (1981–1995), trained East and West. In the late 1980s he learned of British geneticist Alec Jeffreys's minisatellites, used for forensic DNA profiling, and adapted them for mutation monitoring. With Wellcome Trust funding in 1994 he took Chernobyl blood samples from Moscow to Jeffreys's lab at the University of Leicester and showed a higher germline mutation rate in irradiated parents pp. 79–80. Amid the exodus of Soviet geneticists he took a Leicester fellowship, then a professorship; his research covers germline mutation from radiation, chemical mutagens and anticancer drugs, and whether exposed parents' children also produce mutations. He is “one of the very few Soviet geneticists who have emerged from the shadows of Cold War politics and become successful in the West”, because most Soviet science was paralysed after 1991 and many labs closed p. 80.

The Mid-Twentieth-Century Context of Soviet and North American Genetics

Genetics as a discipline grew from the interwar International Genetics Congresses, but by the mid-1930s Soviet, German and American governments had subordinated science to nationalist ideology; Lysenko and the Cold War split scientists into camps p. 80.

Trofim Lysenko Not in G&S (first name), “a Ukrainian peasant who rose quickly through the communist party ranks”, declared evolution Lamarckian (inheritance of acquired characteristics) and genes “imaginary”. By the early 1930s followers attacked Mendelian genetics as a “bourgeois perversion of science”; Lysenko said Mendelism was “developed in Western capitalist countries not for agricultural purposes but for the reactionary eugenics, racism and other purposes”. Critics were arrested, imprisoned or executed. The affair was devastating for all science except physics pp. 80–81.

  • 1948: with Stalin's endorsement, all genetics research officially forbidden, “an illegal discipline”.
  • Yet NKVD secret prison laboratories, Sharashka, did nuclear-project research including genetics. Laboratory B in Sungul held geneticist Nikolay Timofeev-Ressovsky and German nuclear chemist Nikolaus Riehl, studying radiation's genetic effects on plants and animals and the handling of radioactive materials.
  • 1964: the ban lifted after hundreds of scientists opposed Khrushchev's support of Lysenko.
  • Soviet scientists then sporadically published on radiation (Medvedev 1976), rarely in the West because of censorship; exchange programmes helped, but large-scale collaboration resumed only after 1991 p. 81.

The 1958 US–Soviet cooperation agreement brought exchanges distorted by ideology, especially in biology (Geltzer 2012). US delegates saw no use in them except altruism; they called Soviet research isolated, unimaginative and propagandistic, “outside of the channels of world medical developments”, its methods archaic and labs unexciting. A 1959 American Radiobiology Mission: “some of the work seems very good, some very bad, and most of it quite pedestrian” pp. 81–82.

American geneticists enjoyed full government support (Manhattan Project, AEC, ABCC, RERF), but Cold War contingencies shaped them too. Barbara Rose Johnston: the Cold War “not only resulted in overt efforts to keep information from the public (and therefore to deceive and lie to the public), but also generated biases that skewed scientific research from inception to conclusions” (also Welsome, Masco, Gusterson, Hecht). The framework's “conception of Soviet biomedical science … endures”. After 1991 labs were dismantled and funding lost p. 82.

The authors' conclusion to the section

“The resultant disarray of Soviet genetics research and the corresponding Western sense of scientific superiority helps explain how the findings of Neel and his colleagues have long remained privileged and unharmed — in a word, authoritative — in spite of serious challenges from other research scientists.” p. 82

§5pp. 82–83

The Recent Controversy: Dubrova et al. Versus Neel et al.

On the eve of Chernobyl's tenth anniversary Nature published “Human Minisatellite Mutation Rate after the Chernobyl Accident” (Dubrova et al. 1996), contradicting the ABCC findings. Dubrova later found the controversy surprising given evidence that accepted views “may significantly underestimate deleterious effects of mutagens”, notably radiation-induced genomic instability, where high mutation rates appear “not only in the directly irradiated cells, but also during a long time in their unirradiated offspring” (Dubrova 2006). Traditional ABCC-type studies look only at “transmission of de novo mutations to the offspring” pp. 82–83.

The 1996 Nature and 2002 Science papers (the latter after Neel's death) are landmarks; 1996 was the first experimental evidence of radiation-induced increases in human germ cell mutation rates, in exposed parents and their unexposed offspring. Neel replied in two single-authored papers (1996, 1999a) p. 83. Four interrelated issues:

  1. the meaning of minisatellite mutations and junk DNA as indicators;
  2. the comparability of animal and human studies;
  3. measuring the doubling dose in humans;
  4. the choice of control groups.
§6pp. 83–85

Issue 1: Minisatellite Mutations and Junk DNA as Indicators

Low-dose research now concentrates on minisatellite mutations, “junk DNA”. Dubrova's 1996 study compared germline mutations in parents and children in heavily polluted Belarus: the rate was twice as high in children (F1) of Chernobyl-exposed parents as in controls pp. 83–84.

Neel's objections: junk DNA's spontaneous mutation rate is known to be high; in the late 1990s neither its mutation mechanism nor its function was understood. He reasserted the A-bomb studies and cited contrary Chernobyl liquidator studies (Kodaira 1995; Mohrenweiser unpublished). What “counts” as a mutation: Neel (1996) said these were not phenotypically detectable. In “Two Recent Radiation-Related Genetic False Alarms: Leukemia in West Cumbria, England, and Minisatellite Mutations in Belarus” (1999a) he accused Dubrova of bringing “unwarranted distress to an already badly stressed population”, a point he and Schull had made in 1956 and 1991 p. 84.

“While Neel does not believe that political concerns played any role in the selection of what signs counted as mutations at Hiroshima and Nagasaki, he acknowledges that social impact did play a role. A trait that would not ‘upset the new mothers of Hiroshima,’ he has explained, should not count as a mutation. … In cases lacking any compelling reason to decide otherwise, traits that were socially dangerous were classified as major and those without social implications as minor — essentially, as not mutations.”

Lindee 1994, pp. 191–192 · quoted p. 84: Neel's definition still “influenced by the need to assuage Cold War anxieties”

Why Dubrova used minisatellites: protein-coding genes mutate rarely and need huge samples; genomic instability is shown in somatic cells but there is little evidence in germ cells. Minisatellites mutate often, spontaneously and when induced, allowing “mutation induction to be evaluated at low doses of exposure in very small population samples” (Dubrova 1998). As of 2012 the link between minisatellite mutation and disease was unclear, though ENCODE scientists (about thirty articles, September 2012) suggested junk DNA may be vital to gene regulation and disease risk. Studies report increases in exposed human and animal germlines, but “there is no agreement as to the significance” p. 85.

Why no heightened minisatellite mutations in Hiroshima–Nagasaki survivors (Kodaira 1995)? Dubrova (2003): (1) smaller doses; (2) too few people studied; (3) no distinction between paternal and maternal exposure. His studies of Chernobyl children and children near the Semipalatinsk test site found “significant associations between paternal (but not maternal) exposure and increased mutation rates” (Wyrobek 2007). But Susanne Bauer (2010) finds junk DNA hard to turn into a biodosimetry tool: poorly understood function, an unsatisfactory marker, a point Neel also made (1996) p. 85.

Issue 2: Extrapolating from Animal to Human Studies

The Manhattan Project (1942–46) spawned an industry of toxic radioactive materials and a need for standards, with data from national labs such as Oak Ridge and Brookhaven. After 80 years of animal research, “neither ionizing radiation nor chemical exposure has been confirmed as a germ-cell mutagen in humans” (Wyrobek 2007); for decades this was put down to biological difference p. 86.

Early knowledge came from Drosophila. From 1947 Oak Ridge's Mammalian Genetics programme under William and Liane Russell ran the “mega-mouse” study, irradiating hundreds of thousands of male mice; damage might be higher than thought (Russell to Shields Warren, January 1951). Radiation and chemicals caused heritable mutations in mouse germ cells, but human studies failed to show the same. Mouse data nevertheless became the basis of national exposure standards, though extrapolation is hard given “differences in absorption, metabolism, and excretion” (Brent 2004) p. 86.

Neel on mice: with Susan Lewis (1990): “humans may not be as sensitive to the genetic effects of radiation as has been projected … on the basis of data from … the laboratory mouse”. In 1996 he called mouse and A-bomb data “conservative”: “a major revision downward in the previous estimates of the genetic risks of radiation for humans must be very seriously considered”. Mice seem more sensitive, and lab design distorts: a few irradiated males impregnating many females, a single inbred line, the immature mouse fetus at birth, a male-based model pp. 86–87. 1998 PNAS: “yes, there are genetic risks … but current national and international recommendations … now can be seen as incorporating an even wider margin of safety” p. 87.

Faced with Dubrova's far greater sensitivity, Neel wondered about parallel mouse experiments but concluded “at this level of genetic resolution, there is no need for animal experimentation”, except lower-dose work matching Japan, about which he was also unenthusiastic p. 87.

The authors' reading

Neel's rejection “makes sense given Neel's own early involvement and training in Drosophila genetics; it also makes sense given Neel's interest in defending the ABCC work and in reassuring the public about genetic risk after Hiroshima.” But the 2006 NRC report (BEIR VII) found no direct human evidence yet noted harm in animals and “no reason to believe that humans would be immune to this sort of harm”: animal research “cannot be readily dismissed” p. 87.

Rosalie Bertell (1985) read Muller's fly work as foreshadowing reduced human survival over generations, ignored by an AEC bent on underplaying harm. In No Immediate Danger she cites a 1959 AEC report: fallout from tests in September 1958 alone would cause 100,000 major birth defects, 380,000 stillbirths or infant deaths and 900,000 fetal deaths “in the thousands of years to come”. She doesn't say how many are genetic, but saw “a willful mission of the AEC to ignore the dangers” pp. 87–88.

§8pp. 88–89

Issue 3: Doubling Dose Estimates in Human Populations

The doubling dose is the radiation needed to double the natural mutation rate. Neel called it a statistical construct, “a convenient metric”, but “in an imperfect world, the doubling dose concept supplies a perspective, if blurred, difficult to obtain by any other approach”. After about 50 years: an acute gonadal exposure of about 2.0 Sv equivalents, a “conservative” estimate p. 88. His reasons (Teratology 1999a; PNAS 1998):

  • proximally exposed parents had lower socioeconomic status than controls;
  • cousin marriage (and so, by implication, mutation) is high in the bombed region: 6% of newborns in Hiroshima, 8% in Nagasaki;
  • human and Drosophila data are not comparable;
  • human and mouse doubling doses are hard to compare.

He defended the ABCC estimate until his death, and answered Dubrova sharply: he read Dubrova as implying the doubling dose for chronic exposure showed “two hundred times greater sensitivity in humans than the literature suggested”. Dubrova (2006): “ample evidence … suggests that the views, currently accepted in radiation genetics, may significantly underestimate deleterious effects”. Many scientists, including those in the NYT Fukushima article and the LSS defenders cited by Wing, still stand by the ABCC data pp. 88–89.

§9p. 89

Issue 4: Control Group Choice in Human Population Studies

For Chernobyl, Dubrova used parents and children from Great Britain as controls because no suitable unirradiated ethnic population existed nearby. Neel: “a violation of one of the first principles of genetic epidemiology, since these controls are neither matched by environment nor by ethnicity” p. 89.

In 2002 Dubrova repeated the study among offspring of parents heavily exposed by Soviet nuclear testing in Kazakhstan, a design that “addressed all of Neel's critiques”. Fallout from the mid-1940s to early 1950s doubled the germline mutation rate. A scientist at England's National Radiological Protection Board: it “provides the most convincing evidence to date of heritable mutations induced in humans following parental exposure to ionising radiation” (Bouffler and Lloyd 2002) p. 89.

Balonov, Soviet-trained radiobiologist and IAEA consultant, wrote that “intervention of incompetent people, although having academic titles, in this delicate process prevents adequate public information and decision making” (clearly meaning Yablokov). Balonov was among the first Soviet Chernobyl researchers; sent to calm an anxious crowd fearing cover-up, he appeared with “a government scientist, who, along with his pregnant wife, would be living and working in the contaminated area” pp. 90–91.

In 2012 Jargin (People's Friendship University of Russia, Moscow) argued in Mutation Research that Dubrova's families' doses were too low to explain the increases. Dubrova's stern reply: “Where has the author been before?”, implying a neophyte p. 91.

§11pp. 91–93

Conclusions

Anthropologist Barbara Rose Johnston (2011): after the tsunami, the government and TEPCO reported minor damage and said “venting of hydrogen gas” posed “no threat to health”, echoing the Cold War line that “low-level exposure to radiation represents no human threat”, a statement now seen as serving “to ‘meet government and industry needs’” p. 91.

The authors' conclusions

Reassuring discourses have intellectual histories, and so do journal debates that “replicate and embody the epistemological preferences for particular forms of method and experimentation established in the early twentieth century.” “Both James Neel and Yuri Dubrova are products of political and scientific milieux.” Following Latour's “following scientists around”, research on low-dose genetic effects has been “‘co-configured’ by scientific advances and sociopolitical context”; “a set of interests” is at play (Latour, Woolgar, Fujimura) pp. 91–92.

Neel's status let him help set standards for workers and the public; funding and prestige let him build the statistical models that became the gold standard, “while other scientific methods and approaches were dismissed as substandard”. Lysenkoism had already shaped that narrative. Unresolved findings-in-process became consequential in courtrooms p. 92:

  • Neel and Schull (1991b) note a Hanford study used Japan data to dismiss links between fathers' employment and congenital defects.
  • Their final chapter defends the industry over childhood leukemia near Sellafield (Seascale, West Cumbria). “Neel felt both equipped and compelled” to defend existing standards and opine on worker safety pp. 92–93.
  • Historian Kate Brown on 1990s suits by Hanford downwinders against five contractors: “Scientists drawing on American-directed studies of Japanese bomb survivors narrowed the field of inquiry to a few carcinomas and thyroid disease. Downwinders, however, connected their sheep born without eyes to birth defects in their children.” The thyroid study ignored genetic effects and the “chronic radiation syndrome” discussed by Russian scientists, “never documented in the U.S. studies” p. 93.

Final line: the Japan studies, “what they found and what they failed to find”, still matter in journals and courtrooms “that still rely on Hiroshima and Nagasaki atomic bomb data as the standard against which all other information is compared” p. 93.

Quick revision

The week on one screen

The argument in ten lines

  1. Knowledge of radiation harm was made by states and scientists who chose what to measure, and ignorance was produced as deliberately as knowledge (Higuchi's “compartmentalized production of knowledge”).
  2. From 1945 to 1953 the US, USSR and UK treated off-site fallout as an “emergency”: act only above 25–200 R, and monitor only near the site during the test.
  3. The same fallout was valued as a harmless tracer for spying on tests and for modelling world nuclear war (Gabriel, Sunshine), including through bones gathered worldwide under “nuclear paternalism”.
  4. In Japan the US first denied radiation effects, then censored them (Press Code to 1952); Japan's own government stayed silent until 1957.
  5. The ABCC examined but never treated survivors, and denied genetic effects. Survivors felt like “guinea pigs”, and women carried the double stigma of death and blood.
  6. Relief laws made hibakusha patients and welfare cases, called their fear “A-bomb neurosis”, and turned the bomb into “an event without actors”, so neither state paid compensation.
  7. The ABCC's 1975 rebirth as the RERF was driven by money and nuclear power. Its leaders' passports became credentials, and survivors became stand-ins for reactor workers.
  8. Dosimetry is humanistic reenactment: survivor walk-throughs, phantom Japanese houses bombed in Nevada, maps redrawn in 2014.
  9. The Hiroshima data became the gold standard partly because Soviet genetics was discredited (Lysenko). Neel's “no demonstrable effect” beat Dubrova's doubled minisatellite rates.
  10. Fukushima joined bombs and reactors: survivors turned against nuclear power, and Minamisoma joined a “nuclear club” defined by contamination.

Authors' verdicts

  • Higuchi: the three rivals “jointly established the dominant definition” of off-site contamination as harmless; their “definitional power” was fragile and broke after Castle Bravo in 1954.
  • Todeschini: biomedicine and law suppressed the moral question (were the bombings “beneficial” or crimes against humanity?). The bomb is “a weapon of mass destruction … instantaneously and over time”.
  • Lindee: the RERF moved “from a weapons orientation to an economic orientation, from nuclear fear to nuclear hope”. Citizenship, dosimetry and the military–industrial–academic complex shape modern disasters.
  • Goldstein & Stawkowski: both men are “products of political and scientific milieux”. The Japan data remain “the standard against which all other information is compared”, in court too.

Traps to avoid

  • Higuchi does not say officials ignored fallout. They tracked it globally, but as intelligence and not as a health issue.
  • The HASL network came from Kodak's complaints, “not any concern about public health”.
  • Todeschini blames both US and Japanese science. She rejects the national narrative that only “Americans” appropriated hibakusha.
  • Todeschini does not say women's fears were baseless: reproductive cancers were real (p. 74).
  • Lindee's “biological citizenship” is Petryna's term, and Lindee reverses it.
  • Lindee: the RERF name was opposed for hiding the bomb, while utilities wanted the bomb association kept strong.
  • Lindee dates the radical break to 1945, not the 1970s.
  • Neel accepted LNT, and he admitted mutations were probably induced but undetectable.
  • Goldstein & Stawkowski add the Lysenko factor to Wing et al.'s account; they don't replace it. They also call Yablokov's number an outlier.
  • The article has two authors: Goldstein and Stawkowski.

The course lens, answered

1 · Whose order, whose justice?

Higuchi: the order of deterrence (“national welfare”, Bugher) over the justice of protection for peripheral people: ranchers, Kazakhs, Aboriginal Australians.

Todeschini: Japan's order of reconstruction and its “antinuclear spirit” over the justice of compensation. Victims must “endure” a “general sacrifice”.

Lindee: binational order in the RERF's governance; justice claimed by survivors for Fukushima evacuees.

G&S: an epistemic order: the US data rank above the Soviet data. Justice denied in court to downwinders.

2 · Which actors?

Higuchi: the three states and their scientists; a few dissenters (Borst, Messel); affected people mainly as evidence.

Todeschini: hibakusha women first, against ABCC scientists, doctors and the Japanese state.

Lindee: an institution between states (NAS, AEC, Japanese ministries, IAEA/WHO) and survivors, now allied with other exposed groups.

G&S: geneticists on both sides of the Cold War; critics; downwinders.

3 · What makes something “nuclear”?

In all four, radiation harm exists only through what is measured and counted. Higuchi's thresholds make distant fallout “harmless”. Todeschini's women must prove a “biological basis”. Lindee's radiation is knowable only through “formal representations”, and Minamisoma is “nuclear” by contamination. For G&S, what “counts as a mutation” was decided, and so were the controls. This is close to Hecht's nuclearity as a contested category Link to week 4 thesis.

Higuchi · A State of Emergency

1What single framing does Higuchi say all three nuclear powers shared, and what did it lead them to protect against?

They defined off-site exposure as an “emergency”, so they concentrated on the well-known danger of large doses delivered quickly (acute injury), not on low, widespread, long-term exposure. pp. 17, 39

2What does Higuchi mean by the “compartmentalized production of knowledge”, and what did it produce?

Public-health monitoring (near the site, during tests, external dose only) was kept apart from secret intelligence monitoring of global fallout. The split “systematically sustained and deepened ignorance” about widespread and long-term effects. p. 18

3Why does the chapter open with Kodak's X-ray film?

Fall 1945: spots caused by cerium-141 from Trinity, carried by wind and rain into the Wabash River and a Vincennes paper mill's strawboard. One event left a near-instant global footprint, “a historic moment in the Anthropocene”. p. 16

4Name the two Trinity decisions that “set the pattern for U.S. off-site fallout policy”.

(1) No protective action short of clearly injurious levels: an evacuation trigger of 75 R in two weeks, about 50 times the 0.1 R daily limit. (2) Monitoring confined to the vicinity and the test period, measuring external radiation only. p. 20

5What did Hempelmann later say about the ranchers exposed after Trinity?

“A few people were probably overexposed, but they couldn't prove it and we couldn't prove it. So we just assumed we got away with it.” The canyon ranches got up to about 47 R. p. 21

6What inspired the idea of long-range detection of nuclear tests, and how sensitive was the Air Force system?

The 1883 Krakatoa eruption and the Royal Society's report on it (Magee and Turkevich). The Atomic Energy Detection System alerted at 50 cpm, close to background, and had 111 false alarms before it caught the 1949 Soviet test. pp. 21–22

7Why did the US build its first permanent fallout network across North America?

Because of complaints from the photographic industry (Kodak threatened to sue), “not any concern about public health”. Eisenbud's HASL network had about 100 stations by 1952. pp. 23–24

8What two grounds did the USAEC give in 1953 for saying fallout was safe?

(1) The maximum permissible dose: an occupational NCRP standard of 3 R per ten weeks. (2) Detectability: the doubling dose was “1,600 times higher” than the 50 milliR lifetime exposure near the site. p. 25

9What did Lyle Borst challenge, and how was he answered?

“The philosophy implied”: that being below tolerance values means safe. He pointed to pregnant women and children. Bugher replied that tests serve “national welfare”; Bradbury said an atomic war “would be fantastically worse”. p. 26

10What was Project Sunshine for, and what did Libby's “nuclear paternalism” mean?

Using existing global Sr-90 contamination as a “radioactive-tracer chemistry experiment on a world-wide scale” to test Gabriel's model of the limits of nuclear war. Paternalism: the US as caretaker of humanity because it could kill it (“assay the world, not our own backyard”). pp. 27–29

11How was Sunshine's sampling carried out abroad?

Through hard power (embassy yards, Lyle Alexander's tours, Navy seawater, the NRL's 22 stations on the 80th meridian west) and soft power (Rockefeller mission hospitals, AFIP bones from Japan, Kulp's missionary doctor in South India; 26 people in 19 countries by April 1956). pp. 29–30

12What happened around RDS-6 in 1953?

The first Soviet fusion-boosted bomb (400 kt, 12 August 1953). Evacuation above 200 R used a translated US civil defense manual; 2,253 people were moved; Abai's 191 residents got 10–40 R; evacuees returned after two weeks at about 1,000 times normal rates. Vasilevskii: “twenty or thirty deaths can be considered normal.” pp. 34–35

13What was the “black mist”, and when was it officially acknowledged?

After the first Totem shot (Emu, October 1953) a black mist reportedly reached the Yankunytjatjara community of Wallatina, about 100 miles away, making residents acutely sick. The 1985 Royal Commission concluded exposure was likely, with temporary illness. p. 39

Todeschini · Illegitimate Sufferers

14State Todeschini's thesis in one sentence.

“Biomedicine,” deployed by both Japanese and American scientific and political communities, became “a hegemonic force in shaping definitions of hibakusha”, repressing and denying victims' experiences, needs and moral questions. p. 68

15What is her evidence base?

Hibakusha narratives, especially women's: published autobiographies, grassroots publications, oral testimony and her own interviews in Hiroshima and elsewhere (1991–93). Chiefly Hayashi Kyōko, the Osaka Association of Female A-bomb Victims and the Yamashita Group (both 1967). p. 69

16Which “national narrative” does she correct about the early period?

That only “Americans” appropriated hibakusha scientifically. Japanese survey teams entered Hiroshima on 6 August and autopsies began on 10 August, and Japanese scientists were just as interested, while the Japanese state did nothing for victims until 1957. p. 70

17What was the Press Code and what did it do?

US censorship of bomb and radiation information deemed disruptive to “public peace and morals”, in force until the 1952 San Francisco Treaty. It kept hibakusha from learning about their exposure, censored accounts like Ōta Yōko's, blocked medical information among local doctors, and sent data to the US as “top secret”. p. 71

18Why were female hibakusha seen as “doubly dangerous”?

They combined the impurity of death (radiation) with that of blood (reproduction), both polluting in Japanese belief. This drove marriage discrimination; keloids and leukemia worked as signs of “contaminated blood”. pp. 72–73

19Does Todeschini treat women's fears about reproduction as imaginary?

No. Reproductive cancers (ovarian, uterine, breast) were abnormally high. And scientific scrutiny of their menstruation and fertility itself “sent the implicit message that there was a problem”. p. 74

20How does she interpret folk remedies and the idea of radiation as a “poison”?

They cut radiation's mystery down “to the manageable, human size of impurities”, and served as an embodied memory and “a form of resistance” against official denial of understanding and legitimacy. p. 75

21Why, on her account, did the ABCC refuse to treat survivors, and how did victims experience it?

Treatment would have looked like “atonement” and an admission of guilt, delegitimising the bombing (citing Lindee). Victims felt like “guinea pigs”, examined, X-rayed and autopsied but never told results. “Why as a victim, do I help the aggressor?” pp. 76–77

22Give two examples of the ABCC shaping genetics findings.

Selective definition of mutations (only those “threatening for the future survival of the species”); a 1953 report discounting stillbirth and sex-ratio effects “to avoid misinterpretation by the popular press”; the 1955 headline “Thousands of Babies, No A-bomb Effects”; the 1992 chief of research: “science is nothing else but a public consensus”. p. 79

23What was “Atomic Bomb Stress”?

The term of a former ABCC doctor (1955 unpublished report, fn 39, Naglehurst) who found patients “live in fear” and that the ABCC was “completely unaware”. His request for six more months was denied. p. 81

24Why does Todeschini reject “A-bomb neurosis”?

Neurosis implies intrapsychic conflict, whereas hibakusha anxieties rest on “devastatingly ‘real’” experience. The label individualises suffering, cuts it from its political context and makes fear irrational; “the psychiatric idiom is not necessarily more humane”. pp. 86–87

25What does it mean that relief laws turned the bomb into “an event without actors”?

By treating suffering as “just another disease” with medical and welfare allowances, the state avoided moral reflection on war responsibility and on compensation. The 1980 report: soldiers are “rewarded”, civilians must “endure” a “general sacrifice”. pp. 91–93

26What does the coda's 1978 remark reveal, and what is her closing image?

An assemblyman proposed using the Eugenics law to “wipe out hibakusha”, also “better … for government finances”: science and law used to eliminate. Hibakusha are “the wastebins of our collective imagination”, carrying radioactive pollution (Zonabend's “eternal impurity”) “on behalf of all of us”. pp. 94–95

Lindee · Survivors and Scientists

27Why is the RERF trusted, and why distrusted?

Trusted: large, unselected, mixed-sex, all-age sample, relatively high doses, extensive dosimetry, long follow-up. Distrusted: inattention to low doses, residual radiation on early entrants, black rain and internal exposure (Sawada's “cover-up”; Alice Stewart). “Both trusted and not trusted.” pp. 186–187

28What are Lindee's three claims?

(1) Dosimetry “is a fundamentally humanistic exercise critical to all risk assessment”. (2) Disaster epidemiology produces forms of biological citizenship, with citizenship as a credential. (3) The division between military and civilian knowledge does not hold up. p. 187

29What does “scientific at both ends” mean (Beck)?

Nuclear problems are made by science (elite physics, chemistry, engineering) and must be solved by science, including the messy, slow back end of epidemiology and social work. p. 185

30Who created and funded the ABCC, and how “joint” was it?

Truman approved it after the 1946 Joint Commission report; the NAS organised it and the AEC funded it (a “shotgun” wedding). The JNIH alliance of March 1948 made it nominally joint, but Japan paid about 10% until 1975 and leadership was always American. p. 189

31What was the Master Sample?

The Francis Committee's 1955 fixed cohort from the 1950 census: 71,900 proximally exposed (including in utero), about 30,000 remotely exposed and 40,000 controls, about 150,000 people followed “forever”. pp. 189–190

32Why was the ABCC reorganised into the RERF, and what was the “ironic trade”?

Mainly money: the budget more than doubled 1963–73, and in 1972 the US paid ¥1.25 bn to Japan's ¥74 m. The AEC linked a $320 million nuclear fuel sale to Japanese support for the research that would evaluate that technology's risks. pp. 190–191

33Who opposed the name “RERF”, and why did utilities care?

The Japan Radiation Research Society and Japan Science Council opposed it for putting “the A-bomb under cover”. Utilities wanted to keep radiation effects associated with bombs, not with nuclear power; Nader's Hiroshima visit aimed at the opposite. pp. 191–192

34How does Lindee's citizenship argument reverse Petryna?

Petryna's Chernobyl citizens use their biology to claim political status (biological citizenship). Lindee's experts use their political status (Japanese or US nationality, fixed in the RERF's articles) to make claims about biology. p. 193

35What did Shigematsu say about dosimetry?

“More than half of all the efforts made in Hiroshima and Nagasaki during the past 45 years … has been devoted to the dosimetry problem.” He said it as chair of the IAEA–WHO Chernobyl advisory committee (1990–91). p. 193

36Why was early dosimetry so hard?

Bomb radiation data were secret even from the AEC-funded ABCC. Early leukemia papers used distance, not dose. The Hiroshima gun-type bomb was used only once. Sakae Shimizu's Kyoto team's measurements were confiscated by the Occupation and never found. pp. 194–195

37What were Ichiban and the Plumbob houses?

An Oak Ridge programme (1956) that re-enacted the bombings in Nevada with “phantom” Japanese houses (bamboo, seaweed plaster, roof tiles, oyster shells, later cement–asbestos board), loaded with 120 sensors and bombed in 1957 to produce T57D. “A theater in which radiation risk was enacted.” pp. 195–196

38What changed when Grant and Cullings re-mapped the survivors in 2014?

Corrected pre-bombing aerial photographs and restored decimals moved all 93,000 survivors off the grid lines. “People did not move far, but they moved.” p. 198

39How did Fukushima change survivor politics, and what is Minamisoma's “nuclear club”?

Survivors, silent on energy until then, opposed nuclear power (Hidankyo's appeal; Yamada: “not so different”; Taniguchi: “cannot coexist”). Minamisoma, zoned into five areas, joins Hiroshima, Bikini, Hanford, Chelyabinsk, Semipalatinsk, Sellafield and Chernobyl in a club “characterized not by military power but by contamination”. pp. 199–200

Goldstein & Stawkowski · Neel and Dubrova

40State the authors' main thesis.

The elevation of the US-constructed Japan data and the denigration of the Soviet-constructed Chernobyl data “have co-configured our contemporary understanding” of low-dose genetic effects, helped by the stigma of Lysenkoism on Soviet genetics. p. 69

41What assumption in post-Fukushima reporting do they start from?

That “what we know about Fukushima, we know only from what we understand about Hiroshima” (Grady's NYT article citing Evan Douple of the RERF). p. 68

42Name the four models of low-dose effect.

Linear no-threshold (proportional, no safe level), threshold (low doses harmless), hormesis (small doses beneficial), supralinear (low doses more harmful per unit). p. 70

43Did Neel think radiation caused no mutations in survivors' children?

No. He accepted LNT, and Kato, Schull and Neel (1966) said failure to show effects “cannot be construed as evidence that no genetic effects resulted”; mutations were probably induced but not detectable. Yet the summary finding never changed: no serious F1 mutations. pp. 72–73

44What is the “healthy survivor” effect?

Alice Stewart's critique: the Life Span Study began five years after the bombings and included only those who survived, so it was not a representative “natural experiment”. p. 74

45What do the authors add to Wing, Richardson and Stewart's explanation?

Wing et al. explain the A-bomb studies' authority by design and by delegitimising worker studies. The authors add the historical stigmatisation of Soviet science, which made Soviet challenges “more easily dismissible”. p. 75

46What is the Dobzhansky letter and why does it matter?

Neel's letter of 8 July 1959 about the pirated translation of his textbook with Schull, happy to contribute to “Brother Lysenko's discomfiture”. It is archival evidence for his low regard for Soviet science. p. 75

47How did Dubinin and Altukhov's 1979 method differ from the ABCC's?

Selective screening of premature and disabled infants for rare protein variants, cutting sample size “by more than two orders of magnitude”, versus mass screening of mostly healthy newborns. Neel and Mohrenweiser's reply (1984): “Failure to Demonstrate …”. pp. 78–79

48What was Lysenkoism, and when was genetics banned and unbanned in the USSR?

Lysenko's Lamarckian, anti-Mendelian doctrine (genes “imaginary”; Mendelism a “bourgeois perversion”). Genetics was banned as illegal in 1948 with Stalin's endorsement (though Sharashka labs like Laboratory B in Sungul continued), and the ban was lifted in 1964. pp. 80–81

49What did Dubrova find in 1996, and what were Neel's objections on Issue 1?

The minisatellite mutation rate was twice as high in children of Chernobyl-exposed parents in Belarus. Neel: junk DNA mutates spontaneously at high rates, its mechanism and function were unknown, the changes were not phenotypically detectable, and it was a “false alarm” causing “unwarranted distress”. pp. 84

50What was Neel's doubling-dose estimate, and why did he call it conservative?

About 2.0 Sv (acute gonadal). Reasons: exposed parents' lower socioeconomic status; cousin marriage (6% of newborns in Hiroshima, 8% in Nagasaki); human and fly data not comparable; hard human–mouse comparison. p. 88

51How did Dubrova answer the control-group critique?

Neel said British controls were matched neither by environment nor ethnicity. In 2002 Dubrova studied families exposed to Soviet test fallout in Kazakhstan, a design that “addressed all of Neel's critiques”, and found germline mutation doubled: “the most convincing evidence to date” (NRPB). p. 89

52How do the Chernobyl death estimates range, and how do the authors treat Yablokov?

From 28 acute deaths plus 15 thyroid cancer deaths (Chernobyl Forum) through 112,000–125,000 liquidators (IPPNW) and 500,000 (Ukraine) to 985,000 worldwide (Yablokov). Yablokov “drastically deviates” from mainstream and even non-mainstream consensus. pp. 89–90
