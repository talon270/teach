Masco on Nuclear Fear

Summaries of this week's two readings by the anthropologist Joseph Masco. Both argue that the United States governed its citizens through nuclear fear. The first follows 1950s bunkers and civil defense. The second follows images of ruined American cities from 1955 to the war on terror. Every section, figure and endnote is covered, then a revision kit, an evidence ledger and a self-test.

The week in one breath

Masco reads US civil defense as emotional management rather than protection. After the Soviet bomb (1949) and NSC 68 (1950), planners set out to turn paralysing “nuclear terror” into a usable “nuclear fear”. They made every city a target, staged national rehearsals of the country's own destruction (Operation Alert, Operation Cue) and sold the family fallout shelter as a frontier dream space. The result was a “bunker society” that militarised everyday life and moved responsibility for survival from the state to citizens. Its images came back in The Day After (1983), in the 1998 asteroid blockbusters and in the Bush administration's “mushroom cloud” case for invading Iraq. Masco concludes that Americans still live “between terror and fear”, in “bunkers of the imagination”.

Building the Bunker Society

The article in one breath

The bomb turned the United States into a “bunker society”: a nation fixated on nuclear attack that consoled itself with fantasies of mental and physical fortresses. The early Cold War state chose to manage nuclear fear rather than end it. It hardened its weapons in buried command centres like NORAD, and it hardened its citizens through civil defense, the largest federal media campaign in US history. That campaign taught just enough about nuclear war to win support without causing terror. It sold the family fallout shelter as a frontier dream space for a white, suburban, consuming family. Critics, and Kubrick's Dr. Strangelove, exposed the fantasy, but it never went away. After 9/11 the Bush administration made the bunker a full “national security culture”. Americans now live in “bunkers of the imagination” that feel less secure the more is spent on security. Masco ends by urging Americans to leave the bunker, demilitarise and “re-enter the world in all its bright and messy insecurity” p. 28.

Opening

p. 13 · untitled in the source · the bunker society thesis · Figures 1–2

Claim
Nuclear fear remade everyday America as permanently insecure, and the windowless underground room became the place where state and citizen were remade.
Evidence
FCDA plans for school fallout shelters (Figs 1–2); Herman Kahn's calculations at Rand.
Against
The bunker's promise of an “absolute and total form of security”.

Masco opens with three questions: “What are the long-term psychological consequences of living within a nuclear culture? What fears are now so ingrained in American life that we can't seem to live without them? How, in other words, has nuclear fear remade everyday American society as permanently insecure, even as the United States has become the most powerful military state on earth?” p. 13

Among the “many astonishing cultural achievements of the atomic revolution” he picks one: the transformation of “the underground, windowless room into a site of both global power and social dreaming”. In the nuclear age the room with no view, often buried and hardened against attack, became a place where “futures were both held hostage and re-imagined”. There, “the critical relationship between citizens and the State was remade, reorganized within a crucible of nuclear fear”. The turn inward to built spaces stocked with the latest technologies and commodities offered “a utopian vision of an invulnerable America closed off from the outside world but still functioning perfectly”.

Masco's thesis

“One of the first and most powerful effects of the bomb was to transform the United States into a special kind of bunker society, fixated on impending nuclear attack while fantasizing about life within both mental and physical fortresses.” Presenting life in the bunker as livable, “(even exciting)”, “was a vital mechanism of militarizing American society in the face of an expanding nuclear threat”. It also “set the terms for a long-running American fantasy about achieving an absolute and total form of security” p. 13.

The school shelters (Figures 1 and 2)

Figures 1 and 2 are Federal Civil Defense Administration (FCDA) proposals from the mid-1950s “for the next generation of public schools”, captioned “Group Fallout Shelter for 240 Persons”. The nondescript school building above ground “pales in comparison” to the bunker below, whose carefully diagrammed spaces are filled with bunk beds, escape hatches, offices and an infirmary. The swings and slides above ground “seem no match for the playful imagination of the hidden, windowless spaces below, which promise order, self-sufficiency, and insulation from the universe of danger above”. The plans show nothing of the reality of nuclear war: “the scorched and barren radioactive landscape, or the extreme trauma of life in a postnuclear environment”.

Masco's juxtaposition

Even as civil defense publicly promoted the security of the bunker, Rand Corporation nuclear strategist Herman Kahn “was trying to calculate at what point the ‘survivors would envy the dead’” p. 13.

New Fortresses for the Mind and Body

pp. 13–17 · manage fear, don't end it · Rand 1958 · the citizen-soldier · Gaither 1957

Claim
The state managed nuclear fear instead of removing it. It hardened machines and minds together and made the bunker a site of nation and state building.
Evidence
Rand's 1958 plan to put four million New Yorkers under Manhattan; the Gaither Committee's 1957 shelter proposal.
Against
“Long-standing concepts of ‘security’”, which the bomb's contradictions broke.

The atomic bomb “created fundamental military, social, and psychological contradictions that long-standing concepts of ‘security’ could not resolve” pp. 13–14. Federal authorities therefore “sought to manage nuclear fear rather than eliminate it”, shaping how Americans saw the bomb so they would support “a potentially long Cold War”. The nuclear state embraced the contradictions “by normalizing a nuclear state of emergency and then simply calling the result ‘national security’” p. 14.

By the late 1950s the federal government was “feverishly building thermonuclear weapons and the means to deliver them around the world”. It was also considering “a massive investment in fallout shelters across the United States”, a program “promising an entirely new national infrastructure, all underground”.

Rand's underground Manhattan (1958)

In 1958 the Rand Corporation offered a detailed plan to relocate four million New Yorkers deep underneath Manhattan (Rand 1958:7). Masco quotes it at length:

  • shelters excavated 800 feet below the surface with conventional excavation and mining techniques;
  • almost completely isolated from the surface, with air purified and enriched with oxygen “as in a submarine”;
  • water from the Delaware Aqueduct tunnels, or from internal storage in an emergency, and power from diesel generators vented to the surface;
  • each occupant assigned a berth in a large dormitory, with two cold meals and one hot meal a day, and fresh clothing, showers and exercise on a rotating basis;
  • some 91 entrances, so that every point in Manhattan was within 5 to 10 minutes' walk of one; existing elevators would let about a fourth of the people in a building reach the street every 5 minutes;
  • sloped entrance tunnels with 500-psi blast doors at top and bottom, and provision to collapse any tunnel if its upper door gave way pp. 14–15.

“Isolated from the surface as in a submarine.” This imagined underground city “would be physically realized in the command and control centers for U.S. nuclear forces”. The general public, though, “would focus more on constructing psychological defenses in the nuclear age than on actual shelters”. Nuclear civil defense was “an extraordinarily powerful means of defining the boundaries of both security and threat for the public, while training citizens to think about nuclear war in specific ways” p. 15.

The frontier bunker and the new citizen-soldier

1950s civil defense “formally positioned the bunker as a new American frontier space”, populated by a new kind of citizen “defined by the constant preparation for nuclear attack”. This “new Cold War subject was designed to be immune to panic but nonetheless motivated by nuclear fear”. Just as military technologies were being hardened to survive attack, civil defense “sought to engineer a new kind of citizen-soldier, one who was emotionally equipped to support the nuclear state” pp. 15–16.

Masco's view

“Hardening both technologies and psychologies against the bomb was a dual project of the early nuclear state—making the nuclear bunker a new site of nation and state building.” Stone, steel and concrete turned the windowless bunker into “both a technological challenge and a utopian space”. Through the promise of the bunker, “the logical outcome of nuclear war—the destruction of the nation state in a radioactive firestorm—was denied and a different future horizon opened up” p. 16.

As Americans contemplated life underground, “a new kind of social intimacy with mass death was deeply installed in U.S. national security culture”. The military built “multiply redundant technological systems for fighting a nuclear war (including always-on-alert bombers, missiles, and submarines)”. Civil defense tried to build a society able to withstand “living within a constant state of emergency and facing a new kind of totalizing destructive force”. Cold War planners “explicitly merged nuclear fear with the ideology of American Exceptionalism”, engineering a militarized society “in which America was depicted as both powerful and vulnerable”. That ideology “continues to inform U.S. national security culture to this day”. Raising the bunker into “an icon of state power and social responsibility” prepared Americans psychologically “for escalating militarism” p. 16.

The Cold War state reconceived schools, government buildings and mass transit sites as future shelters. It built “a new infrastructure of buried military facilities” for nuclear weapons systems and for continuity of government. It also recruited private citizens, asking them to build home shelters or “risk death or permanent injury in the coming nuclear conflict”. In this “crosscutting embrace of the bunker as the future of the nation”, a national security culture emerged “that reorganized everyday life as permanent warfare” p. 16.

The Gaither Committee (1957)

In 1957 the Gaither Committee brought together “the leading military-industrial planners in the country” to weigh the benefits of a national fallout shelter program in response to the Soviet nuclear program. The same sentence is the page's pull quote p. 16. The committee:

  • declared “(and invent[ed])” a terrifying “missile gap” between the United States and the Soviet Union, which led to a massive arms buildup under the late Eisenhower and the Kennedy administrations;
  • recommended a crash shelter program costing as much as $55 billion over five years;
  • was explicit about the program's value: shelters would save lives as the bombs fell, and would also communicate to the Soviet leadership an American “will to live”, and so “win, either a cold or hot war” p. 17.
Masco's view

“Civil defense was theatrical as well as practical, a means of sending signals out into the world from underground bunker spaces, both real and imagined. The bunker linked public, private, and military domains in a formal contemplation of nuclear war” p. 17.

Moving Underground

pp. 17–20 · NORAD · the “closed world” · civil defense as emotional training · Figures 3–4

Claim
In the first Cold War decade the nuclear state moved underground and learned to see the world as data on a screen. For citizens, civil defense was emotional training more than protection.
Evidence
NORAD in Cheyenne Mountain (Figs 3–4); Paul Edwards's The Closed World (1996); the civil defense media campaign.
Against
The idea that 1950s civil defense was about protecting citizens and cities.

It is hard today to assess “the incredible energy and creativity that went into building American apocalyptic technologies, and the difficult, ongoing social work of normalizing a permanent war economy in the United States” p. 17. In the first decade of the Cold War, “the nuclear state moved underground”. It relied on a new concept of command and control “that focused not on seeing the world directly but rather on approaching the computer screen as world”. As missiles, radar systems and command centres were buried in hardened facilities across the globe, windowless bunkers came to link earth, sea, air and eventually space “as data points on technologically mediated screens” pp. 17–18.

NORAD

The core example was the North American Aerospace Defense Command (NORAD), deep inside Cheyenne Mountain in Colorado. Authorized in 1958, it tracked all flying objects over North America, a job that mattered more as intercontinental missiles and satellites joined bombers as Soviet military power. Masco calls it “the most advanced bunker facility of its time”, one that “perfectly illustrates the passions of the Cold War nuclear project”:

  • the central facility is buried 2,400 feet deep inside a mountain of almost solid granite;
  • it rests on 1,319 steel springs, each three feet in diameter and weighing more than 1,000 pounds, to absorb the shock of nearby nuclear detonations;
  • it sits behind 25-ton blast doors (Fig. 3);
  • it was networked to radar, computers and eventually satellite surveillance, assembling enormous data sets of moving objects tracked in real time on a giant central screen (Fig. 4) p. 17.

NORAD was “simultaneously the most isolated and the most connected site in the United States”. Its central screen “was the lens for viewing nuclear threat throughout the Cold War”, a tool for orchestrating U.S. military deployments “and achieving Mutual Assured Destruction”. NORAD “was designed as the ultimate panopticon”: crew members, invisible in their hardened bunkers, could watch all of North America and give early warning of attack pp. 17–18.

The closed world

The windowless bunker “provided a new kind of vision”, one that amplified the ability to read dots on a giant screen “as friend or foe in the minute-to-minute orchestration of the balance of nuclear terror”. This “representation of the world as data points is among the most profound technological evolutions of the nuclear age” p. 18.

Masco draws on Paul Edwards (1996): the Cold War concept of Soviet “containment” found a perfect technological metaphor in the computer screen, creating a “closed world” system of “mutually reinforcing ideologies, metaphors, and technologies”. NORAD is “both a technological system and a model of an idealized world”, in which points on the global map are preselected for their value and everything else is ignored. Over time the closed-world vision naturalizes these data points “as the world itself, forgetting the messy complexity of cultures, politics, and ecosystems”. Like the concept of “collateral damage” (the unintended violence of warfare), information that could not become a dot on a screen (“culture, intentions, mistakes, technological malfunctions”) was “rendered invisible in favor of a machine-to-machine assessment” pp. 18–19.

By 1965 NORAD's power “was not only defensive, but it was also offensive in its ability to coordinate the use of the 31,000 nuclear weapons in the U.S. arsenal” p. 19.

Two bunker fantasies

Life in the hardened bunker “quickly became a site of Cold War fantasy for both military leaders and citizens”. For the military, it offered “a vision of the globe as a totally knowable and controllable space”. For citizens, the windowless bunker became “a privatized dream space”, where time spent waiting for the bombs to fall and the radioactive clouds to clear “could be a source of renewal not ruin”. But citizens did not approach the bunker “on their own terms or of their own choosing”. Instead “they were taught how to think about nuclear crisis and their own role in managing it” p. 19.

Masco's view

“The project of ‘civil defense’ in the 1950s was less about the protection of citizens and cities than about the emotional training of the populace, and the psychological conversion of U.S. citizens into Cold War Warriors.” Developed with advertising experts, psychologists and military planners, civil defense was “primarily a means of instilling nuclear fear, and a coded response to it”. It took the form of “the largest federal media campaign in U.S. history”, using newspapers, magazines, radio, television and film. It was designed “to teach U.S. citizens just enough about the dangers of nuclear war to mobilize their support but not enough to produce terror or a public movement to end to the Cold War project” pp. 19–20.

The Bunker as New American Frontier

pp. 20–26 · “Ten for Survival” · FCDA home-shelter photographs · Kennedy · duck and cover · Dr. Strangelove · Figures 5–13

Claim
Civil defense was a national conversation about collective death. It first naturalized and then romanticized the shelter as a frontier dream space for a white, suburban, consuming family.
Evidence
The 1959 pamphlet “Ten for Survival”; FCDA photographs and design concepts (Figs 5–13); Kennedy's 1961 shelter push; Dr. Strangelove.
Against
The FCDA's own best-case imagery, which scientists, activists and Kubrick challenged.
Masco's view

“Cold War civil defense was above all an extraordinary national conversation about collective death” p. 20.

The media campaign forced citizens to consider “a postnational state of being” and, as arsenals grew, the possible end of life on planet Earth. Federal authorities “sought simultaneously to mobilize and naturalize nuclear crisis”. Citizens were taught to fear an imminent global attack “each minute of the day” while being told the threat could be approached “as just another form of potential crisis, alongside floods, fires, and earthquakes”. The domestic form of the “balance of terror” posed “a constant problem of emotional and informational calibration” for Cold War planners p. 20.

“Ten for Survival” (1959)

One widely distributed 1959 civil defense pamphlet, “Ten for Survival: Survive Nuclear Attack”, told readers that “survival” is simply a question of knowing “what to do and how to do it”, and promised that “knowledge is survival power”. It paired that promise with a description of nuclear war “that overwhelms ducking and covering as a mode of protection”:

Dangers facing you: The bomb produced heat of several million degrees—a good deal hotter than the temperature on the surface of the sun. This heat travels at the speed of light. A megaton explosion could kill an unshielded man 8 miles from ground zero. A 20-megaton explosion could kill an unshielded man 20 miles away. It could blister and cripple the bodies of unsheltered people well beyond that.

“Ten for Survival” (1959), quoted at p. 20

“At the speed of light.” Faced with “these gruesome facts”, civil defense authorities sought “first to naturalize, and then to romanticize, shelters”. Life in the bunker was shown as “quintessentially American, a new frontier experience where the resilient citizen could outwit a dangerous world with grit, skill, and moral determination” p. 20.

The FCDA's home-shelter photographs (Figures 5–10)

At the height of the fallout shelter debate, the FCDA produced photographs of ordinary Americans in their home bunkers. They present the shelter as “pure dreamspace”, privatized and part of a pastoral landscape. Each shelter appears in sequence: a ground-level view of the owners descending into the entrance, then a view from the windowless interior. The sequence “underscores the break between the world above and the bunker below”. The hatch “begs to be locked down tight”, sealing the inhabitants in “submarine-like security” and “a special kind of fantasy space: militarized, privatized, post-nation-state” pp. 20–21.

  • Fig. 5, Family Fallout Shelter Residential Exterior. A suburban home on a seemingly peaceful, sunny day; a father and daughter slowly descend into a circular hatch cut neatly into the lawn. Framed to enhance sky and grass, the photograph “registers a preternatural calm, belying the context of nuclear war that necessitates this shelter project” p. 21.
  • Fig. 6, Residential Interior. A neatly ordered family space with air purifier, stove and bunk beds, already populated by three generations of happy inhabitants. “The father can sleep in this image precisely because he has put forth the labor to build a shelter as a personal response to the international nuclear crisis.” The canned goods and medical kits become “a register of good parenting”, and time in the bunker “can be quality family time” p. 21.
  • Figs 7–8, Hidden Fallout Shelter and Interior. The same structure “with more humor”, recasting the shelter as a place for peace and quiet. The smiling male owner pops his head out of a carefully hidden entrance, “a covert space surrounded by a thicket of trees and shrubs”. Inside he lies “in relaxed pleasure, legs crossed” on a bunk bed, enjoying a magazine, “a slight smile on his face, the very picture of contentment”. The shelter is “as much bachelor pad as survival kit” pp. 21–22. The hidden entrance sends a double message: a secret retreat, and a “regional secret”, an admission that in a nuclear crisis “less prepared Americans might be scared into violent acts of appropriation”. One immediate concern of the shelter debate was how to cope “not with the bomb or a Soviet invasion, but with traumatized neighbors reduced to a violent state of panic” p. 22.
  • Figs 9–10, Rural Family Fallout Shelter Exterior and Interior Food Storage. As clouds gather, a farming family moves into its hidden shelter, “dwarfed by grass and sky”. The image “magnifies the drama of the world outside the shelter, the enormity of global politics, and replaces it with the cool forward-thinking bunker logics” of Figure 10. Inside, the mother sorts her stock of preserved food, “revealing months of labor already invested in preparing for a postnuclear future” p. 22.

The FCDA divided shelter duties by gender and age: men built and organized the shelter, women handled food and first aid, and children studied nuclear effects “(to keep their parents on track)”. The family farm joined a larger FCDA campaign for food recovery, “including shelters for cattle”. These images merge “the survivalist narrative of self-preservation and independence characteristic of American frontier heritage” with a pastoral landscape that hides the windowless life below. The picture of a family alone in the wilderness preparing for a hard winter makes the fallout shelter “a uniquely American space, bringing together the rural, the pastoral, and the radioactive in one conceptual drama” pp. 22–23.

Shelters and consumer capitalism

Each shelter was also “a privatized enterprise, stocked largely with purchased commodities, from generators to radios, bunk beds and flashlights”. “The fusion of shelters and consumer capitalism was essential right from the start.” President Kennedy asked each American to prepare for nuclear war by finding or building a shelter. He also proposed a $400 million national shelter program one year before the Cuban Missile Crisis, “energizing a new industry in store-bought shelters”. The FCDA sought “to enhance the allure of the shelter” by sponsoring a national campaign to design multiuse rooms, “good for sitting out a nuclear war or for use in the pre-attack everyday”. Families were always shown together, healthy and happy underground when war broke out. The reason: “The FCDA was attempting to relocate the American family to the nuclear bunker—to make the bomb the source of family life rather than the destruction of it” p. 23.

Designing fun (Figures 11–12)

  • Fig. 11, the “Fun Room Fall-Out Shelter”, designed by the Los Angeles firm of Dorothy H. Paul: a dual-use playroom and bunker. Instead of windows, its walls show a painted playground of children in a park, running and climbing trees, “precisely the environment that would likely be scorched or radioactive after a nuclear exchange”. There are stations for reading and watching films, and board games stacked along the wall. The “fun” “requires both faith and imagination, transforming the terror of a nuclear war into an opportunity for game playing and other modes of distraction” p. 24.
  • Fig. 12, the “Family Room of Tomorrow Fall-Out Shelter”, designed by Marc T. Nielsen of Chicago: another windowless cinderblock bunker, decorated with Stone Age wall paintings and a world map, “as if to raise the question of what kind of tomorrow the family will have—the prehistoric or the modern?” Shown in full-scale mock-up at a Chicago trade show, it met “not with praise and admiration but the anger of a crowd sickened by the assumption about what a nuclear ‘tomorrow’ might look like” p. 24.

Duck and cover (Figure 13)

Despite the effort to romanticize the shelter as a dual-use room “suited for all kinds of catastrophe as well as for entertaining guests”, “most Americans did not—indeed, could not—build nuclear bunkers”. Instead Figure 13 shows the most wide-ranging response to the bomb: “the duck-and-cover drill that every American schoolchild practiced for the forty years of official Cold War” p. 25.

Masco's view

“Here, face down, internalized in one's own mind, and completely vulnerable to the world around, is the ultimate Cold War posture—a sightless, private bunker of the most pathos-driven kind” p. 25.

Who the campaign pictured

FCDA campaigns always offered the best-case scenario, in which the bombs explode “well over the horizon”, giving Americans time to reach their shelters. So the FCDA focused on “the middle-class, suburban family living on the periphery of urban centers, creating and reinforcing an image of America as an exclusively white nuclear family”. This “left unrepresented a vast population of Americans while ignoring the predominantly urban concentration of U.S. populations”. The campaign tied the shelter to “a specific American narrative of frontier survival” and presented the bunker “largely as a commodified dream space rather than a disaster zone” p. 25.

Masco's view

“Via civil defense, federal authorities promoted an idea of an invulnerable American—able to exist outside of time and space—located within a new mythology of perfect national security” p. 25.

Critics, and Dr. Strangelove

“Scientists and activists almost immediately challenged this denial of death.” They critiqued the factual claims of civil defense, “helping to foment peace, civil rights, and environmental movements” pp. 25–26.

“Perhaps the most devastating critique of the bunker society” was Stanley Kubrick's Dr. Strangelove, or How I Learned to Stop Worrying and Love the Bomb. Masco dates it 1963 on this page and 1964 in his reading list Not in Masco: released January 1964. At the end, the president and his war council are “holed up in the closed world command center known as the ‘war room’”, while a huge computer screen tracks U.S. and Soviet bombers on their final runs. Rather than despair, the president's science adviser, Dr. Strangelove, suggests moving “a nucleus of human specimens” to the deepest mine shafts, with nuclear reactors for energy and greenhouses for food, to wait out the fallout “for a few hundred years”. His proposed 10:1 ratio of women to men, to repopulate the human species, produces “immediate desire” among the president and his all-male council. It also sets off “a renewed state of competition with the Soviets”, this time to prevent, over the hundreds of years surface radiation would take to decay, a “mine shaft gap!” p. 26.

Masco's view

“The nuclear bunker is revealed here as pure masculine fantasy, participating in an erotics of death that is not subject to self-analysis even as the bombs begin to fall” p. 26.

From a Secure, Undisclosed Location

pp. 26–28 · Cheney after 9/11 · the war on terror · bunkers of the imagination · the perverse concept of security

Claim
The bunker society did not end. After 9/11 it became a full national security culture that governs by fear and leaves Americans feeling less secure.
Evidence
Cheney's “secure, undisclosed location”; the Bush administration's wars, surveillance, renditions and torture; US military spending in 2007.
Against
The belief that ever more spending on “security” produces security.

“What has become of the Cold War bunker society in the 21st century?” p. 26 In the days after the September 2001 attacks on the World Trade Center and the Pentagon, “one of the most theatrical people in the country was Vice President Dick Cheney”. He had spent much of his career on continuity-of-government plans for nuclear war, and he issued his public statements from a “secure, undisclosed location”. It was “almost instantly a joke”, funny because most Americans could picture the windowless underground bunker, “now outfitted with the Internet and video conferencing”. Energizing the whole Cold War system for nuclear crisis, Cheney “began to orchestrate from his buried control center the start of a global military response that he called the ‘new normal’”. This was not the FCDA's idyllic bunker for citizens: “it was the militarized bunker linked to global technologies for war” p. 27.

One of the Bush administration's first projects in the “war on terror” was “to normalize a state of permanent crisis”, using “the legal, rhetorical, and emotional structures of the Cold War security state to radically change both foreign and domestic policy”. By calling “multiple wars, unprecedented domestic surveillance, global renditions, the suspension of the writ of habeas corpus, and torture” by the name “national security”, the administration “was able to suspend law and moral order in the United States” p. 27.

Bunkers of the imagination

Masco's view

“We do not build nuclear shelters anymore. This is not because the bunker society has been outmoded, but because it has been so completely integrated into public life.” Early civil defense sought to militarize the public and mobilize citizens for a long wartime commitment. “The long-term success of that project has created a ‘national security culture’ that is unprecedented in human history.” Today federal authorities “no longer ask citizens to go into underground rooms made of steel and concrete, but into bunkers of the imagination” (also the page's pull quote). These conceptual bunkers “free the national security state to operate in an uncontested field of global action” p. 27.

“No state in history has given as much to ‘security’ as the United States.” With less than 5 percent of the world's population, it “outspends the entire world combined on its military—nearly $1 trillion in 2007”. Yet this spending “does not produce a sense of security; quite the opposite” p. 27.

Masco's view

The history of the Cold War bunker explains why: “the focus has never been on stopping violence but on preparing psychologically to endure it, which has created a perverse concept of security” pp. 27–28. Rituals of security such as “the airport screenings that do not enhance security, the acquiescence to domestic electronic surveillance, and the extraordinary sums of money spent on ‘defense’” work “not to protect but to underscore, and even create, a sense of vulnerability”. The security state then treats that vulnerability as a call for more security (“preventative” wars, covert actions, greater secrecy), “leading to an escalating militarization of national life” p. 28.

The “war on terror” promises “to end the experience of terror, first by saturating national politics with forms of fear, and then by pursuing an ever-greater counterterrorist response to them”. The Bush administration “promoted the ideology of the bunker into a full national security culture”, training citizens to define “security” as a state project and to ignore “the vast manipulation of public life conducted in the name of ‘defense’”. “National security” “demanded docility from citizens” while enabling policies that violated “any concept of democratic governance” and “were deadly” p. 28.

Fallout shelters would not have saved many Americans in a nuclear war: they “would have suffocated most in spaces that became ovens under the full force of nuclear warfare”. In the same way, “national security” has been used to justify “unprecedented sacrifice in terms of life, law, and capital since 2001” p. 28.

Masco's closing

With the wreckage of the “war on terror” becoming visible, “perhaps ... the concept of ‘national security’ and its nuclear roots can be formally reconsidered. Perhaps it is even time for Americans to get out of the nuclear bunker once and for all, begin demilitarizing, and re-enter the world in all its bright and messy insecurity.” p. 28

“Survival Is Your Business”

Engineering Ruins and Affect in Nuclear America

The article in one breath

Masco argues that the early Cold War United States built the nation by making its citizens contemplate its destruction. After the Soviet bomb (1949) and NSC 68 (1950), civil defense became an “emotional management” campaign. It tried to turn paralysing “nuclear terror” into functional “nuclear fear”, to make panic rather than nuclear war the enemy, and so to shift responsibility for survival from the state to citizens. Its centrepiece was theatre: target maps, yearly Operation Alert evacuations and above all Operation Cue (5 May 1955). There a model suburb, its mannequin families and its frozen chicken potpies were atom-bombed on live television. The fear lasted longer than the science, and its images became “the very grammar of nuclear discourse”. The Day After (1983) reused them to shock viewers into antinuclear politics. The 1998 asteroid blockbusters turned them into “post-traumatic play” that relegitimised nuclear weapons. After 9/11 the Bush administration's “mushroom cloud” used the same fear to sell preemptive war. Americans remain “fearful docile bodies”, “caught between terror and fear” p. 390.

Opening

Claim
The US turned ruination into nation-building. A new social contract rested on “the national contemplation of ruins”, and its images still structure American security culture.
Evidence
The nuclear “test program” and civil defense exercises; images circulating from the 1950s to the war on terror.
Against
On method: viewer-response studies. He tracks how images were made and circulated, not how audiences received them.

“Has any nation-state invested as profoundly in ruins as Cold War America?” p. 361 The invention of the atomic bomb “proved to be utterly transformative for the United States”. Many societies have contemplated the ruins they might leave behind, but “it took American ingenuity to transform ruination into a form of nation-building”. The bomb inspired a geopolitical strategy that “quickly enveloped the earth in advanced military technology and colonized everyday life with the minute-to-minute possibility of nuclear war”. It also gave officials “a new means of engaging and disciplining citizens in everyday life”. For policymakers the arms race turned the apocalypse into “a technoscientific project and a geopolitical paradigm, but also a powerful new domestic political resource”.

Masco's thesis

“A new kind of social contract was formed in the first decade of the nuclear age in the United States, one based not on the protection and improvement of everyday life but, rather, on the national contemplation of ruins.” Known initially as “civil defense”, the project of building the bomb and communicating its power “turned engineering ruins into a form of (inter)national theater” p. 361.

Nuclear explosions matched with large-scale emergency exercises became a way of developing the bomb and of imagining nuclear war (Glasstone and Dolan 1977; Kahn 1960; Vanderbilt 2002). This “test program” would make the United States “the most nuclear-bombed country on earth”, spreading its environmental, economic and health effects “to each and every U.S. citizen” p. 361. fn 1 The US conducted 1,054 nuclear detonations between 1945 and 1992, besides bombing Hiroshima and Nagasaki, 928 of them at the Nevada Test Site. On health effects: Miller (1986), Makhijani et al. (1995), the Advisory Committee on Human Radiation Experiments (1996), Makhijani and Schwartz (1998), Masco (2006).

By the mid-1950s it was “no longer a perverse exercise to imagine one's own home and city devastated, on fire, and in ruins; it was a formidable public ritual—a core act of governance, technoscientific practice, and democratic participation”. In the early Cold War it became “a civic obligation to collectively imagine, and at times theatrically enact through ‘civil defense,’ the physical destruction of the nation-state” p. 362. fn 2 Damage assessments of a potential Soviet strike: Keeney (2002); Office of Technology Assessment (1980).

What the article explores

His subject is “this specific nationalization of death”: the first collective formulations of nuclear fear, and their legacies. “Today, we live in a world populated with newly charred landscapes and a production of ruins that speaks directly to this foundational moment” (citing Stoler with Bond 2006). The Bush administration's notions of preemption and emergency response in the “war on terror” “derive meaning from the promises and institutions built by the Cold War security state”. The logics of nuclear fear now exist as “a largely inchoate, but deeply embedded, set of assumptions about power and threat” p. 362.

Masco's argument

“How Americans have come to understand mass death at home and abroad, I argue, has much to do with the legacies of the Cold War nuclear project, and the peculiar psychosocial consequences of attempting to build the nation through the contemplation of nuclear ruins.” And: “key aspects of U.S. security culture have been formed in relation to images of nuclear devastation”. The postwar security state used the bomb as the basis of geopolitical power, “but it also created a new citizen–state relationship mediated by nuclear fear” p. 362.

Method and plan

  • “What follows is largely a study of visual culture, and specifically, of the domestic deployment of images of a ruined United States for ideological effect.”
  • It tracks nuclear ruins “from the Cold War's ‘balance of terror’ through the current ‘war on terror’”.
  • “It is not an exercise in viewer response but, rather, charts the development and circulation of a specific set of ideas and images about nuclear war.”
  • Plan: civil defense first, then images from that campaign that “continued to circulate as afterimages in the popular films of the 1980s and 1990s” p. 362. fn 3 His idea of photographic “afterimages” was developed in conversation with Shawn Michelle Smith (1999).
  • After 9/11, “the affective coordinates of the Cold War arms race provided specific ideological resources to the state, which once again mobilized the image of a United States in nuclear ruins to enable war” p. 363.

Be Afraid But Don't Panic!

pp. 363–372 · Blanchot · NSC 68 · Project East River · Oakes · Peterson · Janis · target maps · Operation Alert · Let's Face It · Figures 1–2

Claim
Civil defense was “psychological reprogramming”, not defense: an official fantasy that calibrated fear so citizens would back a permanent Cold War and carry responsibility for their own survival.
Evidence
NSC 68; Project East River (1952); Oakes (1994); Peterson (1953); a 1956 top-secret study; FCDA media figures; Janis (1951); target maps; Operation Alert; the film Let's Face It.
Against
The idea that civil defense aimed at protection “in the classic sense of avoiding violence or destruction”.

The disaster ruins everything, all the while leaving everything intact. ... To think the disaster (if this is possible, and it is not possible inasmuch as we suspect that the disaster is thought) is to have no longer any future in which to think it.

Maurice Blanchot, The Writing of Disaster (epigraph, p. 363)

“Nuclear ruins are never the end of the story in the United States but, rather, always offer a new beginning.” In the early Cold War, ruins became markers of “a new kind of social intimacy grounded in highly detailed renderings of theatrically rehearsed mass violence”. The intent of the public spectacles (nuclear detonations, city evacuations and duck-and-cover drills) “was not defense in the classic sense of avoiding violence or destruction but rather a psychological reprogramming of the U.S. public for life in a nuclear age”. The early nuclear state's central project was to link US institutions (“military, industrial, legislative, and academic”) to produce the bomb, “while calibrating public perceptions of the nuclear danger to enable that project” p. 363. fn 5 On the Cold War state: Friedberg (2000), Leslie (1993), Schwartz (1998), Gaddis (1982).

As Blanchot (1995) suggests, this effort to think through the disaster “colonized everyday life as well as the future, while fundamentally missing the actual disaster”. Scripting disaster in the imagination “defines the conditions of insecurity, renders other threats invisible, and articulates the terms of both value and loss” p. 363.

Masco's view

“In the United States, civil defense was always a willful act of fabulation, an official fantasy designed to promote an image of nuclear war that would be, above all things, politically useful.” It installed an idea of an American community “under total and unending threat”, creating a new kind of nation-building “that demanded an unprecedented level of militarism in everyday life as the minimum basis for ‘security’” p. 363.

NSC 68 (1950)

After the Soviet Union's first nuclear detonation in 1949, US policymakers committed to a geopolitical strategy that “would ultimately dominate U.S. foreign policy for the remainder of the 20th century”: “containment”, formalized in National Security Council 68 (NSC 68). It proposed, in response to the Soviet bomb, “a total mobilization of American society based on the experience of WWII” pp. 363–364. fn 6 On NSC 68: May (1993); critical analysis, Brands (1989); on “containment”, Gaddis (1982). According to Masco, NSC 68:

  • “articulates the terms of a permanent wartime posture funded by an ever-expanding domestic economy, transforming consumerism into the engine of a new kind of militarized geopolitics”;
  • “identifies internal dissent as perhaps the greatest threat to the project of ‘Cold War’ and calls for a new campaign to discipline citizens for life under the constant shadow of nuclear war” p. 364.

In Washington nuclear fear was at once the basis of US military power and “a means of installing a new normative reality within the United States, one that could consolidate political power at the federal level”. The nuclear danger became “a complex new political ideology”: it mobilized the global Cold War (“fought increasingly on covert terms”) and controlled domestic debate over security. By focusing Americans on an imminent end of the nation-state, federal authorities used the bomb to create the “Cold War consensus” of anticommunism, capitalism, and military expansion p. 364.

The NSC 68 box · pp. 364–365

Masco prints excerpts from NSC 68, “a classified policy document written in 1950 by the National Security Council for President Harry S. Truman”, which sets out Soviet “containment” and “the domestic terms of fighting a ‘Cold War’”. It has five headings:

  • On the Soviet Threat. The Soviet Union, “unlike previous aspirants to hegemony, is animated by a new fanatic faith, antithetical to our own, and seeks to impose its absolute authority over the rest of the world”. Conflict is therefore “endemic”, waged “by violent or non-violent methods in accordance with the dictates of expediency”. With weapons of mass destruction, “every individual faces the ever-present possibility of annihilation”. “No other value system is so wholly irreconcilable with ours, so implacable in its purpose to destroy ours”, so able to turn “the most dangerous and divisive trends in our own society” to its uses, so skilled at evoking “the elements of irrationality in human nature everywhere”, and backed by “a great and growing center of military power”. In “a shrinking world” facing atomic warfare, merely checking “the Kremlin design” is not enough, “for the absence of order among nations is becoming less and less tolerable”. This “imposes on us, in our own interests, the responsibility of world leadership”.
  • On containment. Seek “by all means short of war” to (1) block further expansion of Soviet power, (2) expose the falsities of Soviet pretensions, (3) induce a retraction of the Kremlin's control and influence, and (4) “foster the seeds of destruction within the Soviet system” until the Kremlin at least modifies its behaviour “to conform to generally accepted international standards”. It is “cardinal” that the US possess “superior overall power in ourselves or in dependable combination with other like-minded nations”, and “one of the most important ingredients of power is military strength”. Without “superior aggregate military strength, in being and readily mobilizable”, containment, “in effect a policy of calculated and gradual coercion”, “is no more than a policy of bluff”.
  • On the problem of internal dissent. “The democratic way is harder than the authoritarian way”, because it demands of the individual “understanding, judgment and positive participation” in complex problems, the exercise of “discrimination”, and knowing, while pursuing “through free inquiry the search for truth”, when to “commit an act of faith”. The citizen must “distinguish between the necessity for tolerance and the necessity for just suppression”. “A free society is vulnerable” to excesses: “a permanently open mind wishfully waiting for evidence that evil design may become noble purpose”, faith becoming prejudice, tolerance “degenerating into indulgence of conspiracy”, and “suppression when more moderate measures are not only more appropriate but more effective”.
  • On economic military expansion. World War II showed that the economy, near full efficiency, can supply “enormous resources for purposes other than civilian consumption while simultaneously providing a high standard of living”. “This provides an opportunity for the United States, in cooperation with other free countries, to launch a build-up of strength which will support a firm policy directed to the frustration of the Kremlin design.”
  • On the project of Cold War. A “rapid and sustained build-up of the political, economic, and military strength of the free world” and “an affirmative program intended to wrest the initiative from the Soviet Union”, to show the free world's determination to frustrate the Kremlin's design “of a world dominated by its will”. This is “the only means short of war” to make the Kremlin change course and negotiate. Success hangs on recognition by the government, the American people and all free peoples “that the cold war is in fact a real war in which the survival of the free world is at stake”.

Source given: Harry S. Truman Library and Museum website, accessed 15 November 2007.

Neither apathy nor terror: Project East River (1952)

Defense intellectuals in the Truman and Eisenhower administrations worried that nuclear terror might grow so profound “that the American public would be unwilling to support the military and geopolitical agenda of the Cold War” p. 366. fn 7 Histories of civil defense: Oakes (1994), McEnaney (2000), Garrison (2006), George (2003), Grossman (2001), Krugler (2006), Scheiback (2003). The challenge was to avoid “an apathetic public (which might just give up when faced with the destructive power of the Soviet nuclear arsenal), on the one hand, or a terrorized public (unable to function cognitively), on the other” (Oakes 1994:34; George 2003).

An influential 1952 study, Project East River, argued that civilian response to a nuclear attack “would be all-out panic and mob behavior”. American society “would not only be at war with the Soviets but also at war with itself as society violently broke down along race and class lines” (Associated Universities 1952). A long Cold War therefore needed “new forms of psychological discipline at home”.

Masco's view

“One of the earliest and most profound projects of the Cold War state was thus to deploy the bomb as a mechanism for accessing and controlling the emotions of citizens” p. 366.

From nuclear terror to nuclear fear

As Guy Oakes documented (1994:47), early Cold War civil defense was designed to “emotionally manage” citizens through nuclear fear. Its formal goal was to transform “nuclear terror”, which officials read as “a paralyzing emotion”, into “nuclear fear”, “an affective state that would allow citizens to function in a time of crisis” (Associated Universities 1952; Oakes 1994:62–63). Instead of offering safety or a war that could end in victory, the state tried “to calibrate everyday American life to the minute-to-minute possibility of nuclear warfare”. It turned the home into the front line and told citizens to be ready “every second of the day” p. 366.

Masco's view

“The Civil Defense Program shifted responsibility for nuclear war from the state to its citizens by making public panic the enemy, not nuclear war itself.” It was “up to citizens to take responsibility for their own survival in the nuclear age” p. 366.

Val Peterson, “the first head of the U.S. Civil Defense Administration”, argued in 1953:

Ninety per cent of all emergency measures after an atomic blast will depend on the prevention of panic among the survivors in the first 90 seconds. Like the A-bomb, panic is fissionable. It can produce a chain reaction more deeply destructive than any explosive known. If there is an ultimate weapon, it may well be mass panic—not the A-bomb.

Val Peterson, “Panic: The Ultimate Weapon?”, Collier's, 1953 · pp. 366–367

“Panic is fissionable.” This idea that emotional self-regulation was the most important issue in an attack (with a 90-second window for success or failure) “sought quite formally to turn all Americans into docile bodies that would automatically support the goals of the security state”. Planners wanted to saturate public space with an idea of nuclear war “that would nationalize mass death and transform postnuclear ruins into a new American frontier, simply another arena for citizens to assert their civic spirit and ingenuity”. At its heart were “psychological defenses against the exploding bomb” and belief in national unity after nuclear war, “all via the contemplation of nuclear ruins” p. 367.

fn 9 Peterson called the civil defense media campaign “the greatest mass educational effort” in US history (Garrison 2006:36). Masco adds that the program was also “a laboratory for exploring how to mobilize and control a mass society”. Not in Masco Standard histories name Millard Caldwell (1950–52) as the FCDA's first administrator; Peterson led it from 1953 to 1957.

Atoms for Peace abroad, fear at home

While the Eisenhower administration promoted “Atoms for Peace” around the world to show the benefits of nuclear energy and give atomic science a positive face, “it pursued an opposite emotional management strategy within the United States” (Craig 1998; Hewlett and Holl 1989; Osgood 2006). The domestic answer to the Soviet arsenal was “a new kind of social-engineering project, pursued with help from the advertising industry, to teach citizens a specific kind of nuclear fear while normalizing the nuclear crisis”. As one top-secret study put it in 1956, the goal was an “emotional adaptation” of the citizenry, a program of “psychological defense” aimed at “feelings” p. 367.

fn 8 The Panel on the Human Effects of Nuclear Weapons Development (1956) imagined a Soviet attack in 1959 that destroyed 90 major cities and killed 50 million. It concluded that without “drastically improved preparation of the people, support of the National Government and of the war effort would be in jeopardy, and national disintegration might well result” (1956:9). The problem was how to build the possibility of a sneak attack into citizens' “feelings” (see Vandercook 1986). Masco compares RAND's 2006 study by Charles Meade and Roger Molander, which models a 10-kiloton nuclear explosion in Los Angeles that would overwhelm all services and deal a $1 trillion blow to the US economy.

This took the form of “the largest domestic propaganda campaign to date in U.S. history”. It involved town meetings, education programs in every public school, and mass media: television, radio “and, particularly, film”. By the mid-1950s the FCDA (called the “Federal Civil Defense Agency” on this page) saturated newspapers and magazines with nuclear war planning advertisements and claimed that its radio broadcasts reached an estimated 175 million Americans per year p. 367.

Film, and the censored bodies of Hiroshima

The FCDA built a library of short films on nuclear destruction and civil defense, shown in schools, churches, community halls and cinemas. It concluded in 1955 that “each picture will be seen by a minimum of 20,000,000 persons, giving an anticipated aggregate audience of more than half a billion for the civil defense film program of 1955” (FCDA 1956:78). “A key to winning the Cold War was producing the bomb not only for military use but also in cinematic form for the American public” pp. 367–368.

These images relied on “a simultaneous censorship of images from the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki in 1945”. US authorities released images of destroyed buildings but “withheld the detailed effects of the atomic bomb on the human body, as well as some firsthand accounts of the aftermath” p. 368. fn 10 Braw (1997); Weller (2006); Steven Okazaki's 2006 documentary White Light, Black Rain, which details the censorship and shows once-prohibited footage. The state calibrated the image of atomic war “through the mass circulation of certain images of the bomb and the censorship of all others”. That turned nuclear attack “from an unthinkable apocalypse into an opportunity for psychological self-management, civic responsibility, and ultimately, governance”. The goal was an “atomic bomb proof” society in which nuclear conflict was normalized alongside all other threats, “making public support for the Cold War sustainable” p. 368.

Emotional inoculation (Janis, 1951)

Civil defense theorists held that citizens could reach “this contradictory state of productive fear (simultaneously mobilized and normalized)” only by gaining intimacy with nuclear war and its language, “from blast, heat, and fire to radioactive fallout”. RAND analyst Irving L. Janis called the goal an “emotional inoculation” of the public (1951:220). The dose had to be finely calibrated. Simulated destruction and released test footage had to be formidable enough to mobilize citizens but not so terrifying as “to invalidate the concept of defense altogether”. That was hard with thermonuclear weapons, “which offered no hope of survival to most urban residents” p. 368.

Masco's view

“A central project of civil defense was thus to produce fear but not terror, anxiety but not panic, to inform about nuclear science but not fully educate about nuclear war.” The goal was “the microregulation of a nation community at the emotional level”. Alongside the new security state came “a new public culture of insecurity”: presenting the United States as a global nuclear superpower “was coterminous with a domestic campaign to reveal the United States as completely vulnerable”, creating a citizen–state relationship “mediated by forms of inchoate but ever present nuclear fear” p. 368.

Every city a target (Figure 1)

“One of the first U.S. civil defense projects of the Cold War was to make every U.S. city a target, and every U.S. citizen a potential victim of nuclear attack” p. 368. FCDA maps listed likely targets by population. A 1955 FCDA map (Fig. 1) shows the top 70 Soviet targets, cities and military bases alike. It revealed the vulnerability of big cities and also “the increasingly wide distribution of military industrial sites across the continental United States”. As bombs and delivery systems grew (from bombers to ICBMs), so did the published target lists p. 369.

Thomas J. Martin and Donald C. Latham's 1963 civil defense textbook Strategy for Survival made the case for 303 ground zeros in the United States. The authors conceded that “it might appear that we are trying to know the unknowable, to predict the unpredictable, to impose a logical rationale upon war which is, itself, illogical and irrational”, but called that inference “incorrect”: there were “good reasons to believe that a large fraction of these cities would be attacked”, though no one could say which. So planning “must assume that all could be potential targets. Any other approach is thermonuclear Russian Roulette played with 100 million American lives” (Martin and Latham 1963:182). They marked every population centre of over 50,000 people as a likely target and saw no “safe” area: “From New York to Topeka, from Los Angeles to Waco, from Albuquerque to Anchorage”, each community could argue it was a “first strike” target. Citizens heard “from multiple media sources” that their community, “indeed, their very living room”, was “the literal front line of the Cold War, with Soviet thermonuclear warheads poised to attack” pp. 369–370. fn 11 Grossman (2001:47): government, media and industry deliberately harmonized their civil defense messages, which reinforced the project and reduced the opportunities for critique (also Keever 2004; Scheiback 2003; Rojecki 1999).

Operation Alert, 1953–1961 (Figure 2)

From 1953 to 1961 the yearly centerpiece of civil defense was a simulated nuclear attack on the United States directed by federal authorities. fn 12 Oakes (1994), McEnaney (2000), Davis (2002). Cities were designated as victims. Civic leaders led theatrical evacuations for television, followed by media discussion of blast versus fire versus fallout damage and the expected casualties “if the attack had been ‘real’” p. 370.

The 1955 “Operation Alert” scenario (Fig. 2) involved:

  • 60 cities hit by a variety of atomic and hydrogen bombs;
  • over 8 million instant deaths and another 8 million radiation victims over the following weeks;
  • 25 million homeless, and fallout covering some 63,000 square miles (FCDA 1956; Krugler 2006:126).

“Each year Americans acted out their own incineration in this manner”, with officials cheerfully evacuating cities, while detonations in Nevada and the South Pacific supplied new fireballs and mushroom clouds. The state created “a new psychosocial space caught between the utopian promise of U.S. technoscience and the minute-to-minute threat of thermonuclear incineration”. It made mass death “an intimate psychological experience” while claiming thermonuclear war could be planned for “alongside tornados, floods, and traffic accidents” p. 370.

Masco's view

“Civil defense ultimately sought to make nuclear war a space of nation-building, and thereby bring this new form of death under the control of the state” p. 370.

“A weird, fantastic city” (Let's Face It)

One of the most widely circulated civil defense films of the 1950s, Let's Face It, states the problem. “The tremendous effects of heat and blast on modern structures raise important questions concerning their durability and safety”; damage to industry would affect recovery; and “the nation's lifeblood could be cut if its traffic arteries were severed”. The questions matter to people in metropolitan centres and to rural areas in danger from fallout. The answers could come from “constructing a complete city at our Nevada Proving Ground and then exploding a nuclear bomb over it ... We could study the effects of damage over a wide area, under all conditions, and plan civil defense activities accordingly. But such a gigantic undertaking is not feasible” p. 371. The problem is “ultimately one of scientific detail”: how can the state prepare to survive an attack “if it does not know precisely how every aspect of American life would respond to both the effects of the bomb and the resulting social confusion?” Then the narrator reveals that the state has done exactly that:

Instead we build representative units of a test city. With steel and stone and brick and mortar, with precision and skill—as though it were to last a thousand years. But it is a weird, fantastic city. A creation right out of science fiction. ... Homes, neat and clean and completely furnished, that will never be occupied. Bridges, massive girders of steel spanning the empty desert. Railway tracks that lead to nowhere, for this is the end of the line. ... Every brick, beam, and board will have its story to tell.

Let's Face It (FCDA film), quoted at p. 372

“A weird, fantastic city.” The test city was also “an idealized model of the contemporary American suburb”, and by publicizing its destruction “the state was involved in an explicit act of psychological manipulation”. The Nevada Test Site hosted nuclear-war “simulations” with real explosions and “model American cities destroyed in real time for a national audience”. Each ruin in these “national melodramas” was presented as “a key to solving the ‘problem’ of nuclear warfare, a means of cracking the code for survival” p. 372.

But making citizens confront the logics of the nuclear state also allowed many “to reclaim and reinvest these same ruins with a counternarrative and critique”. fn 13 Histories of the antinuclear movement: Rojecki (1999), Katz (1986), Wittner (1993, 1997, 2003). So real and imagined ruins became “the foundation for competing ideas of national community, producing resistance to, as well as normalization of, a militarized society” p. 372.

Masco's view

The early Cold War effort to produce an “atomic bomb proof” society “may have failed”, but “the psychosocial legacies of this moment continue to haunt and inform U.S. national culture”. The rest of the article offers “a visual history of nuclear ruins” to recover “the affective coordinates of the nuclear security state” and the lasting effects of the “emotional management” strategy p. 372.

fn 14 · the historiographical move

Dee Garrison (2006) argues that Cold War civil defense “should be judged a failure because no nationwide system of shelters was ever built”. Masco: “the project of civil defense was not about building a new urban infrastructure but, rather, an emotional one.” Measured that way it “was much more successful”. It installed ideas and images of nuclear war “that maintained public support for the nuclear state through the end of the Cold War”, and it provided the ideological resources the Bush administration relied on for its “war on terror” pp. 391–392.

“Cue for Survival”

pp. 372–378, Figure 4 on p. 379 · Operation Cue, 5 May 1955 · mannequins · potpies · Landstreet · the 1964 re-release · the 1963 test ban · Figures 3–4

Claim
Operation Cue was national theatre. A model suburb, its mannequin families and its consumer goods were atom-bombed to show that life after nuclear war would be familiar, and to train citizens to obey rather than think.
Evidence
FCDA reports and the Cue film (1955); the frozen-food report (Schmitt 1956); Arthur Landstreet's testimony; the J. C. Penney tour; the 1964 re-release; the 1963 test ban.
Against
Reading Cue's motto “Survival Is Your Business” as camp, “an ironic moment of atomic kitch”.

5 May 1955

On May 5, 1955, 100 million Americans watched live on television as “a ‘typical’ suburban community” was “blown to bits by an atomic bomb” (FCDA 1955, 1956). Many watched from homes and apartments “that were the explicit models for the test city”. They saw mannequin families posed in casual everyday moments, “at the kitchen table, on the couch, in bed—or watching TV”, experience the blast pp. 372–373.

Operation Cue “was the largest of the civil defense spectacles staged at the Nevada Test Site”. It promised to show both the power of the bomb and “exactly what a postnuclear American city would look like”. Besides the live broadcast, film footage was distributed for years afterwards in cinemas and on television. Some of the most enduring US images of atomic destruction were made here and still circulate. The rubble of Operation Cue continues “to structure contemporary U.S. perceptions of postnuclear ruins, constituting a kind of ur-text for the nuclear age” p. 373.

What was tested

  • Residences, shelter designs, utilities, mobile housing, vehicles, warning systems and “a variety of domestic items”, each linked to a test program and research team from Los Alamos Scientific Laboratory, the Atomic Energy Commission and the FCDA.
  • Exercises after the blast: rescue operations, fire control, plane evacuations, communication and sanitation, and mass feeding.
  • A “representative” American community: ramblers, two-story brick houses, trailers and mobile homes; utilities “from electronic towers to propane systems”; numerous bomb shelter designs; office safes to protect records.
  • Over 150 industrial associations installed the latest consumer items “from cars to furniture, clothing to dishware, televisions to radio” in the new houses.
  • Hundreds of civilians were invited to inhabit “not the pristine pretest city but the posttest atomic ruins”. They were “simultaneously witnesses and test subjects”, representative “Americans” tested by watching the blast and joining mass feeding and emergency operations.
  • The formal inhabitants were the mannequin families (Fig. 3), dressed and posed to say “that the bombing was an unexpected intrusion into an intimate home space” p. 373.

Addressed to women

Cue was designed for a domestic audience, “and particularly to women” p. 373. fn 15 Garrison (2006:94–96) on the Mother's Protest against the civil defense project; McEnaney (2000) on women and the militarization of the home; May (1990) on the domestic version of “containment”; Scheiback (2003) on civil defense and youth culture; Cohn (1987) on gender in defense-intellectual language; Orr (2006) on “panic” in Cold War psychology. Unlike earlier civil defense films, the Operation Cue film (FCDA 1955, 15 minutes) had a female narrator, Joan Collins, who promised to see the test “through my own eyes and the eye of the average citizen” pp. 373–374.

Seeking a “bomb proof” society, the FCDA documented the effects of the bomb “against every detail of middle-class, white, suburban life”. The media strategy recalibrated domestic life “by turning the nuclear family into a nuclearized family, preprogrammed for life before, during, and after a nuclear war”. Gender roles were reinforced by splitting responsibility for food and security between women and men, and school campaigns were designed “to deploy children to educate their parents about civil defense”. Normative gender roles made nuclear crisis seem “not an exceptional condition but one that could be incorporated into everyday life with minor changes in household technique and a ‘can do’ American spirit” p. 374.

Food, and the paramilitary housewife

Food tests and mass feeding were a particular concern. The pantries and refrigerators of every model home were stocked. Collins's voiceover addresses women directly: “As a mother and housewife, I was particularly interested in the food test program, a test that included canned and packaged food.” Food in various packaging was also buried in the desert to expose it to radiation, and some mannequins were posed preparing food at the moment of detonation: “at any moment of the day—while enjoying one's breakfast for example—the bomb could drop”. The FCDA sought, as McEnaney argues (2000:109; the article spells her “McEaney”), to create a “paramilitary housewife”, “emotionally and materially in control of her home and thinking about postnuclear social life” p. 374.

Formally, the FCDA wanted to know whether food would be too contaminated to eat and how to feed large groups of “homeless, injured, and traumatized people”. Food was positioned as “a primary means of calming individual anxieties and establishing social authority” (McEnaney 2000:111). Informally, the goal was “to saturate the domestic space of the home with nuclear logics and civic obligations, to militarize men, women, and children to withstand either a very long nuclear confrontation or a very short nuclear war” p. 375.

Food was flown in from Las Vegas, Chicago and San Francisco to show the state could move food around the country in an “emergency”. The FCDA report “Project 32.5: Effects of Nuclear Explosions on Frozen Food” concluded that “under emergency conditions similar to this exposure, frozen foods may be used for both military and civilian feeding” (Schmitt 1956:3). Frozen chicken potpies were a privileged test item. They were placed in the test homes and buried in bulk freezers, then exposed to the blast and tested “for radiation, as well as nutritional value, color, and taste” p. 375.

Masco's view

While building ever more powerful weapons, “the security state set about demonstrating to Americans that even if the nation-state disappeared under nuclear fire, its newly developed prepared foods would still be edible (making the chicken potpie a curious emblem of modernity in the process)”. Like the potpies, “all commodified aspects of American life were to be tested against nuclear blast”, to show “not only that there could be a ‘postnuclear’ moment, but that life within it could be imagined on largely familiar terms” p. 375.

A giant picnic

“Documenting evidence of material survival after the atomic blast was ultimately the point of Operation Cue.” The mass-feeding team pulled equipment, refrigerated food and buried canned goods from the wreckage and served participants an emergency meal of roast beef, tomato juice, baked beans, and coffee (FCDA 1955:67, printed “FDCA”). “The destruction of a model American community thus became the occasion of a giant picnic, with each item of food marked as having survived the atomic bomb, and each witness positioned as a postnuclear survivor.” The rescue group pulled damaged mannequins from the rubble, practised medical and evacuation techniques on them, and flew “several charred and broken dummies to offsite hospitals by charter plane” p. 375.

The formal message: the postnuclear environment “would be only as chaotic as citizens allowed”, resources would still be present, and “society—if not the nation-state—would continue”. “Nuclear war was ultimately presented as a state of mind”: “simply a matter of emotional preparation and mental discipline” pp. 375–376.

“This could be you!”

Mannequin families that survived intact went on a national tour in their tattered, scorched clothes. J. C. Penney, which had supplied the garments, displayed them in its stores under the sign “this could be you!”. This inverted the usual advertising appeal. The viewer was to identify not with “the blue suit or polka dot dress” but with “the mannequin as survivor”, proof that you could “beat the a-bomb”, as one civil defense film promised. Invited to picture themselves as “the docile mannequins of civil defense”, the audience was “caught in a sea of mixed messages about the power of the state to control the bomb” p. 376.

Masco's view

“This kind of ritual enactment did not resolve the problem of the bomb but, rather, focused citizens on emotional self-discipline through nuclear fear. It asked them to live on the knife's edge of a psychotic contradiction—an everyday life founded simultaneously in total threat and absolute normality—with the stakes being nothing less that survival itself” p. 376.

Position Baker: Arthur F. Landstreet

Cue was billed as a test of “the things we use in everyday life”, but “the full intent of the test was to nationalize nuclear fear and install a new civic understanding via the contemplation of mass destruction and death”. Arthur F. Landstreet, general manager of the Hotel King Cotton in Memphis, Tennessee, volunteered to crouch in a trench at the Nevada Test Site about 10,000 feet from ground zero p. 376. Afterwards he explained why ordinary citizens were put there:

Apparently the reason for stationing civilians at Position Baker was to find out what the actual reaction from citizens who were not schooled in the atomic field would be, and to get some idea of what the ordinary citizen might be able to endure under similar conditions. This idea was part of the total pattern to condition civilians for what they might be expected to experience in case of atomic attack. ... We would have about 5 minutes to get at least 2.5 to 3 miles distant, so it was necessary that we learn every move perfectly.

Arthur F. Landstreet, in FCDA 1955:75 · pp. 376–377

Every step of the burst was explained “over and over”. Participants ran timed drills from the trench to their jeeps “time after time, endeavoring to create more speed and less loss of motion”, because if the bomb exploded overhead “with practically no wind, the fallout would drop immediately downward” pp. 376–377.

“The total pattern to condition civilians.” Physical reactions dominate Landstreet's account (spelled “Lanstreet” here), but “a corollary project is also revealed”: “training the participants not to think in a case of emergency but simply to act”. The first project was emotional management, familiarizing citizens with the bomb “to psychologically inoculate them against their own apocalyptic imagination”. The later effort “sought simply to control those same bodies, to train and time their response to official commands” p. 377. fn 16 This civilian test was a small part of a larger military project involving thousands of troops at the Nevada Test Site. Across a series of aboveground tests, soldiers took part in “atomic warfare” exercises and were tested for the cognitive effects of seeing the blast. Some did simple cognitive drills minutes after the explosion, or were timed at basic tasks like dismantling and reassembling a rifle. The state was “testing the limits of participation, and conditioning emotions and bodies to the atomic bomb”. “The atomic bomb extended the docility of the citizen–subject to new levels, as civil defense sought to absorb the everyday within a new normative reality imbued with the potential for an imminent and total destruction.”

Masco's view

“This short-circuiting of the brain, and willingness to take orders under the sign of nuclear emergency, reveals the broader scope of the civil defense project: anesthetizing as well as protecting, producing docility as well as agency” p. 377.

Resistance, and a disclaimer (1959, 1964)

The effort “met with almost immediate resistance”. Besides mounting scientific challenges, a “mothers against the bomb” movement started in 1959, when two young mothers in New York refused to take part in Operation Alert and took their children to Central Park instead of the fallout shelter (Garrison 2006:93–5). Publicity about radioactive fallout and the move from atomic to thermonuclear weapons showed that Cue was not “a ‘realistic’ portrait of nuclear warfare” p. 377. fn 17 On the politics of atmospheric fallout: Masco (2006), Miller (1986), Hewlett and Holl (1989), Wang (1999), Kraus et al. (1963), Bentz et al. (1957), Makhijani and Schwartz (1998).

When the Operation Cue film was re-released in 1964, new text in its introduction minimized its claims:

The nuclear device used was comparatively small. It had an explosive force of 30 kilotons, equivalent to 30,000 tons of TNT. Whereas, some modern thermonuclear weapons are in the 20-megaton range—twenty million tons—more than 600 times as powerful as the bomb shown here, and with a much wider radius of destruction. In this test, many of the structures damaged by the 30-kiloton bomb were approximately one mile from “ground zero.” With a 20-megaton blast, they probably would be obliterated, and comparable damage would occur out to a distance of at least 8.5 or 9 miles.

Operation Cue, 1964 re-release · pp. 377–378

“They probably would be obliterated.” As a test of “everyday objects”, Cue lost value as bigger weapons “rendered civil defense almost immediately obsolete as a security concept”. In Cold War ideology, though, the promise of nuclear ruins secured “the possibility of a postnuclear remainder, and with it, the inevitable reconstitution of social order”. The word “obliteration” exposes the technoscientific limits of that ideological project p. 378.

The frozen visual record (1963)

After the 1963 Partial Test Ban Treaty, testing went underground and the visual effects of the bomb disappeared. This had two immediate effects:

  1. It changed public discourse, since the state no longer had to rationalize “the constant production of mushroom clouds and the related health concerns over radioactive fallout”.
  2. It “locked in place the visual record of the bomb”. The record of the 1945–63 aboveground program, “with its deep implication in manipulating public opinions and emotions, remains the visual record of the bomb to this day” p. 378.
Masco's view

“As science, Operation Cue was always questionable, but as national theater it remains a much more productive enterprise.” It fused an idealized consumer dream space with the bomb, “creating the very vocabulary for thinking about the nuclear emergency that continues to inform U.S. politics” (Fig. 4). The motto “Survival Is Your Business” “is not an ironic moment of atomic kitch, but rather reveals the formal project of the nuclear state, underscoring the link between the production of threat, its militarized response, and the Cold War economic program”. As emotional management, civil defense “proved extraordinarily influential” p. 378.

He then turns to “two afterimages of the 1950s civil defense program, each set roughly a generation apart”, to explore the effects of “deploying highly detailed depictions of the end of the nation-state as a means of establishing national community” p. 378.

Afterimage 1: The Day After (1983)

pp. 380–383 · Derrida · ABC's broadcast · Reagan · PSR and FREEZE · Figure 5

Claim
The Day After replayed Operation Cue's program almost exactly but inverted its emotional project, from producing docile citizens to mobilizing a national community against nuclear war.
Evidence
ABC's 1983 broadcast and its closing text; reactions (ABC, teachers, Reagan); Derrida; the PSR and FREEZE campaigns.
Against
The film's own claim to be “realistic”.

The anticipation of nuclear war (dreaded as the fantasy, or phantasm, of a remainderless destruction) installs humanity—and through all sorts of relays even defines the essence of modern humanity—in its rhetorical condition.

Jacques Derrida, “No Apocalypse, Not Now” (epigraph, p. 380)

On Sunday, November 20, 1983, 100 million Americans watched the United States destroyed by Soviet ICBMs and the few survivors in Lawrence, Kansas, “negotiate everyday life in a postnuclear environment” (Fig. 5). Watched by half of the adult population, The Day After (directed by Nicholas Meyer) refocused attention on radiation, mass casualties “and life without a functioning state”. Presented as a “realistic” account, its blast and radiation effects were backed by health experts and became “a moment of national dialogue” (Rubin and Cummings 1989) p. 380.

  • ABC followed the broadcast with a roundtable on the film and the nuclear emergency.
  • Public school teachers told students to watch it and discuss it in class, “thereby nationalizing the discussion”.
  • President Reagan, “whose arms buildup and provocative nuclear rhetoric helped provoke the film”, watched too and said afterwards that he had been terrified by it.

“In synchronizing 100 million viewing subjects, The Day After created a national community brought together by images of their own destruction.” It “replayed the official program of Operation Cue with uncanny precision” p. 380.

Same script, different survival

The film follows several “idealized” Midwestern families. Its first hour covers everyday life in Lawrence against rising international tension; its second covers the brief attack and life afterwards. The film “rehearses the lessons of Operation Cue with eerie precision”: “the state is absent and it is up to citizens to step in to provide order, food, and medical care to survivors” p. 380.

The key difference is the nature of “survival”. Cue argued that life was possible after attack and that nuclear war was “simply another form of everyday risk (alongside weather, fire, and traffic accidents)”. The Day After “questioned whether life was worth living after nuclear war” pp. 380–381. fn 18 Physicians for Social Responsibility (PSR), one of the strongest voices in the 1980s antinuclear movement, publicized the health effects of nuclear war with detailed descriptions of probable attacks on US cities. For this public health campaign PSR won the Nobel Peace Prize. It used the same elements as 1950s civil defense, with more detail on radiation injury and casualties, to resist the Reagan administration's renewed nuclear project (Forrow and Sidel 1998). Its protagonist, a doctor played by Jason Robards, ends dying of radiation sickness in what may be the ruins of his former home, his wife and children dead. “No triumphal narrative of survival and reinvention here.” The film's closing text warns:

The catastrophic events you have witnessed are, in all likelihood, less severe than the destruction that would actually occur in the event of a full nuclear strike against the United States. It is hoped that the images of this film will inspire the nations of this earth, their peoples and leaders, to find the means to avert the fateful day.

The Day After, closing text · p. 381
Masco's view

“The ‘realism’ of The Day After is revealed in the end to be a deception—just as it was in the Operation Cue film a generation earlier—as the filmmakers are forced to admit that the horror of nuclear war is ultimately unrepresentable” p. 381.

Derrida: “fabulously textual”

The film's simulated realism illustrates Derrida's (1984) claim that nuclear war is “fabulously textual”: until it happens it can only be imagined, and once it happens it ends the human archive. As “the only ‘remainderless event’”, nuclear war belongs to the sublime, “ungraspable and subject only to displacements, compensations, and misrecognitions”. Rehearsing it in imagination or through civil defense does not master the event. “Rather, one domesticates an image of a postnuclear world that ‘stands in’ for the inevitable failure of the imagination to be able to conceive of the end.” This postnuclear imagination is “necessarily an arena of cultural work”, which early officials recognized. It “promotes an idea of order out of the sublime and often becomes a space of pure ideology” pp. 381–382.

A full generation after Cue, filmmakers could rehearse the entire 1950s program “with startling specificity”. “The state was no longer needed to enact this national melodrama of destruction”: its terms were installed in American culture, “simply subject to citation and repositioning”. The entertainment industry supplied firestorm and fallout as “special effects”. This time the ruins were engineered “not to ‘emotionally inoculate’ Americans to nuclear war but, rather, to shock them into action during the nuclear emergency of the early 1980s” p. 382.

The same images, inverted

The Reagan administration's escalating arms race and talk of “winnable” nuclear wars (Sheer 1982) prompted “a cultural return to the images and logics of Operation Cue, mobilized this time as a call to political action rather than normalization”. The form and content of cinematic destruction stayed the same from 1955 to 1983, but “its emotional project has been inverted from promoting the docility of the citizen–subject to mobilizing a national community before the bombs fall”. The Day After reenacted Cue's melodrama 28 years later “not to produce a ‘bomb proof’ society but, rather, as a de facto form of nuclear critique” p. 382.

Activist groups, including Physicians for Social Responsibility and FREEZE, likewise used targeting and blast-damage maps of US cities and medical analyses of radiation injuries against rising military budgets and late Cold War tensions. fn 19 In response to FREEZE, the Reagan administration pursued “a new emotional management strategy” in the Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI), which promised to end the arms race with space-based lasers that would shoot down ICBMs (FitzGerald 2000). Offered as a near-term technological fix, “it was never a realistic proposal. Over 25 years and $100 billion later it has yet to hit a real world target”, but “it has served the emotional needs of its constituency very well—allowing an aggressive pursuit of nuclear weapons technology and the militarization of space all the while praising the goal of disarmament”. The Day After, FREEZE and SDI were all emotional management campaigns that drew on the images of the aboveground testing era: activists for disarmament, the state for the arms race. The calibration of emotional management “was no longer controlled solely by the government”, which allowed counterformulations from the same texts and images p. 382.

Masco's view

“Nuclear ruins are revealed here to be the very grammar of nuclear discourse in the United States—enabling both pronuclear and antinuclear projects”, always used “to articulate the affective terms of national belonging”. Despite its implicit critique, The Day After still mobilizes a nuclear-bombed United States “as a call to American community rather than as a marker of the end of sociability itself” p. 383.

Afterimage 2: Armageddon/Deep Impact (1998)

Claim
Germany repressed its ruins, but the United States proliferated images of its own destruction. The 1990s blockbusters turned nuclear fear into patriotic “post-traumatic play” that relegitimized nuclear weapons and prefigured the war on terror.
Evidence

There was a tacit agreement, equally binding on everyone, that the true state of material and moral ruin in which the country found itself was not to be described. The darkest aspects of the final act of destruction, as experienced by the great majority of the German population, remained under a kind of taboo like a shameful family secret, a secret that could not even be privately acknowledged.

W. G. Sebald, On the Natural History of Destruction (epigraph, p. 383)

Germany repressed; America proliferated

Questioning the near-total silence in German literature about life in the bombed-out ruins of World War II, W. G. Sebald finds an “extraordinary faculty for self-anesthesia shown by a community that seemed to have emerged from a war of annihilation without any signs of psychological impairment” (2004:11). He traces the silence about Dresden and other firebombed cities to a shared understanding that Germany had pioneered mass bombing in Guernica, Warsaw and Belgrade (2004:104). The repression is doubled: of life in the ruins, and of Germany's earlier role as mass bomber, “linking trauma and destruction as part of the same psychosocial legacy” p. 383.

Masco's view

The United States, “a country that did not experience mass bombing in World War II but did conduct it using both conventional and nuclear weapons”, “took an opposite national-cultural route in the Cold War”. Although nuclear war never came, “rather than repress the bomb, American culture proliferated its meaning and influence” (Brians 2006; Evans 1998; Sontag 1966). The bomb became “an intimate part of U.S. popular and political culture”, installed in the 1950s, that “soon functioned outside the direct control of the national security state”. “We live today in the world made by the Cold War, a global project that engineered everyday life—and life itself—around the technological means of apocalyptic destruction” (Masco 2006) p. 383.

Blockbuster ruins

What to make of 1990s Hollywood? This was “the first period in the nuclear age in which U.S. national security was not structured in relationship to a nuclear armed, external enemy”, yet films repeatedly destroyed the nation, reducing US cities to ruins “all in the name of fun”. Blockbusters with “fearsome life-ending asteroids, aliens, earthquakes, floods, and wars” let Americans “rehearse destruction of their nation-state much as their parents and grandparents did in the 1950s and 1980s”. These “technoaesthetic displays of finely rendered destruction are a unique form of American expressive culture” p. 384.

Masco's view

“Only U.S. cinema deploys the cutting edge technological achievements of computer-generated imagery to visualize the destruction of its cities, and does so with such fetishistic glee” p. 384.

fn 20 The destruction of cities recurs in Japanese nuclear cinema, but it is not mobilized for militarized nation-building as in the US (Broderick 1996). And there is “a fundamental difference” between the disaster movie and nuclear cinema. The disaster film uses destruction for drama at an individual level, while “nuclear cinema always nationalizes its content via a friend–enemy configuration”; its project is “to establish and mobilize a national community through mass violence” (also Evans 1998; Sontag 1966; Taylor 1997 on nuclear photography; Virilio 1989 on war and perception).

Consider 1998, when the United States was “cinematically attacked twice by asteroids”: Armageddon (directed by Michael Bay) and Deep Impact (directed by Mimi Leder), “the second and eighth most successful films of the year at the box office”. In both, life on Earth is saved at the last minute “by the heroics of Americans armed with nuclear weapons” (Figs 6–7) p. 384.

  • Armageddon uses the threat “as a vehicle for resuscitating working-class masculinity as protectors of the nation–planet”: oil riggers are sent on the space shuttle to destroy the asteroid with atomic bombs.
  • Deep Impact “is a study of civil defense and individual sacrifice right out of Operation Cue”.
  • In both, “the destruction of the United States is presented as a form of entertainment and redemptive play”.
Masco's view

These films belong to a series of 1990s summer films that “rehearse the destruction of the United States and demonstrate the necessity of U.S. nuclear weapons”, and “we have to read this as a moment of psychic and cultural release from the Cold War arms race” (cf. Rogin 1998; Davis 2001; Mellor 2007). With life no longer hanging “so oppressively in the nuclear balance”, the cinematic imagination was freed to explore the end of the United States in a new way. Cold War nuclear cinema always had a moral point: detonation was marked as “a political–ethical–technological failure of the Cold War system”. “In striking contrast, post–Cold War films have no purpose other than patriotism and pleasure; they seek to reinstall American identity through mass violence, suggesting that it is only threat and reactions to threat that can create national community” pp. 384–385. fn 21 On “regeneration through violence” in the United States: Slotkin (1996); Rogin (1987, 1998).

Nuclear texts

Whatever their form (“asteroids, or tsunamis, or alien invaders”), these spectacles “function as nuclear texts because they use mass destruction as a means of mobilizing the United States as a global superpower”. As allegories of nuclear war they reproduce the emotional language of nuclear threat, “mass death as a vehicle for establishing national community”, and “allow a productive misrecognition of its political content”. Through aestheticized politics they reinforce “the ever-present need for war, and the ubiquity of external enemies with apocalyptic power”. They relegitimize nuclear weapons and present the United States “as a reluctant superpower forced into global military action for the greater good” pp. 385–386.

As meteorites devastate New York in Armageddon, a taxi driver yells: “We at war! Saddam Hussein is bombing us!” This 1998 scene “prefigures the Bush administration's successful (but fabricated) effort to link the terrorist attacks on New York and Washington in 2001 to the Iraqi government of Saddam Hussein”. Naming the enemy this way “underscores the ideological alignment between the Hollywood blockbuster and the U.S. military, which both rely on rehearsing threat as a means of stabilizing their industries” p. 386. fn 22 Armageddon was supported by NASA and the Department of Defense, which let producer Jerry Bruckheimer film at military installations “in exchange for script changes that favored the Armed Services” (Robb 2004:94–95). See also Rogin (1987) on Cold War cinema and his reading (1998) of the militarized gender and race politics of Independence Day (1996). Viewers, “as Benjamin saw so early on (1969)”, absorb such projects “in a state of distraction, allowing both the covert habituation of ideas and a broader aestheticizing of politics in support of increasing militarization and war” (also Sontag 1966).

Post-traumatic play

One afterimage of the Cold War emotional management campaign is “this continued commitment to, and pleasure in, making nuclear ruins, and then searching the wreckage for signs about the collective future”. 1990s nuclear cinema “transforms anonymous mass death into a vehicle for individuals to demonstrate their moral character and for the nation to be regenerated through apocalyptic threat”. Its pleasure lies in “witnessing the destruction of the United States, and then walking out of the theater into the unbroken world” p. 386.

Masco's view

Unlike audiences of Operation Cue in 1955 or The Day After in 1983, viewers of Armageddon and Deep Impact “were not addressed as citizens that needed to demonstrate their civic virtue by performing nuclear fear”. The emotional management strategy “was transformed into a form of post-traumatic play”, with national destruction “presented as a diversion rather than a serious threat or opportunity for political mobilization”. Even so, we should notice “the remarkable coherence of the nuclear images from 1955 to 1998”, and question the effects of “repetitively producing images of destroyed U.S. cities to constitute both pleasure and national community” p. 386.

Epilogue

pp. 387–390 · Bush's mushroom cloud · color codes and shampoo bottles · Pentagons and the skyline · Musharraf's Stone Age · Figures 8–9

Claim
The “war on terror” was run largely as an emotional management campaign. It redeployed Cold War nuclear fear to justify preemptive war and to export ruins, and it left Americans docile, afraid and unable to treat nuclear technology as a problem of global governance.
Evidence
Bush's 7 October 2002 speech; Musharraf's report; color-coded alerts and the 2006 liquids ban; AEC images of the 1952 H-bomb test (Figs 8–9); new warhead designs (2006).
Against
The post-9/11 consensus that “the Cold War is over”.

Facing clear evidence of peril, we cannot wait for the final proof, the smoking gun that could come in the form of a mushroom cloud.

President George W. Bush, address to the nation on Iraq, 7 October 2002 (epigraph, p. 387)

Be prepared to be bombed. Be prepared to go back to the Stone Age.

President Musharraf of Pakistan, reporting a message from the US State Department immediately after 9/11 (epigraph, p. 387)

“Reclaiming the emotional history of the atomic bomb is crucial today, as nuclear fear has been amplified to enable a variety of political projects at precisely the moment American memory of the bomb has become impossibly blurred.” In the United States nuclear fear has recently justified “preemptive war and unlimited domestic surveillance, a worldwide system of secret prisons, and the practices of rendition, torture, and assassination” p. 387.

Masco's view

“We live not in the ruins produced by Soviet ICBMs but, rather, in the emotional ruins of the Cold War as an intellectual and social project.” The half-century project to articulate the nation through contemplating its violent end “has colonized the present”. The 2001 attacks may have produced a consensus that “the Cold War is over” and a formal counterterrorism project, but American reactions “were structured by a multigenerational state project to harness the fear of mass death to divergent political and military industrial agendas” p. 387. fn 23 The Bush doctrine of preemptive war (the National Security Strategy, 2002) “formally ended the Cold War doctrines of containment and deterrence that had defined U.S. foreign policy since the 1950s” (Gaddis 1982).

The mushroom cloud and the color codes

By evoking the mushroom cloud to enable the invasion of Iraq, Bush “appealed directly to citizens' nuclear fear, a cultural product of the very Cold War nuclear standoff he formally disavowed”. The imagery and the “totalizing immediacy” of the threat redeployed a memory of apocalyptic threat from “the four decades of the Soviet–American nuclear arms race” as part of the new “war on terror” p. 387.

The new color-coded terrorist warning system, “first proposed by Project East River in 1952 to deal with Soviet bombers”, and the recent transformation of “shampoo bottles on planes into a totalizing threat by the Homeland Security Administration” are “official efforts to install and regulate fear in everyday life” p. 387. fn 24 In August 2006, after British authorities broke up a plot to smuggle explosive chemicals onto an airliner, the Department of Homeland Security raised the threat level to its highest point, “red”, and the TSA banned fluids on domestic flights. The plot “was in the very early discussion phase and not a viable threat to passenger safety, yet the response from DHS and TSA was total” (see the 10 August 2006 press conference by Michael Chertoff, Alberto Gonzales, Robert Mueller and Kip Hawley). “This total response to a fantasy threat does not make sense as a security strategy, but is an excellent illustration of the mobilization of affect, as well as the demand for public docility.” Expert analysis: Raw Story; Bruce Schneier's blog.

Masco's view

“The ‘war on terror’ has been conducted largely as an emotional management campaign in the United States, using the tropes and logics developed during the early Cold War to enable a new kind of U.S. geopolitical project.” It “redirects but also reiterates the American assumptions about mass violence and democracy I have explored in this essay” pp. 387–388.

Why 9/11 felt familiar

If the September 11 attacks “felt strangely familiar to many U.S. citizens, it was because American society has been imaginatively rehearsing the destruction of these cities for over three generations”, in the civil defense campaigns of the early and late Cold War and in the 1990s blockbusters, “which destroyed these cities each summer with increasing nuance and detail”. The genealogy of this entertainment “is traumatic”: it goes back to Hiroshima and Nagasaki and to Cold War propaganda. The ease with which 9/11 was “nationalized as part of a nuclear discourse by the Bush administration has much to do with this legacy” (Kaplan 2003) p. 388.

“Not coincidentally, the two graphic measures of nuclear blast damage most frequently used during the Cold War were the Pentagon and the New York City skyline” (cf. Eden 2004). Figures 8 and 9 come from the Atomic Energy Commission's campaign to show the size of the first US hydrogen bomb test, in 1952. Fourteen true-to-scale Pentagons, the Pentagon being “identified by the AEC as the largest building in the world”, are placed inside the blast crater, “the former Elugelab Island”. The New York skyline shows the detonation's “vast horizontal and vertical scope” p. 388. Not in Masco The 1952 shot was “Ivy Mike”, at Enewetak Atoll in the Marshall Islands.

Masco's view

9/11 was easily “transformed into a nuclear discourse precisely because our security culture has imagined and rehearsed attacks on Washington and New York for generations”. The symbols attacked, the Pentagon and the tallest building in the New York skyline, had been used by the nuclear state “for three generations as part of its emotional management strategy”. The Bush administration mobilized “a well-established logic of nuclear attack”, “translating discrete, nonnuclear threats into the emotional equivalent of the Cold War nuclear crisis” p. 388.

Exporting ruins

“For a nation that constructs itself via discourses of ruination, it should not be a surprise to see the exportation of ruins on a global scale.” Musharraf “clearly understood” that the Bush administration's “with us or against us” logic of 2001 left no ambiguity about the costs of Pakistan not aligning “with the sole global superpower”. The threat to reduce Pakistan to the “Stone Age” is “the alternative, international deployment of nuclear fear, constituting a U.S. promise to reduce his country to a prenational, pretechnological state” pp. 388–389.

Masco's closing

“The United States enters the 21st century as a nation both fascinated and traumatized by nuclear ruins.” It turns real and imagined mass death into a nationalized space and “supports a political culture that believes bombing campaigns can produce democracy abroad”. It is “simultaneously terrorized by nuclear weapons and threatens to use them”. Its military “both wages preemptive war over nascent ‘WMD’ programs and is preparing to build a new generation of U.S. nuclear weapons” p. 389. fn 25 In fall 2006, under directives from the Bush administration, Los Alamos and Lawrence Livermore submitted to the Department of Energy “their first new nuclear weapons designs since the end of the Cold War”, a first step toward renewed production and perhaps underground testing (see the 2002 Nuclear Posture Review).

“American society is today neither ‘atomic bomb proof’ nor capable of engaging nuclear technologies as a global problem of governance.” Citizens “live today in the emotional residues of the Cold War nuclear arms race, which can only address them as fearful docile bodies”. “Thus, even in the 21st century, Americans remain caught between terror and fear, trapped in the psychosocial space defined by the once and future promise of nuclear ruins” pp. 389–390.

Quick revision

Both readings compressed. Read the ten-line versions, then the comparison, then test yourself further down. The INT354 lens questions are answered at the end of this kit.

Life Underground in ten lines

  1. The bomb made the US a bunker society, fixated on attack and fantasizing about mental and physical fortresses p. 13.
  2. The state chose to manage nuclear fear rather than eliminate it, normalizing a nuclear state of emergency as “national security” p. 14.
  3. Rand (1958) planned to put 4 million New Yorkers 800 ft under Manhattan. Gaither (1957) invented the “missile gap” and proposed $55 bn of shelters to show a “will to live”.
  4. A dual project: harden technologies and psychologies; build a citizen-soldier “immune to panic but ... motivated by nuclear fear” p. 16.
  5. NORAD (1958; 2,400 ft down; 31,000 weapons by 1965) is the “ultimate panopticon”. Edwards's “closed world” reduces the world to data points.
  6. Civil defense was “less about the protection of citizens and cities than about the emotional training of the populace”, the largest federal media campaign ever pp. 19–20.
  7. Shelters were first naturalized, then romanticized as frontier dream spaces: FCDA photographs, gendered duties, consumer goods, Kennedy's $400 m, fun rooms.
  8. The campaign pictured an “exclusively white nuclear family” in the suburbs. Most Americans could only duck and cover, “the ultimate Cold War posture” p. 25.
  9. Critics and Dr. Strangelove exposed the bunker as “pure masculine fantasy” p. 26.
  10. After 9/11 (Cheney's “secure, undisclosed location”) the bunker became a national security culture of “bunkers of the imagination” and a “perverse concept of security”. Masco says leave the bunker and demilitarize pp. 27–28.

“Survival Is Your Business” in ten lines

  1. The first nuclear decade produced a social contract based on “the national contemplation of ruins”. The US became “the most nuclear-bombed country on earth” (1,054 tests) p. 361.
  2. Civil defense was “psychological reprogramming” and “a willful act of fabulation”, not defense p. 363.
  3. NSC 68 (1950) set out a permanent wartime economy and treated internal dissent as the greatest threat. The result was the “Cold War consensus” p. 364.
  4. Planners feared both apathy and terror. Project East River (1952) predicted panic and breakdown along race and class lines p. 366.
  5. Emotional management (Oakes) turned paralysing nuclear terror into functional nuclear fear. Panic, not nuclear war, became the enemy, so responsibility shifted to citizens. Peterson: “panic is fissionable” pp. 366–367.
  6. Atoms for Peace abroad, fear at home: the largest domestic propaganda campaign, with Hiroshima's injured bodies censored. Janis called it “emotional inoculation”: “fear but not terror” pp. 367–368.
  7. Every city a target (70 on the 1955 map; 303 ground zeros). Operation Alert (1953–61) had Americans act out their own incineration pp. 369–370.
  8. Operation Cue (5 May 1955, 100 m viewers): mannequins, the nuclearized family, the paramilitary housewife, potpies, a “giant picnic”, and “the total pattern to condition civilians” pp. 372–377.
  9. The 1963 test ban froze the visual record. The Day After (1983) inverted the images into critique; the 1998 asteroid films made them “post-traumatic play” and “nuclear texts” pp. 378–386.
  10. After 9/11 came Bush's “mushroom cloud”, color codes and the export of ruins. The war on terror is an emotional management campaign, and Americans stay “caught between terror and fear” pp. 387–390.

Masco's own verdicts

  • The bomb's first great effect was a bunker society (L p. 13).
  • The state managed fear instead of ending it, and called the emergency “national security” (L p. 14).
  • Civil defense was emotional training, not protection (L p. 19); “a willful act of fabulation” (S p. 363).
  • It made panic, not nuclear war, the enemy, shifting responsibility to citizens (S p. 366).
  • Its aim: “fear but not terror, anxiety but not panic” (S p. 368).
  • Duck and cover is “the ultimate Cold War posture” (L p. 25); Strangelove shows “pure masculine fantasy” (L p. 26).
  • Operation Cue was questionable science but productive theatre; its motto is not kitsch (S p. 378).
  • Garrison is wrong: civil defense built emotional infrastructure, and succeeded (S fn 14).
  • The realism of both Cue and The Day After is a deception (S p. 381).
  • Nuclear ruins are “the very grammar of nuclear discourse” (S p. 383).
  • The US proliferated the bomb's meaning instead of repressing it (S p. 383).
  • Post–Cold War films serve only “patriotism and pleasure” (S p. 385).
  • The war on terror is an emotional management campaign (S pp. 387–388); the 2006 liquids response answered “a fantasy threat” (S fn 24).
  • The bunker society is integrated, not outmoded; security spending breeds insecurity (L pp. 27–28).
  • Americans should leave the bunker and demilitarize (L p. 28); they remain “between terror and fear” (S p. 390).

Traps

  • Terror is not fear. The program aimed to convert paralysing “nuclear terror” into functional “nuclear fear”.
  • Whose term? Emotional management is Oakes's, emotional inoculation Janis's, paramilitary housewife McEnaney's, closed world Edwards's, “fabulously textual” Derrida's, self-anesthesia Sebald's. Masco's own: bunker society, nationalization of death, nuclearized family, post-traumatic play, bunkers of the imagination.
  • Operation Alert is not Operation Cue. Alert was the yearly simulated attack and evacuation (1953–61); the 1959 mothers' protest was against Alert. Cue was the single 1955 test on a model town in Nevada.
  • Two different 100 millions. The 100 million viewers of Cue (1955) and The Day After (1983) are not the “100 million American lives” in Martin and Latham's Russian Roulette.
  • Dates. NSC 68 1950; Janis 1951; East River 1952; Peterson 1953; the Human Effects study 1956; Gaither 1957; NORAD 1958; the test ban 1963.
  • The FCDA is the Federal Civil Defense Administration. Masco once writes “Agency” (S p. 367) and once prints “FDCA” (S p. 375).
  • Peterson as “first head”. Masco says so (S p. 366, fn 9). Not in Masco Millard Caldwell (1950–52) was the first FCDA administrator; Peterson served 1953–57. In a quiz on this reading, give Masco's wording.
  • Dr. Strangelove's date. 1963 in the text, 1964 in the reading list (L). Not in Masco It was released in January 1964.
  • Spellings in the source. “McEaney” is McEnaney, “Lanstreet” is Landstreet, “Vanderbuilt” is Vanderbilt. Not in Masco “Sheer 1982” is Robert Scheer's book.
  • Which money? $55 bn is Gaither's shelter plan; $400 m is Kennedy's; $100 bn is SDI (fn 19); $1 trillion is both 2007 military spending (L) and RAND's 2006 estimate of a Los Angeles bomb's cost (S fn 8).
  • Independence Day (1996) appears only in fn 22. The two 1998 films are Armageddon and Deep Impact.

For INT354: the course lens

Whose order, whose justice?
The order Masco describes is domestic and emotional. It is the security state's “Cold War consensus” of anticommunism, capitalism and military expansion, the consolidation of federal power, and a citizen–state bond “mediated by nuclear fear”. The international order of containment and deterrence (NORAD's Mutual Assured Destruction) rested on it. The costs of that order are injustices. Citizens were made responsible for their own survival and trained into docility. People the campaign left out were erased: non-white and urban Americans, against an “exclusively white nuclear family”, and Project East River feared breakdown “along race and class lines”. Hiroshima and Nagasaki's injured were hidden from view. 1,054 tests spread health effects to “each and every U.S. citizen”. Abroad, the costs fell on Iraq and on Pakistan, threatened with the “Stone Age”. Bull (Week 1) names the “balance of terror” as an institution that offends against justice yet sustains order; Masco shows its domestic side and sides with justice. His answer to the security order is to demilitarize.
Which actors?
Centre: the US federal security state (FCDA, NSC, Rand, the Gaither Committee, NORAD, AEC and Los Alamos, the Bush administration, DHS and TSA) and its experts (Kahn, Janis, Peterson, Martin and Latham). Partners: advertising experts, 150+ industrial associations, J. C. Penney, ABC, Hollywood (with NASA and Pentagon help). The public as audience and test subject: families, housewives, schoolchildren, mannequins, Landstreet, 100 million viewers. Resisters: scientists, the 1959 mothers, PSR, FREEZE, Kubrick, the Chicago crowd. Largely absent observation not in Masco: hibakusha (present only as censored images), Marshall Islanders (Elugelab's crater is shown, not its people), Soviet citizens, and international bodies such as the IAEA and UN.
What makes something “nuclear”?
For Masco, nuclearity is also affective and visual: a thing becomes “nuclear” when it is placed inside the story of nuclear attack. A chicken potpie, a mannequin family, a suburban living room, a school basement, an asteroid film, shampoo bottles and the 9/11 attacks were all “nuclearized”. The family became a “nuclearized family”; 9/11 was “nationalized as part of a nuclear discourse”; nonnuclear threats became “the emotional equivalent of the Cold War nuclear crisis”; asteroid films are “nuclear texts”. The reverse also happened. Censorship of Hiroshima's bodies and the test ban's frozen record made real radiological harm less visible. Compare Hecht's nuclearity (Week 4), “a technopolitical spectrum that shifts in time and space”. Masco adds a register of fear that can attach to things with no radioactivity at all Comparison not in Masco.

Links to other INT354 readings

Bull, “Order vs. Justice in International Society” (Wk 1)
Bull defines order as a pattern sustaining “the elementary or primary goals of social coexistence”. He names the superpowers' “balance of terror” as an institution that “offends against ordinary and widely agreed notions of international justice and also human justice” yet is central to order. Masco turns the phrase inward: “the domestic form of the ‘balance of terror’” (L p. 20), and a story “from the Cold War's ‘balance of terror’ through the current ‘war on terror’” (S p. 362).
Bensaude-Vincent, Boudia and Sato, “Introduction: shaping the nuclear order” (Wk 2)
They widen “nuclear order” beyond IR's deterrence and non-proliferation to “the dimensions of the nuclear domain that constitute and mediate our experience of the world”. They ask how nuclear technology “became mundane” and how “adaptation to disasters and the forgetting of crises can be manufactured”. Masco's emotional management is a worked case. Their introduction also cites Masco's chapter in the same volume on Kodak's recordings of Pacific tests, and notes that curated mushroom-cloud images became “deeply engraved in the popular imaginary”.
Kraft, “Dissenting Scientists in Early Cold War Britain” (Wk 2)
On 8 March 1955 Britain's secret Strath Committee report told the Cabinet there could be “no defense” against the H-bomb. Citing Hughes, Kraft notes that civil defence debate was suppressed “both to promote the credibility of the nuclear deterrent and to maintain the social and political order”. Two months later the US bombed a model suburb on live television. Same goal, opposite tactics: British secrecy, American spectacle.
Todeschini, “Illegitimate Sufferers” (Wk 3)
The occupation's Press Code, in force until 1952, suppressed information on radiation effects and kept hibakusha from learning about and sharing their condition. Masco shows the American side of the same censorship: US civil defense imagery relied on withholding “the detailed effects of the atomic bomb on the human body” (S p. 368).
Higuchi, Political Fallout, ch. 1 (Wk 3)
The US, Britain and the USSR framed test exposure as an “emergency”. They protected against large short-term doses and declared distant fallout harmless. Set Masco's 1,054 tests (fn 1), his fallout note (fn 17) and the 1963 treaty that “locked in place the visual record of the bomb” (S p. 378) beside Higuchi's account of how radiological danger was defined away.
Eisenhower, “Atoms for Peace” speech (Wk 4)
At the UN on 8 December 1953 Eisenhower promised to move “out of the dark chamber of horrors into the light” and “to hasten the day when fear of the atom will begin to disappear”. Masco: at home, the same administration “pursued an opposite emotional management strategy” (S p. 367).
Hecht, “Nuclear Ontologies” (Wk 4)
Nuclearity is “a technopolitical spectrum that shifts in time and space”. Hecht opens with Bush's 2002 claim that Iraq sought uranium from Africa; Masco's Epilogue opens with Bush's October 2002 “mushroom cloud”. Hecht shows uranium's nuclear status being contested; Masco shows nuclear fear being attached to a non-nuclear threat.
Masco, “Mutant Ecologies” (Wk 5)
Same author, 2004. Its reading of Them! (1954) argues that Americans had already “entered a postnuclear environment of their own invention”; civil defense instead promised a survivable postnuclear future. Its note on shot Galileo (Operation Plumbbob), where 100 soldiers watched a blast and were timed assembling and disassembling rifles, describes the same testing as Survival's fn 16. Survival's editor's note cites “Mutant Ecologies”.
Jacobs, “Atomic Kids” (Wk 5)
Duck and Cover (1951, made by Archer Films for the FCDA, starring Bert the Turtle) and Atomic Alert (1951, Encyclopedia Britannica Films) taught children to survive alone. Jacobs argues the message backfired: children learned that adults could not protect them. Compare Masco on duck and cover as “the ultimate Cold War posture” (L p. 25), children studying nuclear effects to keep parents on track (L p. 22), and school campaigns deploying children to educate parents (S p. 374).

Retrieval practice

Self-test

Answer out loud or on paper before revealing. If you miss one, reread that section of the summary.

Q1 · ThesisLife UndergroundWhat is a “bunker society”, and why did presenting bunker life as livable matter?

A nation “fixated on impending nuclear attack while fantasizing about life within both mental and physical fortresses”. Making the bunker seem livable, “(even exciting)”, was “a vital mechanism of militarizing American society” and set up a fantasy of “absolute and total” security (p. 13).

Q2 · Rand 1958Life UndergroundWhat did the Rand Corporation propose for New York in 1958?

Moving four million New Yorkers into shelters 800 ft under Manhattan, sealed “as in a submarine”, with Delaware Aqueduct water, diesel power, two cold and one hot meal a day, 91 entrances within 5–10 minutes' walk, and 500-psi blast doors (pp. 14–15).

Q3 · Dual projectLife UndergroundWhat was the early nuclear state's “dual project”?

“Hardening both technologies and psychologies against the bomb”: burying weapons and command centres, and engineering a citizen-soldier “immune to panic but nonetheless motivated by nuclear fear”. It made the bunker “a new site of nation and state building” (pp. 15–16).

Q4 · GaitherLife UndergroundWhat did the Gaither Committee (1957) declare and recommend?

It declared “(and invented)” a “missile gap”, which drove a massive arms buildup under late Eisenhower and Kennedy. It recommended a crash shelter program of up to $55 bn over five years, partly to show Soviet leaders an American “will to live” (pp. 16–17).

Q5 · NORADLife UndergroundGive four facts about NORAD and Masco's name for it.

Cheyenne Mountain, Colorado; authorized 1958; 2,400 ft deep in granite; 1,319 steel springs; 25-ton blast doors; a giant central screen; could coordinate 31,000 weapons by 1965. “The ultimate panopticon”, “simultaneously the most isolated and the most connected site” (pp. 17–19).

Q6 · Closed worldLife UndergroundHow does Masco use Paul Edwards's “closed world”?

Containment found its technological metaphor in the computer screen. NORAD models a world of preselected data points that gets naturalized “as the world itself”, erasing culture, intentions, mistakes and malfunctions in favour of “machine-to-machine assessment” (pp. 18–19).

Q7 · Civil defenseLife UndergroundWhat was 1950s civil defense “less about”, and what was it really for?

Less about protecting citizens and cities than about “the emotional training of the populace” and converting citizens into “Cold War Warriors”. It was the largest federal media campaign in US history, teaching “just enough” to mobilize support without terror or a movement against the Cold War (pp. 19–20).

Q8 · PamphletLife UndergroundWhat tension ran through the 1959 pamphlet “Ten for Survival”?

It promised “knowledge is survival power” while describing heat hotter than the sun and kill radii of 8 miles (1 Mt) and 20 miles (20 Mt), a picture that “overwhelms ducking and covering as a mode of protection” (p. 20).

Q9 · PhotographsLife UndergroundHow did the FCDA's home-shelter photographs present the shelter?

As “pure dreamspace”, “militarized, privatized, post-nation-state”: a calm lawn hatch, three happy generations, “quality family time”, a bachelor-pad retreat, a pioneer farm family. Duties split by gender and age: men build, women handle food and first aid, children study nuclear effects (pp. 20–23).

Q10 · ExclusionLife UndergroundWhom did the FCDA campaign picture, and whom did it leave out?

Assuming best-case attacks, it pictured the middle-class suburban family, “an exclusively white nuclear family”. It left out “a vast population of Americans” and ignored that most lived in cities (p. 25).

Q11 · StrangeloveLife UndergroundWhat does Masco read in the ending of Dr. Strangelove?

A plan for “a nucleus of human specimens” in mine shafts at a 10:1 ratio of women to men, and a race against a “mine shaft gap”: the bunker as “pure masculine fantasy” in “an erotics of death” (p. 26).

Q12 · TodayLife UndergroundWhy, for Masco, do Americans “not build nuclear shelters anymore”?

Not because the bunker society is outmoded but because it is “so completely integrated into public life”. Citizens now enter “bunkers of the imagination”, which free the security state to act globally (p. 27).

Q13 · SecurityLife UndergroundWhat is the “perverse concept of security”?

Security aimed “not on stopping violence but on preparing psychologically to endure it”. Rituals like airport screening, surveillance and huge budgets create vulnerability, which justifies more security and militarization. The US has under 5% of world population and spent ≈$1 trillion in 2007 (pp. 27–28).

Q14 · Social contractSurvivalState Masco's “new kind of social contract”.

Formed in the first nuclear decade, “based not on the protection and improvement of everyday life but, rather, on the national contemplation of ruins” (p. 361).

Q15 · NSC 68SurvivalWhat did NSC 68 (1950) do, according to Masco?

It formalized containment and proposed “a total mobilization of American society based on the experience of WWII”: a permanent wartime posture funded by a growing consumer economy, with internal dissent as perhaps the greatest threat. It helped create the “Cold War consensus” of anticommunism, capitalism and military expansion (pp. 363–364).

Q16 · East RiverSurvivalWhat did Project East River (1952) predict, and what did it later hand the war on terror?

All-out panic and mob behaviour after an attack, with society breaking down “along race and class lines”. It first proposed the color-coded warning system later used against terrorism (pp. 366, 387).

Q17 · Terror vs fearSurvivalDistinguish “nuclear terror” from “nuclear fear”. Whose framework is this?

Terror was read as a paralysing emotion; fear is “an affective state that would allow citizens to function in a time of crisis”. Civil defense aimed to turn the first into the second. Guy Oakes's “emotional management” (1994), drawing on Project East River (p. 366).

Q18 · PanicSurvivalWho said “panic is fissionable”, and what did civil defense do with responsibility for survival?

Val Peterson (1953): 90% of emergency measures depend on preventing panic in the first 90 seconds; “mass panic—not the A-bomb” may be the ultimate weapon. Civil defense “shifted responsibility for nuclear war from the state to its citizens by making public panic the enemy” (pp. 366–367).

Q19 · InoculationSurvivalWhat is “emotional inoculation”, and whose phrase is it?

RAND analyst Irving Janis (1951): exposure to nuclear effects calibrated to mobilize citizens without making defense seem pointless. Masco's summary: “fear but not terror, anxiety but not panic” (p. 368).

Q20 · CensorshipSurvivalWhat did the censorship of Hiroshima and Nagasaki have to do with US civil defense?

US civil defense imagery depended on it. Destroyed buildings were shown, but “the detailed effects of the atomic bomb on the human body” and some firsthand accounts were withheld, so the state could calibrate the image of atomic war (p. 368; fn 10).

Q21 · Alert 1955SurvivalGive the numbers for the 1955 Operation Alert scenario.

60 cities hit; over 8 million instant deaths and another 8 million radiation victims; 25 million homeless; fallout over about 63,000 square miles (p. 370).

Q22 · Operation CueSurvivalWhat was Operation Cue, and why does Masco call it an “ur-text”?

On 5 May 1955 at the Nevada Test Site, a model suburb furnished by 150+ industrial associations and peopled by mannequin families was atom-bombed live for 100 million viewers. Its rubble still structures US images of postnuclear ruins (pp. 372–373).

Q23 · PotpiesSurvivalWhy do chicken potpies and a picnic matter in Operation Cue?

They showed that commodities, and so familiar life, would survive. Frozen potpies were tested for radiation, nutrition, colour and taste (Project 32.5), and a post-blast meal of roast beef, tomato juice, baked beans and coffee turned destruction into “a giant picnic” (p. 375).

Q24 · LandstreetSurvivalWhat does Arthur Landstreet's account reveal?

Civilians at Position Baker, about 10,000 ft from ground zero, were part of “the total pattern to condition civilians”. Timed drills (5 minutes to get 2.5–3 miles away) trained them “not to think in a case of emergency but simply to act”, extending the citizen's docility (pp. 376–377).

Q25 · 1963SurvivalWhat did the 1963 Partial Test Ban Treaty change?

Testing went underground, so (1) the state no longer had to explain mushroom clouds and fallout, and (2) the 1945–63 images were locked in as “the visual record of the bomb” (p. 378).

Q26 · The Day AfterSurvivalHow did The Day After both repeat and invert Operation Cue?

Same script: idealized families, the attack, an absent state, citizens providing order. But it questioned whether life was worth living, and was engineered to shock viewers into action, “a de facto form of nuclear critique”. Its closing “realism” disclaimer echoes Cue's (pp. 380–382).

Q27 · DerridaSurvivalWhat does Masco take from Derrida?

Nuclear war is “fabulously textual” and the only “remainderless event”: it can only be imagined, so rehearsals domesticate a stand-in image of the postnuclear world, which “often becomes a space of pure ideology” (pp. 381–382).

Q28 · SebaldSurvivalHow does the US differ from Sebald's Germany?

Germany repressed its ruins, a “self-anesthesia” doubled by its record as pioneer of mass bombing (Guernica, Warsaw, Belgrade). The US, a mass bomber never itself bombed, “proliferated” the bomb's meaning through its culture (p. 383).

Q29 · 1998SurvivalWhy are Armageddon and Deep Impact “nuclear texts”?

They use mass destruction to mobilize the US as superpower, relegitimize nuclear weapons and cast the US as “a reluctant superpower”. They are “post-traumatic play” with “no purpose other than patriotism and pleasure” (pp. 384–386).

Q30 · 9/11SurvivalWhy did 9/11 feel “strangely familiar”, according to Masco?

Three generations of rehearsal in civil defense and blockbusters, and because the Pentagon and the New York skyline were the Cold War's standard measures of blast damage, as in the AEC's images of the 1952 H-bomb test (p. 388).

Q31 · GarrisonSurvivalDid civil defense fail? Give Garrison's view and Masco's reply.

Garrison (2006): a failure, because no nationwide shelter system was built. Masco: it built emotional, not urban, infrastructure, and succeeded. It kept public support for the nuclear state through the Cold War and supplied the “war on terror” (fn 14).

Q32 · Course lensBothWhose order, whose justice, in Masco's account?

The order of the national security state and its “Cold War consensus”, resting on a citizen–state bond mediated by fear. Its costs fall on citizens made responsible and docile, on those excluded (non-white and urban Americans), on Hiroshima's hidden victims, and abroad (Iraq; Pakistan's “Stone Age”). Compare Bull: the “balance of terror” sustains order while offending justice.

Q33 · Cross-weekBothHow does Kraft's British case (Week 2) contrast with Operation Cue?

In March 1955 Britain's secret Strath report found “no defense” against the H-bomb, and civil defence debate was suppressed to protect deterrence and “social and political order”. Two months later the US bombed a model suburb on live TV. Secrecy and spectacle served the same end.
