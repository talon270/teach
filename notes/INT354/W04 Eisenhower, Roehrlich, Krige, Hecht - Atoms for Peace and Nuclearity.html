Atoms for Peace & Nuclearity

Summaries of this week's seven readings. The first four follow Eisenhower's 1953 proposal as it became the International Atomic Energy Agency: the speech, two histories of the negotiations, and a study of science as diplomacy and surveillance. The last three are Gabrielle Hecht's essays on nuclearity, the contested question of what counts as “nuclear” and who gets to decide. Every section and note is covered. After the summaries come a revision kit, an evidence ledger, a self-test and a coverage audit.

The week in one breath: one line per reading

1 · Eisenhower (1953). The US monopoly is over and no arsenal prevents surprise attack. So the powers “principally involved”, the USSR included, should give fissile material to a UN-linked agency. It would serve “the needs rather than the fears of mankind”, and its “great virtue” is needing no world-wide inspection.

2 · Roehrlich, ch. 2 (2022). From Operation Candor to the Statute, 1953–57. The “pool” faded; safeguards “moved from the margins to the center”. The hardest fight was India against South Africa over the Board. The word “proliferation” came up once, about paper.

3 · Krige (2006). Atoms for Peace was “polyvalent”: propaganda, diversion, industrial policy and a conference. Isotope exports and the 1955 Geneva meeting show internationalism and intelligence as “two sides of the same coin”. Americans went home “chastened”, Soviets “reassured”.

4 · Roehrlich (2016). The IAEA's founding is a North–South story. Uranium producers and aid recipients were rival blocs, and the IAEA was the first major body shaped by developing countries. Safeguards were called “atomic colonialism”, and Strauss said of proliferation: “They will in any event.”

5 · Hecht, “Negotiating Global Nuclearities” (2006). Through South Africa's archives: the separation of technology and politics was “discursive and never enacted in practice”. Donald Sole kept apartheid on the Board by calling the IAEA technical, performing a western and African identity, and calibrating a “barometer of nuclearity”.

6 · Hecht, “Nuclear Ontologies” (2006). “Uranium” is as underspecified as “Africa”. Nuclearity “is a technopolitical spectrum that shifts in time and space”. Colonial and nuclear rupture-talk intertwined. By 1972 ore was no longer nuclear, and miners, dose rules and depleted uranium show the human cost.

7 · Hecht, “The Power of Nuclear Things” (2010). Three genealogies of “uranium from Africa”. Stripping ore of nuclearity created “the uranium market”. Niger's Diori priced exceptionalism and France answered with “the banality of the market”. Namibia's flag swaps erased African provenance.

How the seven readings fit

The speech and its afterlife (1–4). Eisenhower's text is the primary source; Roehrlich's chapter and article are the institutional history of how it became the IAEA, built on the same archives and often the same sentences. Krige supplies the other half of Atoms for Peace: the isotope programme and the 1955 Geneva conference, where sharing science was also a way of watching. Read together, they show a proposal framed as arms control turning into a promotional agency with safeguards attached.

Nuclearity (5–7). Hecht's three essays reread the same founding through South Africa and Africa's uranium. Where Roehrlich sees Cold War cooperation plus North–South bargaining, Hecht sees a fight over what counts as “nuclear”: whether uranium ore makes a state “advanced”, whether mines are nuclear workplaces, whether provenance can be erased. Krige borrows her “barometer of nuclearity”; she cites Krige's “panopticon”. Reading 6 condenses Reading 5 and extends it; Reading 7 carries the argument into markets, Niger and Namibia.

Reading 1 · Primary source · IAEA web transcript, 5 printed sheets · pp. 1–4

Atoms for Peace

Dwight D. Eisenhower to the UN General Assembly, 8 December 1953

The speech in one breath

Eisenhower tells the General Assembly, in “the language of atomic warfare”, what the bomb has become: 42 US tests since 16 July 1945, bombs 25 times stronger than the first, hydrogen weapons in the millions of tons, and a stockpile greater than all the explosives of the Second World War. The monopoly is over: Britain, Canada and the Soviet Union have the secret, others will follow, and no quantity of weapons or defences can prevent a surprise attack. Retaliation is real but “is not the true expression of the purpose and the hopes of the United States”. After listing the West's offers to negotiate (Germany, Austria, Korea, a four-Power meeting), he takes up the Assembly's resolution of 28 November 1953 and proposes a “new conception”: the atom must be taken out of the soldiers' hands and “put into the hands of those who will know how to strip its military casing and adapt it to the arts of peace”. The governments “principally involved”, the Soviet Union among them, should contribute uranium and fissionable material from their stockpiles to “an international atomic energy agency … under the aegis of the United Nations”. The agency would store and guard the material and allocate it to agriculture, medicine and above all electricity for “the power-starved areas of the world”. Its “great virtue” is that it needs no “world-wide inspection and control”. He closes by pledging that man's inventiveness “shall not be dedicated to his death, but consecrated to his life”.

Opening: a speech for the whole Assembly

p. 1 · untitled in the source · Bermuda · the UN as hope · “the language of atomic warfare”

Hammarskjold's invitation “reached me in Bermuda”, where Eisenhower was “just beginning a series of conferences with the prime Ministers and Foreign Ministers of the United Kingdom and France” on “some of the problems that beset our world” p. 1. He feels “a sense of exhilaration”: “Never before in history has so much hope for so many people been gathered together in a single organization.” The Assembly's “deliberations and decisions during these sombre years have already realized part of those hopes”, but “the great tests and the great accomplishments still lie ahead”. The US Government “will remain steadfast in its support of this body”, trusting the UN to supply “the wisdom, … the courage and … the faith which can bring to this world lasting peace for all nations”.

It would not be fitting to give “a unilateral American report on Bermuda”, though the island's talks invoked “universal peace and human dignity”, the concepts “so clearly etched in your Charter”. Nor would he “recite … pious platitudes”. Instead he will say what has been on the minds of his “legislative and executive associates” for months: “thoughts I had originally planned to say primarily to the American people” p. 1.

His premise: “if a danger exists in the world, it is a danger shared by all; and equally, that if hope exists in the mind of one nation, that hope should be shared by all.” What better audience for a proposal to ease tension than the General Assembly?

Eisenhower's framing

“I feel impelled to speak today in a language that in a sense is new, one which I, who have spent so much of my life in the military profession, would have preferred never to use. That new language is the language of atomic warfare.” p. 1

Every citizen should have “some comprehension, at least in comparative terms”, of atomic development: “if the peoples of the world are to conduct an intelligent search for peace, they must be armed with the significant facts of today's existence.” His facts are “necessarily stated in United States terms, for these are the only incontrovertible facts that I know”, though the subject is “global, not merely national in character” p. 1.

The language of atomic warfare

pp. 1–2 · tests, yields, stockpile · “not ours alone” · two facts · no absolute safety

The numbers

  • “On 16 July 1945, the United States set off the world's biggest atomic explosion.” Since then the US “has conducted forty-two test explosions” p. 1.
  • Atomic bombs are “more than twenty-five times as powerful as the weapons with which the atomic age dawned”; hydrogen weapons “are in the ranges of millions of tons of TNT equivalent” pp. 1–2.
  • The US stockpile, which “increases daily, exceeds by many times the total equivalent of the total of all bombs and all shells that came from every plane and every gun in every theatre of war in all the years of the Second World War” p. 2.
  • “A single air group … can now deliver to any reachable target a destructive cargo exceeding in power all the bombs that fell on Britain in all the Second World War.”
  • Atomic weapons “have virtually achieved conventional status within our armed services”: the Army, Navy, Air Force and Marine Corps can all use them.

Not ours alone

“But the dread secret and the fearful engines of atomic might are not ours alone.” The secret is held by “our friends and allies, the United Kingdom and Canada, whose scientific genius made a tremendous contribution to our original discoveries and the designs of atomic bombs”. It is also known by the Soviet Union, which has “exploded a series of atomic devices, including at least one involving thermo-nuclear reactions” p. 2.

“If at one time the United States possessed what might have been called a monopoly of atomic power, that monopoly ceased to exist several years ago.” The American head start gives “a great quantitative advantage”, but two facts matter more:

  1. “the knowledge now possessed by several nations will eventually be shared by others, possibly all others.”
  2. “even a vast superiority in numbers of weapons, and a consequent capability of devastating retaliation, is no preventive, of itself, against the fearful material damage and toll of human lives that would be inflicted by surprise aggression.”

No absolute safety

The free world, “at least dimly aware of these facts”, has “embarked on a large programme of warning and defence systems”, which will be “accelerated and extended”. But no spending can “guarantee absolute safety for the cities and citizens of any nation”. “The awful arithmetic of the atomic bomb doesn't permit of any such easy solution.” An aggressor with “the effective minimum number of atomic bombs” could, even against the strongest defence, “cause hideous damage” p. 2.

Roehrlich's reading

He described the threat “in blunt terms” but “carefully avoided referring to Hiroshima and Nagasaki by name” Roehrlich p. 31.

Beyond retaliation

p. 2 · “swift and resolute” · two atomic colossi · the book of history · out of the chamber of horrors

If the US were attacked, “our reactions would be swift and resolute”. But to say that US retaliation could lay an aggressor's land waste, “all this, while fact, is not the true expression of the purpose and the hopes of the United States” p. 2.

Eisenhower's refusal

“To pause there would be to confirm the hopeless finality of a belief that two atomic colossi are doomed malevolently to eye each other indefinitely across a trembling world.” To stop there would accept “the probability of civilization destroyed”, “the annihilation of the irreplaceable heritage of mankind”, and mankind condemned to begin again “the age-old struggle upward from savagery towards decency, and right, and justice” p. 2.

“Surely no sane member of the human race could discover victory in such desolation.” History records a few “great destroyers”, but “the whole book of history reveals mankind's never-ending quest for peace and mankind's God-given capacity to build.” The US wishes to be identified “with the book of history, and not with isolated pages”.

“My country wants to be constructive, not destructive. It wants agreements, not wars, among nations.” It wants to live in freedom and for “the peoples of every other nation” to enjoy “the right of choosing their own way of life”. Its purpose is “to help us to move out of the dark chamber of horrors into the light”, so that “the minds of men, the hopes of men, the souls of men everywhere, can move forward towards peace and happiness and well-being” p. 2.

Steps to peace and a new avenue

p. 3 · patience · the conference table · the Soviet note · decolonisation as “deeds of peace” · resolution 715 (VIII)

“In a world divided, such as ours today, salvation cannot be attained by one dramatic act.” Many steps over many months are needed before “a new climate of mutually peaceful confidence is abroad in the world”. “But I know, above all else, that we must start to take these steps - now.” p. 3

The record at the conference table

“Let no one say that we shun the conference table.” The US, UK and France have long asked to negotiate with the Soviet Union on a divided Germany and an Austrian peace treaty; the UN still asks to negotiate on Korea. The Soviet Union has now sent “what is in effect an expression of willingness to hold a four-Power meeting”, and its note “did not contain the unacceptable pre-conditions previously put forward”. As the Bermuda communiqué said, the three Western powers “have agreed promptly to meet with the Soviet Union”. The US approaches it “with hopeful sincerity” and will “bend every effort” toward “tangible results towards peace, the only true way of lessening international tension” p. 3.

The US “will never say that the peoples of the USSR are an enemy”, and will never ask the Soviet Union to “surrender what rightly belongs to it”. It hopes for “a freer mingling of the peoples of the East and of the West”. Instead of the discontent “settling upon Eastern Germany, occupied Austria and the countries of Eastern Europe”, it seeks “a harmonious family of free European nations”. Beyond “the turmoil and strife and misery” of Asia (“Asis” in the transcript) it seeks “peaceful opportunity for these peoples to develop their natural resources and to elevate their lot”.

These are “not idle words”. Behind them lies “a story of nations lately come to independence, not as a result of war, but through free grant or peaceful negotiation”, and Western aid to people hit by “famine, drought and natural disaster”. “These are deeds of peace. They speak more loudly than promises or protestations of peaceful intent.” p. 3

“But I do not wish to rest either upon the reiteration of past proposals or the restatement of past deeds. The gravity of the time is such that every new avenue of peace, no matter how dimly discernible, should be explored.” p. 3

The new avenue: resolution 715 (VIII)

“There is at least one new avenue of peace which has not been well explored.” The General Assembly's resolution 715 (VIII) of 28 November 1953 suggested “that the Disarmament Commission study the desirability of establishing a sub-committee consisting of representatives of the Powers principally involved, which should seek in private an acceptable solution” and report by 1 September 1954 p. 3.

The US is “instantly prepared to meet privately” with the countries “principally involved” to find “an acceptable solution” to “the atomic armaments race which overshadows not only the peace, but the very life, of the world”.

The “new conception”

“The United States would seek more than the mere reduction or elimination of atomic materials for military purposes. It is not enough to take this weapon out of the hands of the soldiers. It must be put into the hands of those who will know how to strip its military casing and adapt it to the arts of peace.” p. 3

If the military build-up can be reversed, “this greatest of destructive forces can be developed into a great boon, for the benefit of all mankind.” “The United States knows that peaceful power from atomic energy is no dream of the future. The capability, already proved, is here today.”

The proposal and the pledge

pp. 3–4 · contributions to an agency · the bank · power-starved areas · the four aims · the close

“Who can doubt that, if the entire body of the world's scientists and engineers had adequate amounts of fissionable material …, this capability would rapidly be transformed into universal, efficient and economic usage?” To hasten the day “when fear of the atom will begin to disappear”, steps can be taken now p. 4.

The proposal, verbatim

“The governments principally involved, to the extent permitted by elementary prudence, should begin now and continue to make joint contributions from their stockpiles of normal uranium and fissionable materials to an international atomic energy agency. We would expect that such an agency would be set up under the aegis of the United Nations. The ratios of contributions, the procedures and other details would properly be within the scope of the ‘private conversations’ I referred to earlier.” p. 4

The US will explore this “in good faith”; any partner in the same good faith “will find the United States a not unreasonable or ungenerous associate”. Contributions would start “small in quantity”. But the plan “has the great virtue that it can be undertaken without the irritations and mutual suspicions incident to any attempt to set up a completely acceptable system of world-wide inspection and control.”

What the agency would do

  • Store and guard. It “could be made responsible for the impounding, storage and protection of the contributed fissionable and other materials”. Scientists will provide “special safe conditions under which such a bank of fissionable material can be made essentially immune to surprise seizure” p. 4.
  • Allocate, its “more important responsibility”. It would “devise methods whereby this fissionable material would be allocated to serve the peaceful pursuits of mankind”. Experts would apply atomic energy “to the needs of agriculture, medicine and other peaceful activities”. “A special purpose would be to provide abundant electrical energy in the power-starved areas of the world.”

“Thus the contributing Powers would be dedicating some of their strength to serve the needs rather than the fears of mankind.” The US would be “proud” to plan this with others “principally involved”, and “Of those ‘principally involved’ the Soviet Union must, of course, be one.” p. 4

The four aims he would put to Congress

He would submit to the US Congress, “with every expectation of approval”, any plan that would:

  1. “encourage world-wide investigation into the most effective peacetime uses of fissionable material”, with investigators having “all the material needed”;
  2. “begin to diminish the potential destructive power of the world's atomic stockpiles”;
  3. “allow all peoples of all nations to see that … the great Powers of the earth, both of the East and of the West, are interested in human aspirations first rather than in building up the armaments of war”;
  4. “open up a new channel for peaceful discussion and initiative”, so the world can “shake off the inertia imposed by fear” p. 4.

The close

“Against the dark background of the atomic bomb, the United States does not wish merely to present strength, but also the desire and the hope for peace.” May the coming “fateful decisions” in the Assembly, in capitals and headquarters and “in the hearts of men everywhere” lead the world “out of fear and into peace”.

The pledge

The United States “pledges before you, and therefore before the world, its determination to help solve the fearful atomic dilemma - to devote its entire heart and mind to finding the way by which the miraculous inventiveness of man shall not be dedicated to his death, but consecrated to his life.” p. 4

Reading 2 · Book chapter · Inspectors for Peace (Johns Hopkins, 2022), ch. 2 · pp. 31–60

Atoms for Peace

Elisabeth Roehrlich on how Eisenhower's speech became the IAEA Statute, 1953–1957

The chapter in one breath

Roehrlich follows Eisenhower's idea from its drafting in 1953 to the IAEA Statute's entry into force in July 1957. The speech grew out of Operation Candor, a campaign to tell Americans about the nuclear threat. The “pool” of fissile material was added late and was the president's own idea. It left the agency's role blurry: pool, bank and channel for dialogue at once. Two shifts define the negotiations: the arms-control aspect “declined in importance”, and safeguards, “not mentioned in Eisenhower's speech—moved from the margins to the center” p. 33. Molotov's 1954 warning that civilian reactors make weapons material, and scientist-led talks in Geneva in 1955, convinced US officials that the Soviets shared an interest in stopping new nuclear states. The US drafted the Statute with seven Western and uranium-producing states, widened the group to twelve under UN pressure, and argued it out in the private “Washington talks” of 1956. There the real clash was India against South Africa and the uranium producers over the Board of Governors. The 81-nation Conference on the Statute heard developing states call safeguards “atomic colonialism”, but the Washington compromise survived. The word “proliferation” came up once, about paper. Her verdict: the Statute shows that Cold War rivals could cooperate and compete at the same time, and that the IAEA moved from Baruch-era control to promotion, “with control as an adjunct” p. 60.

Thesis
The chapter asks how the founders pictured the agency's promotion and control roles, why the Cold War rivals could cooperate here when the UNAEC talks had failed, and “what price was paid” p. 33. Its answers: arms control faded and safeguards rose; private, technical and businesslike talks made agreement possible; the price was a Board and safeguards that developing states saw as discriminatory, and a Statute with no ban on the weapon option.
Against
(1) Historians split on whether Atoms for Peace was “a mere act of US Cold War propaganda or an honest attempt” p. 33; she answers that the drafts combined several aims (Osgood, Chernus). (2) The historiography of the nuclear age, which has “largely forgotten” the Washington talks p. 54. (3) Today's picture of the IAEA as chiefly a nonproliferation body: in 1956 “proliferation” was never discussed p. 57.

The scene

The UN grounds had been closed for hours when Eisenhower entered the General Assembly Hall on the afternoon of 8 December 1953. The seats were packed, with some 3,500 people; thousands more waited outside. It was his first appearance before the UN. Vijaya Lakshmi Pandit, in a bright sari, walked him to the podium: she was Nehru's sister, Indira Gandhi's aunt, and “the first woman to preside over a UN General Assembly session” p. 31. In “a firm and sincere voice” he gave “a 25-minute tour d'horizon of the nuclear revolution”.

He took his audience back to 16 July 1945 in New Mexico and the 42 US tests since; hydrogen bombs were more than 25 times as powerful. “He carefully avoided referring to Hiroshima and Nagasaki by name.” He claimed the US still led but admitted the monopoly was gone: “The dread secret and the fearful engines of atomic might are not ours alone.” Britain and, “most importantly, the Soviet Union” had tested their own weapons pp. 31–32.

Rejecting the “two atomic colossi” fatalism, he turned to the UN's First Committee (the Disarmament and International Security Committee), where the rivals discussed arms control. Talk was not enough: “It is not enough to take this weapon out of the hands of soldiers.” Almost twenty minutes in, he proposed an international atomic energy agency “under the aegis of the United Nations”. The task seemed simple: nuclear-armed and advanced states would donate fissionable material, which the agency would make available for medicine, agriculture, science and power. More civilian use would stimulate research and diminish “the potential destructive power of the world's atomic stockpiles” p. 32.

He proposed working out the details privately among the states “principally involved”, the Soviet Union included. He “characterized the new technology's civilian and military uses as separable, but glossed over how this might be achieved” p. 32.

The applause lasted ten minutes, and even the Soviet delegation joined in. Some called it one of the most impressive speeches ever given at the UN. What excited listeners was the prospect of nuclear power “independent of a country's natural resources and applicable around the world”. Sceptics noted that Eisenhower “had not called for a ban of the atomic bomb” p. 32.

Historians divided

Historians “continue to be divided on whether Eisenhower's Atoms for Peace initiative represented a mere act of US Cold War propaganda or an honest attempt to reduce the nuclear threat; a tool to prevent the uncontrolled and global spread of nuclear weapons or an initiative that eventually furthered the distribution of nuclear weapons; or all of the above” p. 33. Some doubt his sincerity on arms control, pointing to the New Look (his national security policy, built on nuclear weapons) and the build-up under his presidency. Atoms for Peace “shifted the public's attention from military uses of the atom to civilian purposes”.

  • Kenneth Osgood describes the presidency as juxtaposing “talking peace and waging Cold War”.
  • Ira Chernus, in a historical linguistic analysis of the speech: “In fact, the president suggested some half dozen goals.”
Roehrlich's argument

The chapter runs from the first drafts in 1953 to the final Statute negotiations in 1956. It asks how the founders pictured the agency's promotion and control roles, why cooperation between the rivals proved possible, “other than at the failed UNAEC negotiations”, and “what price was paid as a condition for this cooperation”. “The prominent arms control aspect of Atoms for Peace declined in importance during the international negotiations to establish the IAEA. On the other hand, nuclear safeguards—designed to detect diversions of nuclear material from civilian to military uses and not mentioned in Eisenhower's speech—moved from the margins to the center of the debate on the new agency.” p. 33

The Genesis of Eisenhower's Plan

pp. 33–38 · secrecy · “too cheap to meter” · Operation Candor · the X-kilogram memo · Operation Wheaties · Bermuda · the name

Claim
The speech began as a campaign of public candour about the threat and gained its hopeful “pool” proposal late, from Eisenhower himself. It was drafted in a tiny circle, and the agency's role stayed undefined.
Evidence
The Oppenheimer Panel, Jackson's drafts, the 10 September 1953 memo, Strauss's objections, Bermuda, the Cole and Bohlen episodes.

A president briefed in secret

Eisenhower entered politics a war hero, the Allied supreme commander in Europe; both parties tried to recruit him (“I like Ike”). In January 1953 he succeeded Truman as the thirty-fourth president, a Republican. Like Truman, he had only “a limited understanding” of the nuclear programme because of its secrecy. Two months before his inauguration, senior Atomic Energy Commission (AEC) officials briefed him p. 33.

The briefings covered the atom's two sides: destruction, above all the hydrogen bomb, and benefits in energy and science. The Manhattan Project had been dissolved in 1946, but secrecy remained. The espionage trial and execution of Julius and Ethel Rosenberg made headlines. “The atmosphere in regard to nuclear technology was not one of scientific exchange and openness, but of secrecy and denial.” The Atomic Energy Act of 1946 (the McMahon Act) put weapons and civilian technology under the new AEC pp. 34–35.

Public and industry interest was rising. The atom promised energy “too cheap to meter”, would “revolutionize medicine and turn deserts into green fields”. But the Act tied private industry's hands, and it would not invest. Eisenhower sympathised with business and, with most Republicans, rejected an “atomic TVA” on the model of Roosevelt's state-run Tennessee Valley Authority. Yet he was “deeply worried about the threat of nuclear warfare and appalled” by what he heard of a recent hydrogen bomb test in the Pacific, and he accepted the need for secrecy p. 35.

From Operation Candor to a positive twist

The drafts reflect his conflicting thinking. The ideas came from the Truman administration's committee on nuclear war, the Oppenheimer Panel, named for J. Robert Oppenheimer, who had also advised the Acheson-Lilienthal committee in 1946. A Soviet atomic “knockout blow” was a real fear. The panel recommended telling the public the full dimension of the threat and the defence plans. That became a public information campaign, with a major speech, overseen by C. D. Jackson, Eisenhower's public relations adviser and speechwriter: Operation Candor p. 35.

Stalin's death in March 1953 promised relaxation, but on 12 August 1953 the Soviets announced their first thermonuclear test. Secrecy had not stopped them, and faith in Candor faded. Jackson found the drafts demoralising: they would leave the audience thinking “bang bang, no hope, no way out in the end.” The positive twist was a new international organisation focused on civilian uses p. 36.

The X-kilogram memo

The pool was debated late, in September and October 1953; his team later recalled it as the president's own idea. On 10 September 1953 he wrote to Lewis Strauss, AEC chairman, and Jackson:

Suppose the United States and the Soviets were each to turn over to the United Nations, for peaceful use, X kilograms of fissionable material. The amount X could be fixed at a figure which we could handle from our stockpile, but which would be difficult for the Soviets to match.

Eisenhower, memo of 10 September 1953 · p. 36
Roehrlich's reading

“The idea was technically an arms control proposal, as it would reduce the stockpiles of the nuclear weapon states …, but at the same time would not undermine US nuclear hegemony.” Like the Baruch Plan, it gave the UN a role, and the UN “was as much a battlefield of Cold War rivalries as a place to overcome conflict” p. 36.

Strauss “was not a big fan”. He agreed it made propaganda sense but stressed how little was known about the Soviet programme, and that the hydrogen bomb had reduced “the relative importance of a stockpile of fissionable material”. He and Jackson revised over breakfast at the Metropolitan Club, calling it Operation Wheaties after the cereal. In late October Eisenhower was invited to address the General Assembly and saw the perfect setting, so the draft, written for Americans, was rewritten for the world p. 36.

Bermuda, 4–7 December 1953

Days before the speech, Eisenhower met British prime minister Winston Churchill and French leader Joseph Laniel (“French president” in the text Laniel was prime minister: not in Roehrlich) in Bermuda. The island had hosted an inconclusive Anglo-American meeting on Jewish refugees from Nazi Germany in April 1943. This time they covered nearly everything: the Soviet Union and communist China, Europe and NATO, the Middle and Far East p. 37.

Delegations were small, about ten each. Eisenhower discussed the speech only with the three leaders and foreign ministers, and informally with Churchill, Churchill's science adviser and “personal nuclear guru” Lord Cherwell, and Strauss. Churchill remarked that it would be difficult to draw a line between civilian and military uses, “a separation that was vital to Eisenhower's plan”. Still, Churchill and the French supported it. Eisenhower called it a message to the West and a Cold War initiative, which would give “these nations a stronger feeling of participation in the struggle of East and West” p. 37.

Cole, Bohlen, and the name

Only Strauss knew inside the AEC, “to the annoyance of the other commissioners”. Eisenhower did not even tell William Sterling Cole, Republican chairman of the Joint Committee on Atomic Energy (JCAE), who would become the IAEA's first director general four years later. In early December Cole, after talking with Edward Teller, “the inventor of the hydrogen bomb”, planned a speech to revive parts of the Baruch Plan. On a short car ride Strauss, unable to reveal the speech, asked him to wait. “Four days later, Eisenhower's speech at the UN made Cole's initiative obsolete.” pp. 37–38

On 7 December, the US ambassador to Moscow, Charles E. Bohlen, called on Foreign Minister Vyacheslav Molotov without telling the press. Molotov, with the failed Baruch Plan in mind, asked whether this was a new control proposal. Bohlen could say no more but promised to keep the Soviets informed p. 38.

On the plane back to New York's LaGuardia airport the team made final changes. Only then did the agency get its name; the previous draft said “International Atomic Energy Authority”, which echoed the Baruch Plan's International Atomic Development Authority of 1946. The final text was run off on a mimeograph machine on board p. 38.

Three overlapping objectives

  1. Pool. Receive and store fissionable material from military stockpiles, a neutralised pool promising to slow the arms race. “This was the arms control aspect.”
  2. Bank or broker. Make the material available for civilian use in member states: the promotional role.
  3. Channel. “Open a new channel for peaceful discussion” in a divided world: “the moral, psychological dimension” p. 38.

“Despite the ambitious goals suggested, nobody had put much thought into how this would look in practice.” Everything was left to the negotiations pp. 38–39.

Soviets and Americans at the Negotiating Table

pp. 39–42 · three Soviet objections · Molotov's dual-use warning · vertical vs horizontal · Merchant's reply · safeguards

Claim
Molotov saw the flaw in Atoms for Peace, the dual use of civilian reactors, before Washington did. The US answer, “safeguards”, put a concept the speech had avoided at the centre of the IAEA debate.
Evidence
The 1954 note exchange; Molotov's Geneva aide-mémoire; Smith, Dulles, a State Department analyst, Merchant.

From January 1954 the two governments quietly exchanged notes. The Americans kept both options open, Soviet participation or not, and officials disagreed about it. The Soviets were unenthusiastic but willing to talk. Eisenhower stayed in the background, typical of the “hidden-hand president” p. 39.

Three Soviet objections

  1. Not real disarmament. The Soviets had publicly called for a prohibition of nuclear weapons, “a key propaganda point”.
  2. China. They wanted the People's Republic of China in the IAEA.

Both were unacceptable. US policy rested on nuclear deterrence, and the New Look stressed nuclear weapons over conventional forces “not least to reduce military spending”, so a ban was out of the question. The US recognised the Republic of China, not the PRC p. 39.

  1. Dual use. This one “transcended mere Cold War propaganda”. At the Geneva conference on Indochina (April 1954), which also discussed divided Korea, Molotov gave Secretary of State John Foster Dulles a memo:

[T]hat the peaceful application of atomic energy is connected with the simultaneous production of atomic materials utilized for the production of atomic weapons is indisputable and has been proved in practice. … [It] not only fails to lead to a reduction of the stocks of atomic materials utilized for the manufacture of atomic weapons, but also leads to an increase of these stocks without any limitations being applied either to the constantly increasing production of these materials in individual states or to the production by the International Agency itself.

Molotov to Dulles, 1954 · pp. 39–40

This is the dual-use problem: power reactors also produce fissile material, notably plutonium. The Soviets were designing such a reactor p. 40.

Washington caught off guard

Gerard Smith, Dulles's special assistant for atomic energy affairs, put the Soviet message bluntly: they were “asking what in the world Americans thought they were doing proposing to spread weapon-grade nuclear materials around the world.” Dulles admitted he had been “completely puzzled by Molotov's reference”. Smith had to tell him that Molotov had been better briefed by Soviet scientists than Dulles had been by American experts p. 40.

Roehrlich's diagnosis

Several members of the administration “had turned a blind eye to the security risks of Atoms for Peace, focusing instead on the public relations impact”. The neglect came from how the speech was drafted: the early focus on Operation Candor and a closed circle. And whether weapons would spread “could only be answered theoretically” p. 40.

Two proliferations

The sides were talking past each other. Molotov meant growth of US and Soviet stockpiles through dual-use technology: vertical proliferation. The Americans thought he meant new nuclear-weapon states emerging from the spread of technology: horizontal proliferation. A State Department analyst wrote that “The US and the USSR have been worried about two different aspects of a related question”; the US had answered with “‘diversion’ rather than the overall increase problem”, and must “be prepared to consider both aspects” pp. 40–41.

The flaws were “not simply due to ignorance”. In 1953 uranium was thought scarce, so a neutral pool would have cut the material available for weapons. Only when new deposits were found worldwide in the mid-1950s did it become clear there could be no “zero-sum game between uranium for peaceful purposes and for weapons” p. 41.

The US replies

Dulles told Molotov in an “informal paper” that Atoms for Peace “was not intended as a measure for the control of atomic weapons” but as civilian cooperation. Roehrlich: that was not true of the speech, which “had certainly focused on an arms control proposal—the ‘pool’”, though the pool had been controversial and poorly thought out. Arms control now moved to the background p. 41.

On 9 July 1954 Assistant Secretary of State Livingston Merchant replied officially: the Soviet Union “appears to assume that any form of peaceful utilization … must necessarily increase stocks …. In reality, however, ways can be devised to safeguard against diversion of materials from power producing reactors.” The ways were unspecified, but nuclear safeguards, “tools and activities to verify that peaceful nuclear assistance is not being used for military purposes”, became central p. 41.

Safeguards had featured in US debate since the 1945 Three-Nation Declaration on international control. But Eisenhower had presented the absence of inspection as the plan's “great virtue”, answering critics who expected it to fail like the Baruch Plan, which had relied on inspection and control pp. 41–42.

The exchange paused until late September 1954, not deadlocked like the UNAEC talks but unsure how to proceed. In October 1954 the secret letters were published in both countries because of worldwide interest: “This act of transparency was one thing both rivals agreed on” p. 42.

Bringing the Allies on Board

pp. 42–44 · Britain's “bridge” · the eight-nation group · uranium and empire · a “global West” draft

Claim
The Statute's foundations were laid by a Western, largely colonial and uranium-producing group, with the US and Britain drafting.
Evidence
British Foreign Office working party; State Department correspondence; the early draft's “general manager”.

The US also consulted Britain and Canada, its Manhattan Project partners. London was worried by the superpower quarrel. For the Foreign Office the IAEA's key contribution was easing Cold War tension: it should become a “bridge between East and West”. A working party thought that without the Soviets it would be better to have “the plan dropped altogether” p. 42.

Economics explained British coolness: London preferred nuclear cooperation within the Commonwealth to a new body likely dominated by the US. But it would support the IAEA if the Soviets joined, “as a price worth paying to secure the relaxation of international tension” p. 42.

The eight-nation group

In spring and summer 1954 the State Department brought in Australia, Belgium, France, Portugal and South Africa. Eisenhower's “principally involved” referred to a First Committee resolution envisaging a Subcommittee of the Disarmament Commission of the US, USSR, UK, Canada and France, so France had to be added; excluding it would also “offend the country's sense of national prestige” p. 43.

The other four were there for their raw materials:

  • Belgian Congo: “the Western world's biggest supplier of uranium”;
  • South Africa: close behind;
  • Australia: extracting radioactive sources since the early twentieth century;
  • Portugal: a minor producer, supplying uranium oxide to Britain and the US.

“Five of the eight states were colonial powers.” p. 43

The US and UK wrote the first draft. In an early version the top official was a “general manager”, the title of the AEC's head of divisions, showing US influence. Without the Soviets the pool “did not play a major role”: siphoning off US military stockpiles “did not make much sense” p. 43.

Roehrlich's verdict

The talks were ad hoc meetings at the State Department, yet the IAEA's aims, policy bodies and membership rules were outlined in 1954. “The foundations of the IAEA's statute were laid by a group of states that could be described as the global West”: two Western nuclear-weapon states (US, UK), four more NATO members (Belgium, Canada, France, Portugal) and two uranium-producing Commonwealth states (Australia, South Africa) pp. 43–44.

The State Department shared the Soviet notes with the seven on an “eyes only, top secret basis” p. 44.

The Role of the United Nations

pp. 44–46 · why not the First Committee · a UN that turns anticolonial · the IAEA as development agency · bilateral Atoms for Peace

Claim
The US kept the negotiations out of the UN, but the General Assembly, enlarged by new states, pushed for Soviet participation and recast the IAEA as a development agency.
Evidence
First Committee debates 1954–55; Mazower; Jackson to Strauss; Walser; the 1954 Atomic Energy Act.

Why not the UN?

Eisenhower had promised an agency “under the aegis” of the UN, so why negotiate by letters and meetings? Three reasons p. 44:

  1. The US wanted to lead the project it had started.
  2. The speech had said the details would come in “private or diplomatic talks”.
  3. Atoms for Peace assumed civilian and military uses were “distinct and separable”, with promotion as the agency's main purpose. The administration “increasingly tried to distance it from disarmament”, so it was not only a First Committee matter.

Still, the General Assembly, where the speech had been most warmly received, “ultimately played an influential role”. Delegations debated the IAEA at the 1954 and 1955 sessions, and these debates “would be decisive in engendering Soviet support” p. 44.

New states, new UN

In 1955, 16 new members joined, including Cambodia, Ceylon, Laos and Libya; in 1956 Sudan, Morocco and Tunisia. Mark Mazower argues the UN then shifted from an “instrument of empire into an anticolonial forum” p. 44.

In the First Committee, states as different as Egypt, France, India, Pakistan and the UK wanted the Soviets included. The Soviets signalled they might compromise, even on prohibiting atomic weapons. Egypt, Pakistan and Peru stressed the needs of the “economically less advanced nations”. Recalling the speech's “power-starved areas”, several states in autumn 1954 began “framing the IAEA as a development agency” p. 45.

For new states, nuclear technology “symbolized modernity and offered national prestige”. Nuclear aid became a field of Cold War competition. Jackson told Strauss that, given Soviet economic offensives, “Atoms for Peace, wisely placed in various parts of the world” could recapture “for the US a lot of the recent international slippage.” Gladys Walser, Hammarskjöld's observer at the IAEA talks, wrote that both superpowers presented themselves as the developing countries' “true friend and champion in bringing them the benefits of the atomic revolution” p. 45.

Bilateral programmes

From 1955 both superpowers ran bilateral nuclear aid. “Somewhat confusingly”, the US programme used the same motto, “Atoms for Peace”. The first bilateral agreement was with Turkey, already a big Marshall Plan recipient for its geostrategic importance p. 45.

  • US. Congress amended the McMahon Act. The Atomic Energy Act of 1954, signed 30 August, allowed peaceful cooperation as long as material was subject to “strict nuclear safeguards”.
  • USSR. Treaties with Eastern European states in spring 1955, with no safeguards. Instead recipients gave “strong guarantees” not to divert, and had to return used fuel to the Soviet Union to prevent plutonium reprocessing pp. 45–46.

Nuclear Safeguards against Diversion

pp. 46–49 · Geneva 1955 · the “meetings of six governments” · Bunche kept out · Rabi and Skobeltzyn · a “community of interest”

Claim
Scientist-led technical talks failed to solve the “diversion problem” but convinced US officials that Moscow shared their interest in preventing new nuclear states. That belief kept the IAEA negotiations going.
Evidence
UNSAC; the 1955 Geneva conference; Bunche's note; Rabi's statements; Smith's September 1955 report.

Geneva, August 1955

In 1954 the General Assembly created the UN Scientific Advisory Committee (UNSAC) to advise Hammarskjöld on a conference on peaceful uses, held in Geneva on 8–20 August 1955. With almost 2,000 scientists it was the largest scientific conference to date. Eisenhower introduced a swimming-pool research reactor from Oak Ridge for the exhibition and even worked its control panel; Enrico Fermi's wife called it the world's “most beautiful little reactor” p. 46.

Afterwards a small group of top US and Soviet delegates stayed on to discuss safeguards. The Soviets had resumed the note exchange, thanks to worldwide enthusiasm, revived East-West scientific exchange, and General Assembly pressure pp. 46–47.

“Outside the UN and not UN business”

The question: how “nuclear material for use in reactors can be supplied to members of an International Atomic Energy Agency without increasing the risk to the security of the world”. The meetings, officially “meetings of six governments” (US, USSR, Canada, Czechoslovakia, France, UK), were held in the Palais des Nations but led by scientists, not diplomats. The press called them “ultra-secret”; even some State Department staff knew little p. 47.

The UN itself was kept out. Ralph Bunche, Hammarskjöld's undersecretary for special political affairs, found out by chance at a reception at the Yugoslav embassy. He asked the Soviet scientist Dimitri Skobeltzyn whether he could rest after the exhausting conference. No, said Skobeltzyn: safeguards talks started on Monday, in a UN facility but “outside the UN and not UN business.” Bunche wrote Hammarskjöld a handwritten note about his irritation p. 47.

A technical problem, technically

Isidor Rabi, the lead US delegate, said the meetings were “to talk about a technical problem in a technical manner”. The Statute was “a matter for diplomats and lawyers”; scientists would judge whether inspection and control were feasible. Disarmament and Soviet conditions were off the agenda pp. 47–48.

They reached no technical solution. The US offered a “half-baked proposal, drafted on short notice in a hotel room”, to track fuel with radioactive tracers such as high-energy gamma emitters. The Americans said the Soviets came unprepared, but they themselves could not tell which controls made sense. The meetings “made clear that no one really knew how these safeguards might work in practice.” Smith later wrote that exchanges between American and Soviet scientists had a better chance “than if it were left to politicians, bureaucrats, and military officers” p. 48.

Washington wakes up

“The two Cold War rivals had more in common than either had expected.” The State Department's Atomic Energy Affairs section gave “unprecedented attention” to the dangers of nuclear aid. Rabi, a Manhattan Project scientist, Nobel laureate and chair of the AEC's General Advisory Committee, who also sat on UNSAC with Skobeltzyn, drove the discussion. Its questions: which kinds of aid were vulnerable to diversion, how to prevent it, how serious proliferation was, and what role the IAEA could play p. 48.

Smith and his colleagues realised Atoms for Peace had been released “without giving proper consideration” to the risks. His September 1955 report warned:

[T]he Atoms for Peace program is approaching the stage where dual-use power reactors will be constructed around the world. In these reactors, weapon-grade material will be formed in large quantities which will pose a worldwide security problem that has not been squarely faced.

Gerard Smith, September 1955 · p. 49
  • Rabi: before US aid went ahead, a control system was “imperative”, run by the US or the future IAEA.
  • Smith: “the Atoms for Peace program will result in a number of countries having competence in atomic energy technology at an earlier date than if such program did not exist.”
  • Smith: “the important question of control and disposition of plutonium formed in power reactors abroad has not been considered by the US” p. 49.
Roehrlich's verdict

The most important result was that State Department officials came to believe the Soviet Union was as worried as the US about other states getting the bomb. Smith: “This is one of the rare instances where the US and the USSR may have a clear community of interest.” Their belief in a “community of interest”, though unproven, “led them to proceed with the negotiations to create an international nuclear watchdog” p. 49.

The Washington Talks

pp. 49–55 · the twelve · Bhabha and Fischer · 40,000 kg · pool, bank or broker · India vs South Africa · 23 seats · two budgets

Claim
The private, businesslike Washington talks were an early and largely forgotten case of superpower cooperation. The fiercest conflict was North-South, not East-West.
Evidence
Meeting records, a South African diplomat's letter, the Christian Science Monitor, Goodby's recollection.
Against
A historiography of the nuclear age that has “largely forgotten” these talks.

From eight to twelve

In October 1955 the General Assembly backed the negotiators but asked for an international conference on the Statute in autumn 1956, open to all UN members and specialised agencies. It asked for Brazil, Czechoslovakia, India and the Soviet Union to be added. Brazil and India, both on UNSAC, were expected to speak for developing countries; Czechoslovakia supplied uranium to the USSR pp. 49–50.

Not everyone agreed. Pakistan and the Philippines, both SEATO members, said India and Brazil did not fully represent the developing world, and proposed adding two or three states with no nuclear programme or uranium, to get insight from “under-developed economies”. For Pakistan it was also a way to counter India. It dropped the demand under British and Commonwealth pressure p. 50.

The talks

The twelve met from February 1956, officially as the “Working Level Meeting on the Draft Statute of the International Atomic Energy Agency”, known as the “Washington talks”. They lasted eight weeks. Washington had all the ambassadors, and hosting “underlined its claim to leadership”; the USSR and Czechoslovakia had asked for New York in vain. They met in Foggy Bottom, at the State Department's International Office on Pennsylvania Avenue, with US-hired staff. 78 delegates and 15 staff registered p. 50.

Among the negotiators: Maurice Couve de Murville, the elegant French ambassador and later foreign minister; Homi Bhabha, India's most politically influential nuclear physicist, president of the Geneva conference six months earlier, Cambridge-educated and a “true gentleman” who always poured water for the assistant technical secretary beside him; and South Africa's David Fischer, the only negotiator who spent his whole career in the IAEA, becoming its official historian in 1997 pp. 50–51.

They had to revise the eight-nation draft to satisfy other UN members; delegates from developing countries said they would not “simply rubber stamp” it p. 51.

40,000 kilograms, and a blurry agency

On 22 February 1956 Eisenhower announced 40,000 kg of U-235 (in enriched uranium) for peaceful programmes, half domestic, half for the foreign Atoms for Peace programme. He did not say whether that meant bilateral deals or the IAEA. He “was smart enough not to commit himself in advance to an agency whose inspection and control functions remained under negotiation”. It was mainly a signal to India, which wanted aid through the agency but “fiercely opposed nuclear safeguards as too intrusive” p. 51.

At the first meeting, on 27 February 1956, Bhabha asked what the announcement meant for the IAEA. James Wadsworth, the chief US negotiator, promised full support without answering pp. 51–52. The question “hit a nerve”: would the agency be a pool neutralising military material, a bank, a broker in industry deals, a clearinghouse, “or all of the above”? A South African diplomat wrote: “It is still not clear to me how far the agency is intended as a propaganda façade and how far it will exercise actual control over an appreciable amount of fissionable material.” p. 52

The speech's “surprise seizure” did not mean terrorists. Since the Acheson-Lilienthal Report and the UNAEC talks, experts had feared that a national government might seize an internationally controlled installation. In three and a half years of talks, storage and security were far less discussed than how much the US, USSR and UK would contribute p. 52.

Procedure

Seventeen plenary meetings went through the draft article by article, in English, French and Russian; an article needed seven votes. Side meetings ran among the Western states, between the superpowers, and among the uranium producers, “a faction throughout the negotiations”. On 2 March the Soviet ambassador Georgiy Zarubin met Wadsworth for two hours; Wadsworth sensed Zarubin had a free hand. Both sides found the talks businesslike pp. 52–53.

The Board of Governors crisis

By mid-March all was agreed except the composition of the Board of Governors, the most powerful policy organ, which would decide each state's power. Every negotiator wanted a permanent seat and a majority for its allies. The 10 March deadline passed; on 21 March they adjourned until 9 April and told the press all was fine. Then William R. Frye of the Christian Science Monitor published “Atoms-for-Peace Plan Torn by Rival Blocs”, “so close to the truth that it dumbfounded the delegates” p. 53.

The rival blocs were not the superpowers but “India on one hand and the uranium-producing states on the other.” The US diplomat James Goodby, then a junior staff member, recalled: “The interesting thing to me was not what the Soviet Union said—they were relatively cooperative—but the newcomers.” p. 53

At the centre were South Africa and India. The clash came from apartheid's “racist treatment of the Indian minority”, disputed at the UN for years. The IAEA gave South Africa, “probably the most unpopular member of the United Nations at that time”, a rare chance at a major international role. Pretoria tried to keep India “out of the inner circle”. The South African high commissioner to Canada, W. C. du Plessis: “every endeavor should be made to prevent her [India] from becoming a member of the agency before it has been finally established.” It resented India's bid to lead anticolonial movements; in Africa, uranium mining was tied to the colonial question because key deposits lay in colonies pp. 53–54.

The Soviets backed India, and the US gave in to India's demand for a bigger, more diverse Board. In April they agreed on 23 members, with “a rather specific formula” ensuring every region was represented alongside nuclear leaders p. 54.

Two budgets

From a Canadian proposal came a two-budget system. Mandatory contributions pay the regular budget: staff salaries, meetings, scientific publications. Voluntary contributions pay the operational budget: materials, facilities, projects, technical assistance, and later safeguards. The design somewhat reflects the IAEA's dual mandate p. 54.

Roehrlich's verdict

The Washington talks, “though largely forgotten by the historiography of the nuclear age, are an important and early example of cooperation between the United States and the Soviet Union on nuclear matters.” Why? The thaw after Stalin, but mostly the setting: private, no press, no notes released, which kept propaganda “largely at bay”. The US asserted leadership but sought common ground, and the Soviets reciprocated. In the last session, on Zarubin's suggestion, they voted on the Statute as a whole to show unanimity. At the start of the thermonuclear age, both superpowers wanted agreement pp. 54–55.

In September and October 1956, delegates from 81 countries met in the General Assembly Hall, where the speech had been given. Hammarskjöld welcomed them, but it was not an official UN event: the US invited states on behalf of the twelve, partly because it refused to invite the PRC. The USSR acquiesced while disagreeing, and the invitations explained the dispute. All UN members and members of the specialised agencies were invited; West Germany and Switzerland belonged only to the latter p. 55.

China

The Soviet bloc and many new states pushed to include communist China. They made their point but did not press a vote, and the debate came before the president was elected, sparing the chair. The Statute let non-UN states join later. Walser recalled a Western feeling that “the Western world would benefit from an opportunity to observe and perhaps supervise Beijing's atomic development.” Using the IAEA to gather information about national programmes, absent from Eisenhower's proposal, thus emerged as a possible task pp. 55–56.

Safeguards as imperialism

Safeguards were meant to verify that IAEA aid was not misused. Most new states saw them as “a new form of great power imperialism”, and the Soviet bloc agreed that the agency must not discriminate. India argued that supplier states would not be inspected p. 56. Bhabha:

If a large part of the world is subject to controls and the other free of them, we will stand on the brink of a dangerous era sharply dividing the world into atomic ‘haves’ and ‘have-nots’ dominated by the Agency. Such a division would in itself, by creating dangerous tensions, defeat the very purpose of the safeguards, that is, to build a secure and peaceful world.

Homi Bhabha, 1956 · p. 56

The Ukrainian delegate said safeguards would put developing countries in “a constant position of discrimination” and under the “yoke of atomic colonialism.” Underrepresented in Washington, the Soviet bloc now had the Eastern European states plus Ukraine and Belarus, UN members since its founding. The developing countries were “the real majority”, so East and West both bid for their favour pp. 56–57.

The Board and the General Conference

India's demands had shaped the Board formula “in large part, but not entirely”. Many developing states still saw it as “a power cartel of the industrialized nations and uranium-producing states” with a near-certain US majority, and wanted the General Conference of all members strengthened. The Indonesian delegate had said developing countries were “deliberately excluded from taking part, and from contributing their share in cementing the foundations of this world-wide agency.” The conference slightly strengthened the General Conference and weakened the Board “in some minor ways”, but the Washington compromise “by and large” survived p. 57.

What was not discussed

Roehrlich's point

Today the IAEA is seen as the UN body for stopping the spread of nuclear weapons, but “proliferation” and “nonproliferation” “were never part of the discussion.” Hundreds of pages of records contain the word “proliferation” once, in a warning about the “proliferation of paper” a big bureaucracy might produce. “Access to peaceful nuclear technologies, not the denial of nuclear weapons, emerged as the primary objective of the IAEA.” The Statute did not ask members to give up the weapon option p. 57.

On 26 October 1956 the Statute opened for signature. A Preparatory Commission (PrepCom) would prepare the first General Conference and Board. There was consensus, but no formal decision, that the first conference would be in Vienna p. 57.

Time was short: autumn is the UN's busiest season, and the hall was needed. Days after the conference, the Suez crisis escalated as France, Israel and Britain attacked Egypt after it nationalised the canal; on 1–10 November the General Assembly held its first emergency special session. Roehrlich asks: would the conference have succeeded had it run a few days longer into Suez? pp. 57–58

Claim
The Statute was negotiated remarkably fast for the Cold War. It opened a channel of cooperation, showed rivals could compete and cooperate at once, and moved the IAEA from control to promotion.
Evidence
Ratification record; McCarthy and Dulles; the offers of U-235; Bechhoefer, Wadsworth, Krige, McKnight.

Ratification

The Statute entered into force on 29 July 1957. It needed 18 ratifications, including at least 3 of 5 states: Canada, France, the USSR, the UK and the US. “Ironically, the Soviet Union—once a critic of Eisenhower's Atoms for Peace—was the first of the five major powers to sign the statute and the third country to ratify it.” The US, “the main architect”, was slow p. 58.

Senator Joseph McCarthy opposed it passionately. At the Wisconsin GOP Club in Milwaukee on 9 February 1957 he imagined the IAEA forcing the US to deliver fissionable material “to communist ports”: “What has been sold to the public in glowing terms as an ‘Atoms for Peace’ program is, potentially, an ‘Atoms for War’ program.” On 10 May 1957 Dulles urged the Senate to ratify: failure would have “disastrous effects on the prestige and influence of the United States in the world”; the IAEA was “a native American product” p. 58.

The Senate ratified on 18 June, “more than a year after the statute entered into force” [as printed: this clashes with the 29 July 1957 date given above], making the US only the twenty-second country to ratify p. 58.

One horse, one rabbit

Before entry into force the US offered up to 5,000 kg of U-235, the USSR 50 kg, the UK 20 kg. An American official called it a “one horse, one rabbit proposition”, after the joke about a merchant who mixes rabbit with cheaper horse meat “in what he euphemistically calls a fifty percent ratio”. The Americans expected the Soviets to raise their offer for propaganda reasons pp. 58–59.

The IAEA “would never become the nuclear material pool envisioned”. That followed from its legal basis: Article IX lets it establish or acquire “facilities for the receipt, storage, and issue of materials” but does not require it p. 59.

Lessons

Contemporaries thought three years a long time. “Viewed from today, however, it is striking that during a tense phase of the Cold War, an international agreement on nuclear matters could be reached within a few years.” A field “as sensitive and as secret as nuclear energy opened up a new channel for international cooperation.” In 1959 Bernhard G. Bechhoefer, special assistant to the US delegation, saw lessons in “the art of negotiating with the Soviet Union”. His boss Wadsworth compared the IAEA's founding to the Congress of Vienna p. 59.

For developing countries, many newly independent, the drafting was “a litmus test for their new status”. The IAEA's creation “was strongly shaped by North-South tensions a decade before the Group of 77 formed”. Their leaders, like those of industrial states, saw nuclear progress as “an important pillar of modern national identity”. Their high hopes made the superpowers “act both as competitors and partners”. The idea of nuclear energy as the engine of a “new industrial revolution” united negotiators East and West, North and South pp. 59–60.

She quotes John Krige: “In an age in which the power of the modern state increasingly rested on its scientific and technological capability, scientific and technological ‘leadership’ was an essential complement to political, economic, and military leadership.” Despite the technology race, cooperation was possible in some fields. The Statute, negotiated alongside but “largely unaffected by” the crises at Suez and on the Hungarian border, is “an important example for this kind of simultaneity of cooperation and conflict.” p. 60

Roehrlich's closing verdict

Optimism about civilian nuclear energy marked the drafting, “a considerable change from the much more restrictive Baruch Plan”. The IAEA's first inspector general, the Australian Allan McKnight, called it “a shift in emphasis from control, with the dissemination of nuclear science and technology as an adjunct, to a proposal for the promotion of peaceful uses, with control as an adjunct.” Yet despite developing states' hopes, the final Statute did not use the term technical assistance, while external safeguards, “not part of Eisenhower's original plan, featured prominently”. Almost as long as the UN Charter, it “foresaw many possible roles for the agency”. “It was up to its member states what they would make of them.” p. 60

Atoms for Peace, Scientific Internationalism, and Scientific Intelligence

John Krige on sharing the atom as a way of watching

The article in one breath

Krige argues that in the early Cold War, “openness and security, sharing knowledge or technology and implementing regimes of surveillance, were two sides of the same coin” p. 161. Atoms for Peace was “polyvalent”: it distracted from the largest weapons build-up in US history (841 weapons in 1952, 18,638 by 1960), tried to steer other countries' scarce resources into civil programmes under international watch, pushed a reluctant US industry into nuclear power, and staged a great scientific conference. Its precedent was the AEC's 1947 programme of sending radioisotopes abroad. That programme was won by appeals to scientific internationalism, but its conditions (reports, publication, open visits) also made transparency a means of inspection. The 1955 Geneva conference did the same on a bigger scale. It revived East–West exchange, and it was also “a panopticon” where each side measured the other: Americans went home “chastened” by Soviet competence, Soviets went home “reassured”. Building on Paul Forman, Krige concludes that sharing let a scientist be “both a good scientist and a good patriot”. The enriched-uranium reactors the US offered the developing world also “propelled the recipient up the ‘barometer of nuclearity’” and tied it into safeguards: autonomy “interwoven with dependence” pp. 180–181.

The speech

On 8 December 1953, back from Bermuda, where Churchill and Lord Cherwell had heard the idea, Eisenhower told the UN that an attack on the US would bring swift retaliation, but that the “two atomic colossi” must work together or be “doomed malevolently to eye each other indefinitely across a trembling world”. Stalin was dead, the Korean War over, and Moscow had shown willingness for a Four Power meeting without “unacceptable preconditions [regarding disarmament]”. The time had come for “a new avenue of peace”, coherent with the wish to be “constructive, not destructive”: “atoms for peace” pp. 161–162.

A UN agency would receive contributions of uranium and fissionable material and allocate them to agriculture, medicine and, above all, “abundant electrical energy in the power-starved areas of the world”. The proposal met “rapturous applause”; “the president himself was almost moved to tears” p. 162.

1. Distraction from the build-up

“Atoms for Peace was a polyvalent policy initiative”; Krige takes four of its dimensions. First, it was meant “to divert attention from Eisenhower's commitment to the use, expansion, and improvement of increasingly lethal nuclear weapons”. “Banalizing the bomb”, NSC 162/2 (approved 30 October 1953) said that if attacked the US would treat nuclear weapons “as munitions like any other”. The New Look shifted defence from manpower to nuclear power, and Eisenhower oversaw “the most massive weapons buildup in U.S. history”: 841 weapons in 1952, 18,638 by 1960 p. 162. fn 3 Military spending was 70% of federal spending in 1952–54 (the Korean War), 50% by the end of Eisenhower's term, and just over 30% in 1948–50.

It was “a weapon of what Eisenhower called ‘psychological warfare’”, for the “struggle for the minds and wills of men”: “Americans want a world at peace”. C. D. Jackson, recruited from Time Incorporated, saw it as a “direct challenge to the Soviets' near monopoly of ‘peace’ propaganda”, a way to “go on the moral and ideological offensive against the Communists … give it a bite and a punch which would really register on both sides of the Iron Curtain” pp. 162–163.

Castle Bravo

The need for “punchy” propaganda grew after the first Castle test, on 1 March 1954: “a technological triumph, and a human and public relations disaster”. Bravo vaporised three islands of Bikini atoll. At 15 megatons it was about a thousand times the Hiroshima bomb and 2.5 times the expected yield, and wind spread fallout far wider than planned. Personnel and islanders were evacuated; some islanders were exiled for years. The Japanese tuna boat Lucky Dragon Five, about 80 nautical miles east, had white radioactive ash fall on it for nearly three hours; most of its 23 crew suffered nausea, skin irritation and hair loss, and one died in September 1954 p. 163.

Protest seemed to confirm that thermonuclear weapons were “morally repugnant instruments of genocide”. At the NSC on 6 May 1954 Eisenhower worried the world would “think that we're skunks, saber-rattlers and warmongers.” Dulles said European allies thought “we are so militaristic”. On 27 May Eisenhower feared “a future which contained nothing but more and more bombs”. The US had to show that a country able to unleash “unimaginable destruction could contain that power and use it for human betterment” p. 163.

2. Diversion and superiority

It was “not simply an instrument of propaganda”. It aimed “to divert skills and resources from Moscow's military program and to restrict developing nations to purely civil activities” p. 163. fn 10 Krige's own work shows the same tactic in space: sharing to divert resources from national missile programmes. His aides suggested the donation be X, “a figure which we could handle from our stockpile, but which it would be difficult for the Soviets to match” p. 164 (Roehrlich attributes the memo to Eisenhower himself). The idea was to contain proliferation and set up safeguards “enforceable thanks to the transparency provided by an international agency”. Atoms for Peace was “an attempt to maintain U.S. nuclear superiority” by making others, the USSR included, devote scarce nuclear resources “to civil programs under international surveillance”. fn 12 The policy failed because far more uranium turned up than expected and plutonium was easy to make in reactors.

3. Pushing industry into nuclear power

The plan dovetailed with AEC pressure on “an immensely reluctant private sector”. At the end of 1952 reactor technology was still a military secret (the navy's submarine) and a government monopoly; engineering was hard, no best design existed, and “economic prospects were dismal”. General Electric and Westinghouse would not proceed without guarantees. Lacking an economic case, the AEC used another: “Cold war rivalry and psychological warfare provided that argument”, national prestige and leadership p. 164.

In October 1953, at a Chicago convention of electric utilities, Commissioner Thomas E. Murray, “a devout Roman Catholic determined to do all he could to combat atheistic Communism”, announced a demonstration pressurised water reactor at Shippingport, Pennsylvania (printed “60kW” Shippingport was 60 MW: not in Krige): “For years … the splitting atom, packaged in weapons has been our main shield against the Barbarians—now, in addition it is to become a God-given instrument to do the constructive work of mankind.” U.S. News and World Report: “An international race for supremacy has started. Britain … is in the race. Russia is probably starting. Now the U.S. is jumping in.” p. 164

Industry and exports needed the 1946 Act's secrecy relaxed. Three weeks before the speech the AEC sent two draft bills to the Budget Bureau, one for private industry and one for “a freer flow of information”. They became the Atomic Energy Act signed 30 August 1954. By February 1956, 25,000 technical reports had been reviewed: about a third fully declassified, about a quarter reclassified “L” (limited) for industry engineers p. 165.

4. The conference

That relaxation was a condition for the fourth facet: an international conference on the “benign and peaceful uses of atomic energy”. Lewis Strauss first raised it in Bermuda for its “propaganda value in winning worldwide support among scientists”. The first plan was a small NSF meeting in the US. With Isidor Rabi (Columbia, Nobel laureate, chair of the AEC's General Advisory Committee) it became a meeting to avoid overt politics and serve as “a real forum for the exchange of information in biology, medicine, basic science and engineering”. Talks with Sir John Cockcroft made power reactors the focus; the British proposed an exhibition, both trade fair and explanation. It would meet under UN auspices in Geneva in August 1955, giving time to declassify p. 165.

Historians' neglect, and what Geneva really was

“Historians of science and technology have paid little or no attention to Atoms for Peace.” Their interest in postwar physics centred on “winning future wars and … winning Nobel Prizes, not … winning hearts and minds” p. 165. The Swiss physicist Jean Weigle told Max Delbrück: “Nothing very interesting.” fn 22 Yet the meeting was “an outstanding scientific success in other ways”. A New Scientist columnist wrote that after about three days it was “becoming the most momentous scientific occasion the post war world had ever seen.” It exposed hundreds to reactors, lifted secrecy from reactor programmes, above all the Soviet one, and let Soviet and American researchers talk “relatively openly” for the first time since the war. Weigle: “The few Russian physicists [were] very nice and open.” The Soviet accelerator expert Vladimir Veksler: “a conference of scientists unique in history” p. 166.

Krige's argument

For scientists and their states Geneva was also “a way to probe into the laboratory life of others”, to assess their quality and “gauge the strategic significance of what their rivals were doing”. “It was a site not only of scientific exchange but also of scientific intelligence gathering.” p. 166

The Berkner report

This was official policy. Lloyd Berkner's panel for the State Department wrote Science and Foreign Relations, partly released in May 1950, insisting that awareness of foreign science was crucial. A classified appendix noted how hard this was now that science was identified with state power and secrecy. The “prime target” was the Soviet Union, but other countries mattered too, because their results could help US science or reach the Soviets pp. 166–167.

Science attachés in embassies “generally lacked any scientific credibility”. So the panel wanted qualified American scientists to gather intelligence “informally and without raising suspicions”, through “the free and open discussion of the content, procedures and mechanisms of the science involved”: publications, personal contacts at UNESCO, the international unions, congresses p. 167. fn 31 The CIA's late-1950s REDSKIN programme recruited travellers (tourists, scientists, even chess players and church leaders) to report on Soviet infrastructure.

Krige's view

“For Berkner and his panel, in the context of cold war rivalry, scientific internationalism and scientific intelligence were two sides of the same coin.” The first built trust and a shared body of knowledge; the second “exploited that trust to learn what others were doing … and to assess the dangers that may lurk behind what they left unsaid.” When science is “an affair of state”, “international exchange is at once a window and a probe, an ideology of transparency and, by virtue of that, an instrument of control”. “The Atoms for Peace Conference in Geneva in 1955 was such a panopticon.” p. 167

The precedent was 1947, when the AEC, under pressure from scientists at home and in Europe, agreed to send select radioisotopes abroad “under controlled conditions” pp. 167–168.

The Benign Atom in the 1940s: The Radioisotope Program

pp. 168–174 · Truman's 1947 offer · Strauss's objections · European anger · internationalism wins · conditions as inspection · three steps beyond isotopes · research bilaterals

Claim
The foreign isotope programme was won by appeals to scientific internationalism, and its openness also served US security, surveillance and leadership. Eisenhower's plan went further, sharing reactors.
Evidence
AEC minutes and catalogue; Strauss's letters; Oppenheimer's testimony; NSC 5507/2; the bilateral agreements.

Truman's offer

At the Fourth International Cancer Research Congress in St. Louis in September 1947, Truman announced that the AEC would make selected radioisotopes available abroad: first 28 isotopes of 19 elements, for research and therapy only. Demand soared; in February 1950 the AEC opened “a sort of atomic pharmacy” at Oak Ridge on “an assembly-line basis”. In 1951 foreign users could use isotopes in industry and have all domestic isotopes except tritium p. 168.

The programme “owed its immense success to the Manhattan Project”: the plutonium piles made isotopes and “rendered the cyclotrons obsolete”. Oak Ridge could make 200 millicuries of carbon-14 in weeks for about $10,000; cyclotrons would need 1,000 machines and over $1 million. By summer 1947, US and Hawaiian users had received over 1,000 shipments of 90 isotopes, and the AEC had almost 100 inquiries from 28 foreign countries, 75% medical p. 168.

Security says no

Before the war Europeans got isotopes from American cyclotrons, later chiefly MIT's. Moving production to an AEC pile and the “immensely restrictive” 1946 Act changed that: foreign requests were deflected p. 169.

The AEC's main worry was security: a shipment might reach people “who wish to develop atomic weapons”. Commissioner Lewis Strauss argued isotopes “would be useful as tools in biological research, metallurgical research, petroleum chemistry, and other areas which are part of the war-making potential of nations.” Without international control, the US “could not help scientists who may work for a putative enemy one jot or tittle without displaying naivete and imperiling our own security.” He thought scientists would “work just as zealously for dictatorships of the Right and Left as they were for democracy.” He blocked a little phosphorus-32 for the University of Helsinki, fearing Soviet hands, and worried about Norway's request for one millicurie of iron-59 because it came from a military laboratory and a team member “could be described as a Communist.” p. 169

European frustration

Continental scientists, with cities bombed and labs destroyed, looked to the US. After talking to Niels Bohr, an American wrote in July 1947 of “a crying, insistent need”: “even the bottle-washings we throw away can be used literally for months of research over there.” Denied “even the crumbs from the rich man's table”, Europeans felt “resentful and rebuffed” p. 169.

The atom meant something different in Europe. In the US “atoms for peace” was “haunted—and dwarfed—by atoms for war”; Spencer Weart notes the industry handled fissile material “by the ton” while peaceful imagery relied on isotopes “that could have been stored in a closet.” In Europe, rebuilding, the atom was “an opportunity, a symbol of modernity”, nuclear power “a promise for energy and independence”. Hiroshima was a “(ghastly) scientific experiment” proving scientists could harness the nucleus. Refusing a few millicuries was “simply absurd”, “an uninformed projection abroad of the meaning that the atom had in America” p. 170. fn 41 Strasser: the American history of the atom “cannot be transplanted to Europe”.

Europeans also thought US science compromised by military money. Caltech's Charles Lauritsen told Commissioner Robert Bacher that Europeans had “a somewhat exaggerated idea of the control which the Army and Navy exert”. Truman's Executive Order 9835 (March 1947) imposed loyalty checks on federal employees; liberal-left scientists were suspected on “extremely flimsy” evidence. Some foreigners put the US “in somewhat the same light as Russia on scientific and political matters” p. 170.

Internationalism wins

The refusals bred hostility and embarrassed US scientists, so sharing became “imperative to win back hearts and minds”. American scientists, four of five AEC commissioners (not Strauss) and the State Department supported it. Acting Secretary “Richard Lovett” [Robert A. Lovett Not in Krige] enthused about “the beneficient [sic] use of this new force” p. 171.

“The appeal to scientific internationalism was crucial in promoting this change in policy.” Oppenheimer told Congress in 1949 that America was “morally” obliged:

[isotopes] were discovered in Europe; they were applied in Europe; they are available in Europe, and the positive arguments for making them available … lie in fostering science; in making cordial effective relations with the scientists and technical people in western Europe; in assisting the recovery of western Europe; in doing the decent thing.

J. Robert Oppenheimer, JCAE hearings, 13 June 1949 · p. 171
Krige's view

Exchange redrew the line between permitted and forbidden, and also made sure the line was kept. In restoring “the international fraternity of knowledge” (Lilienthal), the US gained access to foreign labs, “and access was an insurance against abuse. The transparency that was intrinsic to international scientific exchange was also the means for monitoring what the other was doing.” p. 171

The conditions: requests had to go through the State Department, not scientist to scientist; recipients sent three copies of a progress report every six months, published results where possible, and agreed that “qualified scientists irrespective of nationality will be permitted to visit the institutions where the material will be used” “in accordance with well-established scientific tradition” p. 171.

So internationalism rebuilt research abroad and “opened doors and loosened tongues”, letting “inspectors” check use. It fed US science and enhanced “our national security, which depends on continued progress”. And it served leadership: “With its superior technological potential … the United States can expect to profit more quickly and more fully than any other nation from the exploitation of published findings.” Trading on internationalism reinforced “the common defense and security of the United States.” p. 172

At home, too: the programme would win “the wholehearted support of United States scientists and medical doctors” and wean the public from nuclear fear. The American Museum of Atomic Energy opened at Oak Ridge in 1949, “in the shadow of the massive uranium enrichment plants”. fn 51 It gave away irradiated dimes and sold uranium ore and ashtrays: the effect was “to familiarize and banalize the nuclear”. The atomic pharmacy was open to press and public too p. 172.

Three steps beyond isotopes

By the time Eisenhower made Atoms for Peace “an official plank of U.S. foreign policy, with the full weight of presidential authority behind it”, the idea that science and foreign policy could be advanced while “actually enhancing, U.S. national security” was “already well established”. This is “not to belittle” the initiative: it was still “a huge step forward” in three respects p. 172:

  1. Reactors. It shared the technology that makes isotopes. The US had lost its monopoly: Britain, Canada, France and the USSR had reactors, and in 1951 Britain and Canada were already giving isotopes on easier terms. “Research reactors provided” a new platform for both superpowers pp. 172–173.
  2. New states. About 20 new nations appeared in 1945–55, about 30 more in the next decade. NSC 5507/2 (12 March 1955) expected Moscow to use atomic energy “to gain the allegiance of the uncommitted areas of the world”, “a critical sector of the cold war struggle”. In Medhurst's “purple prose”, “the reactor program functioned as a form of industrial imperialism” p. 173.
  3. Bargaining chips. Reactors eased the growth of the US stockpile under the New Look and the nuclearisation of NATO: access to uranium and thorium, training NATO allies, securing bases, “an icon around which to rally pro-American sentiment” p. 173. fn 56 After the 1954 Act, new NATO defence treaties loosened restrictions and let West Germany plan for atomic energy.

Research bilaterals

From June 1955 the US signed bilaterals offering unclassified reactor information and up to 6 kg at a time of 20%-enriched uranium. Turkey was first; Greece and Portugal followed; uranium suppliers Argentina, Belgium and Brazil were early; Franco's Spain and apartheid South Africa “(whose gold mines were rich in uranium ore) were not forgotten.” “The United States' addiction to nuclear raw materials … quashed any scruples” about partners' politics. Two dozen bilaterals by August 1955; 39 by 1961 pp. 173–174.

The Soviet Union, excluded from the isotope programme, was now “an essential partner”: relations were easier and “the secret was out”, with a fission bomb in August 1949 and a thermonuclear test (“Joe-4”) in August 1953. Secrecy now made less sense than exchange that would show US scientists Soviet capabilities; “coffee breaks and walks by the lake in Geneva” would help p. 174.

The 1955 Atoms for Peace Conference in Geneva

pp. 174–180 · the numbers · the swimming-pool reactor · media wonderland · Soviet counter-moves · détente · intelligence · chastened and reassured

Claim
Geneva was a triumph of marketing and internationalism and, at the same time, an intelligence exercise that shocked the West into new spending.
Evidence
Laura Fermi's account, Union Carbide documents, NYT and CBS coverage, Veksler, Price, Seitz, Hewlett and Holl.

The meeting

The first International Conference on the Peaceful Uses of Atomic Energy opened at the Palais des Nations on 8 August 1955, “a decade almost to the day after the first use of a nuclear weapon”. Homi J. Bhabha presided over twelve days. More than 1,400 delegates from 73 countries, almost as many observers, and more than 900 journalists attended p. 174. Britain, France, India, Switzerland and the US sent greetings; Eisenhower repeated his pledge that man's inventiveness be “consecrated to his life”.

The US delegation was the largest, 259 people, 183 of them scientists. Laura Fermi, Enrico Fermi's widow, wrote its official account fn 61 (Krige notes its “irritating sexism”: men's achievements beside “pretty girls” as guides and translators). Britain was second; the USSR sent 78, including “the usual KGB staffers” p. 174.

Three themes: physics and atomic piles; chemistry, metallurgy and technology; medicine, biology and isotopes. Of 3,000 papers, more than 550 were American (from over 1,000 submissions), with “full engineering details” of US plants p. 175. Exhibits were for delegates until 4 p.m., then public; a trade fair ran downtown. fn 64 Firms from ten countries: 50 British against 16 American, and the British “were the stars”, with inquiries from 33 countries within days.

The swimming-pool reactor

The US centrepiece was a working swimming-pool reactor of Oak Ridge design, run by Union Carbide for the AEC. It was flown in in June, set in a wooden chalet in the Palais grounds, seen by 50,000 visitors, and sold to Switzerland for $180,000 afterwards p. 175.

Krige's view

“The presentation of the U.S. reactor in Geneva was a masterpiece of marketing. It was intended to demystify nuclear power and to show that anyone and any nation could exploit it safely and to social advantage.” p. 175

  • It ran at 10 kW, as close as possible to what Atoms for Peace could supply.
  • Fuel: 18 kg of uranium, 20% U-235, “identical to that in the United States' allocation of 200 kilograms of fissionable material for the proposed International pool”.
  • Three control rods; simple enough that “technically qualified visitors” could operate it, as Eisenhower did a few days before opening.
  • Union Carbide: it showed a reactor could run “in complete safety without elaborate preparations or complicated facilities”; a panel explained protection by detectors, shielding, gloves and jackets p. 175.
  • A research tool: capsules placed by long tongs or compressed air. A panel in four languages: “An Enriched Uranium-235 reactor has many uses”, from education to biomedical research. Seven films and a research library pp. 175–176.

A media wonderland

CBS's Watson Davis promised to report “the good, the true and the beautiful, about atomic energy”, noting the absence of “bad and naughty atomic words” such as “bomb”, “radiation”, “fallout” and “secrecy”. Strauss announced a $1 million Ford Fund with $75,000 annual awards and a medal for peaceful-atom work. The Times described “a veritable wonderland of advanced designs” and a “virtually unlimited supply of energy”; William L. Laurence: “[M]an is on the eve of his most extensive industrial, social and economic revolution.” p. 176

The Soviet side

Moscow was “equally determined” to use the benign atom for propaganda: “Let the atom be a worker, not a soldier.” Paul Josephson: the peaceful atom showed that a once illiterate, agrarian nation had become a leading power, a symbol of the regime's legitimacy, and scored points with Eastern Europe, whose programmes rested on Soviet isotopes and training p. 176.

The US reactor in the UN gardens was “somewhat galling”, since the USSR had brought the first working power reactor on stream the year before fn 74 (5,000 kW to the grid in 1954); its model “lacked the impact”. Moscow invited scientists from 41 nations to its own conference on 1–5 July 1955; the British and American academies declined, citing timing; attendees saw the new power station p. 177.

Easing tension

The conference apparently reduced fear: ten of its twelve days made the Times front page. Veksler said it “moved public opinion” and strengthened the goodwill of the Four Power Conference in Geneva weeks before fn 77 (18–23 July 1955; during the science meeting the USSR announced a cut of 640,000 troops). Control of weapons was not formally discussed, but an American report said the IAEA was “given an enormous boost”, and more than 30 countries were starting nuclear programmes p. 177. Scientists formed “lasting friendships”; Veksler: “the scientists of the world easily found a common language” p. 178. “In Geneva scientific internationalism blossomed.”

Intelligence, and a Western shock

The same openness allowed intelligence gathering, “given added urgency by the closed character of the Soviet program”. The meeting “opened eyes in the West to what the Soviets had achieved, and it alerted Moscow to the extent of the nuclear programs in the United States and its allies.” It “created a declassified space in which one could learn what others were doing, judge their competences, and assess their priorities” p. 178. Soviet work had been sealed for almost a decade; the brief rapport ended in 1947; few journals were translated; Soviet delegates were ordered to boycott meetings. Many in the West thought little of Soviet science. Teller believed Klaus Fuchs had advanced the Soviet bomb by ten years: “a ludicrous exaggeration.” Hewlett and Holl: the US delegation was “surprised” by “the highly technical competence of Russian scientists and engineers generally, and the large numbers of students”. General John B. Medaris recalled that before Sputnik Russians were thought “retarded folk who depended mainly on a few captured German scientists” p. 178.

Veksler reported that the USSR “is very successfully advancing along its own road”, and that foreign colleagues were impressed by its nearly finished 10-billion-electron-volt proton accelerator pp. 178–179. Western colleagues “were not just stunned, but panicked”. Melvyn Price, of the JCAE's research subcommittee, concluded in March 1956 that the US lacked scientists and engineers; at stake was “our ability to compete with the Soviets in the struggle for men's minds”. Fred Seitz (Illinois) called high-energy physics “the principle [principal] frontier”, producing “uniquely trained manpower” apt for weapons planning, and urged a Defense Department programme so the US would “retain its leadership” p. 179.

Krige's view

We do not know how the Soviets used what they learned (Veksler admired Ernest Lawrence on accelerators and Walter Zinn's boiling water reactor report). But Soviet scientists, like their state, wanted respect. “Scientific credibility, legitimacy, and cognitive authority are social accomplishments.” They are “constructed and consolidated in interactions between peers”, whose judgement of another's truth claims is “interwoven with an assessment of their competence”. Americans came seeing Soviet colleagues as trapped in a backward society: “They went home chastened.” Soviets came awed and uncertain: “They went home reassured.” pp. 179–180

Conclusion

pp. 180–181 · Forman · good scientist, good patriot · the developing world · barometer of nuclearity · autonomy and dependence

Paul Forman (1973) showed that scientific internationalism in Weimar Germany expressed nationalism, reconciled by “the eminently simple formula that the [international] fame and honor which the scientist wins accrues also to his nation and patron”:

According to this classical conception—largely due to and propagandized by the scientists themselves—the contribution of science to national prestige is an automatic and inevitable byproduct of scientific achievement. It does not require a choice on the scientist's part between serving the interests of science and serving the interests of his nation, between behaving like a good scientist and behaving like a good patriot.

Forman, quoted p. 180
Krige's conclusion

The paper shows how “the fusion between the invocation of internationalism and the pursuit of national interest” was achieved in the early Cold War nuclear field. Generosity and prowess were to win hearts and minds and prove each system's legitimacy; sharing also gave “a window into the scientific life-worlds of allies and enemies alike”. “International scientific exchange deftly reconciled the universalistic appeal to the pursuit of truth with the particularist needs of national security. By weaving surveillance and security into the fabric of openness and internationalism … one could be both a good scientist and a good patriot.” “One chord was struck when they spoke to their colleagues abroad, the other when they spoke to their patrons in Washington or Moscow.” p. 180

The same logic governed technology for less industrialised countries. The US would share through the IAEA, fn 89 (in December 1954 US officials sorted countries into developed ones such as Western Europe, less developed ones such as Turkey and Iran, and what a Swiss report called “des territoires primitifs”; the middle tier would get aid and reactors). But the Geneva model used enriched U-235, and so “propelled the recipient up the ‘barometer of nuclearity’ and inevitably embedded the ‘benefactor’ in a regime of surveillance implemented through the imposition of ‘safeguards.’” fn 90 (the phrase is from Hecht's chapter in the same volume) pp. 180–181.

The US pushed fissile-fuelled technology while Britain, Canada and France sold natural-uranium power reactors, perhaps “to combine sharing with surveillance”. “‘Autonomy’ in the nuclear field was interwoven with dependence”, reinforced by tying US aid to enriched-uranium reactors from General Electric and Westinghouse p. 181. fn 91 In Spain, 1955–58, “every significant element of choice” was pre-empted by the US rule that loaned fuel be used only in a swimming-pool reactor; Abraham discusses “self-reliant” India's dilemmas.

The Cold War, the Developing World, and the Creation of the IAEA

Elisabeth Roehrlich on the IAEA's founding as a North–South story, 1953–1957

The article in one breath

The IAEA's creation “was shaped by the overlapping dynamics of superpower relations, decolonisation, and the growing influence of the ‘global South’ in the United Nations” p. 3. US–Soviet understanding was “strikingly good at times”, but the uranium producers and the future recipients of aid “faced each other as rival blocs”. That explains why the agency began with a broad membership (56 states when only eight had reactors) and why both superpowers backed it. The Statute was first drafted by eight Western, colonial and uranium-producing states. UN pressure widened the table to twelve and turned the IAEA into a development agency. The hard fights were North–South: the Board of Governors formula (India against South Africa), the IAEA's link to the UN, and safeguards, which new states called “atomic colonialism”. It was “the first major international organisation in the creation of which developing countries had considerable impact” p. 19. Proliferation was not central; sovereignty and access outweighed verification. The founding story, borrowing Gavin, is “both part of yet outside the story of the US-Soviet rivalry” p. 20.

Abstract and introduction

pp. 1–4 · untitled in the source · 81 states, 56 founders · why so broad, why cooperation · the thesis · the archives

Abstract. The creation of the IAEA was shaped by superpower relations, decolonisation and the global South's rise at the UN. Many developing countries argued the new body “should not exacerbate global inequalities, practice discrimination, or institutionalise ‘atomic colonialism’”. US–Soviet understanding “was at times strikingly good”, but uranium producers and future aid recipients “often faced each other as rival blocs” p. 1.

In September–October 1956, delegates from 81 countries adopted the Statute in New York; a year later the IAEA was established in Vienna with 56 initial members: the US and USSR, Australia, five African states (Egypt, Ethiopia, Morocco, Tunisia, South Africa), Asian states including India, Japan, South Korea and Thailand, almost all of Europe (not East Germany), Latin America (including Mexico, Argentina, Brazil) and Israel. Today it has 166 members pp. 1–2.

Two puzzles

  • Why so broad? When the conference opened, “no more than eight countries had nuclear reactors under operation” p. 2 (fn 4, the Belgian delegate Errera).
  • Why did the rivals cooperate? The talks overlapped with crises: the Hungarian Uprising was crushed a week after the conference ended; the Suez Crisis peaked days after. “The nuclear question, of all things, opened up a new channel for international cooperation”, in stark contrast to the “fruitless UN disarmament talks” going on at the same time p. 2.

The mid-1950s saw new states' influence grow: 16 new UN members in 1955 (Cambodia, Ceylon, Laos, Libya among them), Sudan, Morocco and Tunisia in 1956. Mazower: the UN moved from an “instrument of empire into an anticolonial forum”. So: how did the North–South divide and the Cold War interact, and how did developing countries position themselves? p. 3

Roehrlich's thesis

“The creation of the IAEA (1953–1957) was shaped by the overlapping dynamics of superpower relations, decolonisation, and the growing influence of the ‘global South’ in the United Nations.” US–Soviet understanding “was strikingly good at times”, while “the uranium-producing states and the future recipients of IAEA technical assistance faced each other as rival blocs”. A close look at this “multi-polar negotiation history” explains the broad membership and superpower support p. 3.

Bechhoefer (1959) saw lessons in “the art of negotiating with the Soviet Union”; Wadsworth compared the founding to the Congress of Vienna fn 11 (his editors even put his chapter right after one on the Congress). Yet the founding “has, as yet, been somewhat neglected”. fn 2 IAEA history is “still in its infancy”: Fischer's official history (1997), Scheinman (1987), Brown (2015). Hecht has studied South Africa and the IAEA, but an international history of the inception “has been missing” p. 3.

The beginnings of the negotiations

pp. 4–7 · UNAEC and the word “Cold War” · the speech · Maddock · Holloway's ambivalence · Soviet objections · Britain

The IAEA was not the first attempt to internationalise the nuclear question. The UN Atomic Energy Commission (UNAEC) was set up in 1946. Its tense atmosphere led Herbert Swope, adviser to US delegate Bernard Baruch, to speak of a “Cold War” between the superpowers, a notion soon popularised p. 4. fn 14 Lippmann's The Cold War (1947) spread it, though George Orwell used it first. The origin shows how much the nuclear question, East–West confrontation and building a postwar order were intertwined. The UNAEC deadlocked and was dissolved in 1952.

A year later came the speech: the “dread secret”, the “two atomic colossi”, peaceful uses for agriculture, medicine and power, the “power-starved areas”, and an “international atomic energy organisation” fed from stockpiles of “normal uranium and fissionable materials”, moving material from military to civilian use “with the promise of slowing down the development of nuclear weapons” pp. 4–5.

Shane Maddock argues Eisenhower chose an inspirational UN speech over confidential talks with Moscow because Atoms for Peace mattered as “a Cold War weapon”. Yet bilateral notes began soon after fn 18 (Bohlen had told Molotov on 7 December). The US kept both options open. South Africa's ambassador to the US, J. E. Holloway, summed up the ambivalence p. 5:

If the Russians are prepared to take part in it, it could be a means of bringing East and West together in the field of atomic energy and gradually reducing international tension. If Russia stays out it could become an additional weapon in the cold war.

Holloway to Pretoria, 4 February 1955 · p. 5

The Foreign Office read US aims the same way p. 5.

Soviet scepticism, 1954–55

  • No disarmament. Moscow wanted a declaration “not to use the atomic, the hydrogen, or any other weapon of mass destruction” fn 23 (Zarubin's note to Dulles, 19 January 1954).
  • The bank. It objected to a “bank of fissionable material”. “Briefed by Soviet scientists”, Molotov argued that small transfers would hardly reduce weapons, and since “the peaceful application of atomic energy is connected with the possibility of simultaneous production of atomic materials utilised for the manufacture of atomic weapons”, the plan might even “accelerate the nuclear arms race” p. 6. The State Department began to see the proliferation dangers.
  • China. Membership should be universal, including the PRC; the US recognised only the ROC p. 6.

Britain, as in Reading 2, wanted a “bridge between East and West”, would rather drop the plan than proceed without Moscow, preferred Commonwealth cooperation, but would accept the IAEA with the Soviets “as a price worth paying to secure the relaxation of international tension” pp. 6–7.

The early influence of the uranium-producing states

pp. 7–8 · the eight · Portugal's “overseas provinces” · a double standard · Baruch precedent · Western Europe left out

In 1954 Australia, Belgium, France, Portugal and South Africa joined the correspondence. “States principally involved” was the language of a UN resolution for a Subcommittee of the “Disarmament Committee” (US, USSR, UK, Canada, France), so France was “self-evident”, and leaving it out would hurt its prestige p. 7. The other four came for raw materials: the Belgian Congo (“the Western world's most abundant supplier”), South Africa, Australia (mining since the early twentieth century), Portugal (uranium oxide to Britain and the US). “Five of these eight states were colonial powers.”

Colonies mattered most for Belgium and the Congo, but also for Portugal: at the 1956 conference its delegate talked up uranium prospects in what he “euphemistically called Portugal's ‘overseas provinces Angola and Mozambique’”. The Soviet Union had vetoed Portugal's UN admission from 1945 to 1955 (it joined in December 1955). So the US excluded the PRC for not being represented at the UN, while inviting Portugal before it was a UN member p. 7.

The producers formed a faction. They were, as Holloway put it, “in the fortunate position of actually being asked by the leading Western powers” to associate closely. This leverage went back to the UNAEC: Belgium and Brazil backed the Baruch Plan's International Atomic Development Authority only if unmined uranium and thorium would not become its property pp. 7–8.

Western Europe was underrepresented: of Euratom's six founders (1957), only Belgium and France were among the eight. The Netherlands and the Scandinavians were “irritated” not to be asked. The US and UK drafted; aims, policy bodies and membership rules were set in 1954 by two Western nuclear states, four more NATO members and two Commonwealth uranium producers p. 8.

The group of negotiators expands

pp. 8–11 · the First Committee, November 1954 · SAC precedent · Geneva as the Soviet entry point · twelve · Zarubin · the Conference

Moscow took the talks more seriously from late 1954 and joined in early 1956. From 5 to 23 November 1954 the First Committee held 19 meetings on peaceful nuclear cooperation. Three things stood out p. 9:

  1. A broad wish, from France, Britain, Pakistan, Egypt and India, to include the USSR, which signalled it might compromise even on banning atomic weapons.
  2. Egypt, Peru and “notably Pakistan” stressed the needs of less advanced countries. Eisenhower had mentioned “power-starved areas”, but “it was only during the UN General Assembly debate of autumn 1954 that the new organisation was characterised as a development agency.”
  3. Resolution 810 (printed “810(XI)”; fn 42 gives “810 (IX)”) created a Scientific Advisory Committee (US, France, Canada, UK, USSR, Brazil, India) for the Geneva conference, “an important precedent” for adding Brazil and India.

After Geneva, American, Soviet, British, Canadian, French and Czechoslovak experts discussed diversion. The talks produced no result but mattered: “It was in Geneva that the Soviet Union in fact entered the IAEA negotiations.” In early 1956 Czechoslovakia (a uranium producer Moscow had called “necessary” since early 1954), Brazil and India joined p. 9. The enlargement to twelve “was the direct result of pressure from within the UN General Assemblies of 1954 and 1955” p. 10.

Pakistan and the Philippines wanted more non-nuclear developing states and dropped the idea under British and Commonwealth pressure. The Assembly also decided on a Conference on the Statute; developing states insisted it must not be a “rubber stamp” p. 10.

The twelve in Washington

The twelve met in Washington from February to April 1956. Thirty-nine UN members had commented on the draft circulated on 22 August 1955. US–Soviet relations were “surprisingly good”, with an outstanding “spirit of cooperation”. Wadsworth on his counterpart George N. Zarubin:

He actively sought consultations with the United States Delegation, and emphasized the importance of achieving unanimity. While he adhered tenaciously to many of the standard Soviet ideological positions, he was apparently given considerable leeway in his instructions to accept compromise, and, in fact, at times to suggest them.

Wadsworth to Dulles, 26 April 1956 · p. 10

“In contrast to the cooperative nature of American-Soviet relations stood the growing tensions between the uranium producers and non-aligned India.” p. 10

The Conference on the Statute

The five-week conference in autumn 1956 strengthened the Soviet bloc (Eastern Europe plus Ukraine and Belarus), but “the real majority group … were the developing countries, and American and Soviet delegates kept bidding for their favour.” Zarubin said Moscow joined to support developing countries: standard rhetoric, but more too, since non-participation “was not a political option”. Wadsworth: the USSR knew “it would face almost certain hostility from the ‘developing world’ if it refused to help achieve what they so desperately desired” pp. 10–11. The Statute was opened for signature and a Preparatory Commission set up for the First General Conference in Vienna p. 11.

Cold War competition and the nuclear ‘have-nots’

pp. 11–13 · proliferation off-centre · Bhabha and the British · two proliferations · “They will in any event” · development as competition

The arms race was seen as an East–West symptom. By 1952 the US, USSR and UK had tested; France followed in 1960. “In contrast to current debates on nuclear proliferation, the danger of nuclear weapons spreading to other states was not at the centre of the IAEA negotiations.” fn 52 Gavin: nonproliferation became a major superpower concern only in the late 1950s and early 1960s. fn 53 Albert Wohlstetter introduced the metaphor of “proliferation” p. 11.

Some aspects came up. In March 1956 British and Indian delegates agreed privately on the need “to prevent the less responsible countries from possessing small and possibly untested weapons with which they could threaten their neighbours”. But India's main aim was to prevent strict safeguards, so Bhabha called the danger minor, since such states would lack know-how p. 11.

The Geneva technical talks touched on both proliferations. Here Roehrlich defines vertical proliferation as “the indigenous development of nuclear weapons” and horizontal as “the spread of nuclear weapons from one state to another”; the Soviets meant vertical (bigger stockpiles), the Americans heard horizontal pp. 11–12. (Reading 2 defines vertical as growth of US and Soviet stockpiles.) The question, in Rabi's words, was which safeguards were “feasible and necessary” to supply reactor material “without increasing the risk to the security of the world”. No technical solution emerged p. 12.

Parts of the administration thought proliferation inevitable. Asked whether the agency might help more countries get weapons, AEC chairman Lewis Strauss answered: “They will in any event.” fn 58 (a conference with the president, 2 May 1957). Henry Cabot Lodge assured the First Committee in 1954 that “the ingenuity of the scientists and statesmen will find ways of assuring that materials are not diverted”. Both sides went ahead although “the ingenuity of the scientists” had found no such ways p. 12.

Roehrlich's view

Critics call the speech “more a propaganda ploy and cold-war weapon than a sincere attempt at containing the spread of nuclear weapons”, but the Geneva discussions show the initiative “had a nuclear arms control background”. Still, peaceful uses dominated the agenda p. 12.

For new states nuclear technology meant growth, prestige and modernity, a means “to create political legitimacy for the postcolonial state” (Itty Abraham). Nuclear aid became Cold War development competition. C. D. Jackson told Strauss in May 1956 that “Atoms for Peace, wisely placed in various parts of the world, [could] recapture for the U.S. a lot of the recent international slippage” pp. 12–13.

In spring 1954 the eight had argued over development aims; some leading US negotiators “strongly opposed” going from 8 to 12 and “wanted the IAEA to be operated by the United States and its allies”. Only after the 1954–55 Assemblies and Soviet entry did development become “an accepted fact”. Both superpowers courted developing states as their “true friend and champion” (Walser). Bilateral programmes from 1955: the US with Turkey first; the USSR with Eastern Europe in spring 1955 p. 13.

Controversies over the Statute

pp. 13–18 · the Board formula · India vs South Africa · Pakistan's manoeuvre · China · the UN link and Vienna · Cole as director general · safeguards

Three debates dominated: (1) membership, especially the Board of Governors; (2) the IAEA's relation to the UN; (3) inspection and safeguards pp. 13–14.

1. The Board of Governors

Early US plans had five permanent members (US, USSR, Canada, UK, France) plus six elected for two years. Australia, Belgium, Portugal and South Africa objected. With twelve negotiators, “ten countries would hold permanent seats” if all got their way (the text then lists nine). A State Department memo: “almost political suicide to submit a statute with a Board composed in this manner since, excepting for the Soviet Union and India, the Board would have the appearance of a NATO uranium cartel” p. 14.

Mainly on India's proposals, the Board went to 23. The formula combined geography and “advancement”: the outgoing Board designates the five members “most advanced in the technology of atomic energy including the production of source materials”, plus the “most advanced” member in each of eight geographical regions; the General Conference elects the rest. It showed developing-state influence “but, at the same time, allowed for a majority vote of the United States”. The IAEA was the first international organisation to replace a general call for geographic spread “with a specific list of geographic areas” p. 14.

Egypt, Jordan and Liberia criticised the Africa/Middle East region having one shared designated seat. South Africa had got “production of source materials” into the formula, guaranteeing it, isolated at the UN and unable to win election, “quasi-permanent membership on the Board” pp. 14–15.

India against the uranium producers

India cast itself as “the spokes-country of the developing nations”, against the privileges of Australia, Belgium, Portugal, South Africa “and to some extent Brazil”. Brazil championed “the needs of the under-developed areas of the world” but, as a producer, mostly sided with the producers p. 15. India's main target was South Africa, over apartheid's treatment of Indians. Pretoria saw India as the biggest threat to its role and wanted to keep “her out of the inner circle”; Du Plessis wanted India kept out “before it has been finally established”. Uranium mining was a colonial question. Bhabha pointed out that India was the only Asian or African country among the twelve p. 15.

India argued that uranium would soon be found everywhere, so producers deserved no special role. The new deposits of the mid-1950s also undercut the plan's arms-control logic: no “zero-sum game between uranium for peaceful purposes and for weapons” p. 15.

There was a regional angle. Pakistan did not feel represented by India and knew the twelve wanted their draft to pass unchanged. Before the Washington talks a Pakistani diplomat approached the South African embassy, saying Pakistan's attitude to the producers was “considerably less extreme” than India's. The South Africans considered backing Pakistan, but the move failed. Already in 1955 the State Department had backed Bhabha as president of the Geneva conference, against strong AEC opposition, seeing that concessions to India were needed for Soviet cooperation. The US was “ready to support non-aligned India instead of SEATO member Pakistan” when success required it pp. 15–16.

China

The US, its allies and the ROC opposed PRC membership; the USSR, Eastern Europe and new states including India, Syria and Indonesia wanted it. The solution shows “a diplomatic pattern that was characteristic”: Moscow accepted the US red line but objected on record, reserving its right to raise the question; at the conference it made its point without forcing a vote, before the president's election. The Statute let non-UN states join later, since some in the West thought they would benefit from a chance “to observe and perhaps supervise Beijing's atomic development” (Walser) pp. 16–17.

2. The link to the UN, and Vienna

India and the new states wanted a close UN link; South Africa, under fire over its rule of Namibia (South-West Africa), wanted the IAEA as independent of the UN as possible, and US observers noted its delegates' “open hostility” to the UN. The US wanted a UN specialised agency; the USSR, following UNAEC ideas, wanted it under the Security Council and its veto. Compromise: an autonomous organisation linked to the UN by a separate agreement, reporting to the General Assembly and, on peace and security, to the Security Council (Article III.B.4). The details went to the Preparatory Commission p. 17.

Hammarskjöld and Bunche wanted the IAEA in New York and close to the UN, and watched sceptically as the superpowers agreed on Vienna, which “underlined the autonomy of the new organisation and its rather thin link to the UN system” p. 17.

Yet Bunche helped when rivalry returned. At the First General Conference (autumn 1957), Strauss insisted on an American director general, Congressman William Sterling Cole; the Soviets refused, and even US allies felt the top official should come from neither superpower. Bunche suggested compromises on other posts and had Strauss and the Soviet delegate Vassily Emelyanov meet privately in his office for an hour and a half. Next day the Soviets said they would not oppose Cole “in order to continue the business-like atmosphere of the Agency”, a diplomatic success for which they were “praised for its cooperative attitude” pp. 17–18.

3. Safeguards

Most new states saw safeguards as “a new form of great power imperialism”; the Soviet bloc agreed the IAEA should not practise “discrimination”; India said donors would not be inspected. Bhabha's “haves” and “have-nots” warning (as in Reading 2) was backed by the Soviet bloc and opposed by the US, Canada and the UK; the Ukrainian delegate spoke of a “yoke of atomic colonialism” p. 18. The USSR had stressed state sovereignty but accepted the safeguards: “as a supplier state, it would not fall under Agency safeguards.”

Western Europeans were irritated at being treated like developing countries. “Colonialism dies hard”, a State Department memo commented “laconically”. Like the developing countries, the Netherlands and the Scandinavians resented the producers' role p. 18.

Participants thought the four years of drafting long; seen from today, agreement “within only four years” in a tense phase of the Cold War is “striking” p. 19.

Roehrlich's conclusion

“What makes the founding history of the IAEA unique is that it was the first major international organisation in the creation of which developing countries had considerable impact on the drafting of the Statute.” They were the majority in 1956; for Morocco, Sudan and Tunisia it was their first major diplomatic gathering. The 1954–55 Assemblies paved the way for both broader representation and US–Soviet compromise p. 19.

The IAEA was a “litmus test” for new states' status and was shaped by North–South tensions “a decade before the Group of 77”. Leaders saw nuclear progress as a pillar of national identity. For the superpowers the peaceful atom offered “alliance management”: technical assistance could “define spheres of influence, shape political geographies, and strengthen loyalties”. Yet the idea of a “new industrial revolution” (from Hammarskjöld's welcome) united negotiators across East, West, North and South p. 19.

The “haves” and “have-nots” bargained, but the line was fluid, as Pakistan and the Philippines showed. The talks were “not shaped by a simple confrontation of the first, the second, and the third world”; the global South “showed many different faces”: India, speaker of the non-aligned and model of Asian decolonisation; South Africa, “in terms of industrial progress a developing country”, but a key uranium supplier and Southern ally of the US; Brazil, a producer and US supporter yet an advocate of developing countries, independent since 1822. The USSR and Czechoslovakia mostly voted with India; South Africa and Brazil usually with the US pp. 19–20.

With Francis Gavin, the story “reveals a narrative that is both part of yet outside the story of the US-Soviet rivalry”. The arms race prompted the speech, but for new states decolonisation, voting power and access mattered more. At the conference, fears for sovereignty and slower progress “outweighed the complex problem of security and verification”; the Board's composition shows their influence p. 20.

The superpowers acted “both as competitors and partners”, trading propaganda while building understanding. The IAEA was framed as a non-Cold War project: the Polish delegate wanted the conference free of “all the arguments dug up in the dustbins of the Cold War”; “compromise” and “balance” were its key words p. 20.

Arms control still “played a certain role”, but participation in civilian programmes was more prominent. Proliferation seemed hard to prevent and “in the long run, inevitable”. The rivals nonetheless built an organisation that, “if not a genuine effort towards what was called ‘non-proliferation’ in the 1960s, at least established an international setting to address these issues” pp. 20–21.

It was “an important diplomatic success”. But at the First General Conference in September–October 1957, Sputnik made delegates realise “that the Cold War was intensifying once more” p. 21. fn 100 Fischer recalls Emelyanov startling delegates, and disconcerting NATO members, by announcing the satellite's flight on 4 October 1957.

Negotiating Global Nuclearities

Gabrielle Hecht on apartheid, decolonization, and the Cold War in the making of the IAEA

The article in one breath

The IAEA has been portrayed as a technical agency where geopolitics is “extraneous or inappropriate”. Hecht argues that “this separation of technology and politics was discursive and never enacted in practice” p. 25. Seen through South Africa's archives, the agency's “technopolitical regime” was the contested outcome of two visions of order: a hierarchical, bipolar Cold War order and a decentralized order inspired by decolonization. Nuclearity, “the degree to which a nation, a program, a policy, a technology, or even a material counted as ‘nuclear’”, was “a spectrum, not an on-off condition”. South Africa's diplomat Donald Sole kept an ostracised apartheid state on the Board of Governors for twenty years by triangulating three positions. He insisted the IAEA be technical, not political. He performed a dual identity, “western” and “African”. And he pushed Pretoria to acquire nuclearity beyond uranium mining. Along the way he helped calibrate the “barometer of nuclearity” (what counts as “most advanced”, what counts as “source material”), cut safeguards down to a “façade”, and won the ten-ton exemption that still applies. Hecht's verdict targets Fischer and Scheinman: their story of “politicization” reproduces a Cold War vision in which postcolonial nations “have less right to weigh in on nuclear issues by the simple virtue of being less nuclear” p. 47. Without understanding this, workable solutions to proliferation are out of reach.

Abstract and Introduction

pp. 25–27 · the IAEA as arbiter · Cold War framings · two missing elements · nuclearity defined · the South African route

Claim
IAEA history needs a “double reframing”: its regime came from decolonization as well as the Cold War, and nuclearity itself was negotiated.
Evidence
The South African archives.
Against
Cold War-only IR accounts; Scheinman's intrinsic/extraneous split; teleological safeguards histories.

After years of obscurity the IAEA is now portrayed as “the most authoritative judge of a nation's ability to produce atomic weapons”, its impartiality guaranteed by its inspectors' expertise and diverse origins. Its verdict decides whether a state is a “rogue state”. When the US ignored IAEA findings on Iraq, protest used those findings to show the failure “(or fabrication)” of US “intelligence”. Most leaders treat it as “the ultimate arbiter of global nuclearity”, though it cannot temper the bellicose behaviour of “the world's most powerful nation or of the world's worst pariahs” pp. 25–26.

IR experts explaining the IAEA's failure to control proliferation frame it “primarily in cold war terms”: its mission was to transcend East–West divisions, and they focus on nonproliferation regimes. Some distinguish “intrinsic” politics (nuclear matters such as US–Soviet safeguards talks) from “extraneous” ones, such as the campaign that expelled apartheid South Africa from the Board in 1977 and the General Conference in 1979 p. 26. fn 1 Scheinman (1987), Fischer's insider history (1997), and Forland's thesis (1997), “the most sophisticated”.

Hecht's two corrections

(1) The IAEA's technopolitical regime, “the political programs, technical and scientific practices, and institutional ideology that constituted and governed its structure and development”, “was not merely a product of cold war politics. It was also a product of decolonization politics.” It was “the continually contested outcome of negotiations between visions of a hierarchical, bipolar global order structured by cold war tensions and visions of a decentralized global order inspired by decolonization.”

(2) “Nuclearity—that is, the degree to which a nation, a program, a policy, a technology, or even a material counted as ‘nuclear’—was a spectrum, not an on-off condition.” Its meanings were “neither universally agreed upon nor stable in time or space” pp. 26–27.

fn 4 Fischer and Forland acknowledge developing nations. Fischer dates the G-77's growing influence to 1973, but treats it as “an obstacle to be overcome” and calls charges that safeguards are “neocolonial” frivolous; he celebrates aid “without excavating the self-interest of donor nations”. Forland takes India more seriously and shows safeguards “emerged in fits and starts”, not as the IAEA's founding purpose. Both offer “teleological narratives”, with India's objections overcome “on the road to ‘technical perfection’”.

The conclusion will use this reframing to explain “the stunning inadequacy of today's international nonproliferation regime”. The route is “somewhat unorthodox”: the South African archives p. 27.

Why South Africa

In the late 1940s South Africa contracted with the Combined Development Agency (CDA) to supply thousands of tons of uranium to US and British weapons programmes. That got it into the eight-nation drafting group in 1954 and onto the Board, where it stayed among the most powerful second-tier nations until its expulsion in the late 1970s. From the start, Egypt, India and “(more discreetly)” the Soviet Union opposed it, and new African members joined the opposition in the early 1960s. Postcolonial states saw South Africa's centrality “in the world's only truly global nuclear institution as a major affront, an indication of the West's insincerity about decolonization and racial equality.” p. 27

South African delegates navigated by triangulating three points: (1) the relation between technology and politics the IAEA should enact; (2) how far uranium production counted as nuclearity in the IAEA's hierarchy; (3) a dual identity for South Africa as both “Western” and “African” p. 27.

Framing Global Nuclearities

pp. 27–33 · the Board's 23 seats · what is “source material”? · uranium's eroding nuclearity · Sole · technical vs political · keeping the UN out · western and African

Claim
The Board formula made Cold War rankings of “advancement” a structural feature of the IAEA, conjugated “into a postcolonial tense”. As uranium lost nuclearity, South Africa had to work to stay “most advanced”.
Evidence
The 1956 seat list; South African minutes; Sole's speeches, reports and memoirs.

Timeline of decolonization and the IAEA

Drafting began in 1954 with eight weapons states and uranium suppliers; in 1956 the USSR, Brazil, India and Czechoslovakia joined. The twelve submitted a draft to the UN in September 1956, around the decolonization of Morocco, Sudan and Tunisia. In July 1957 the IAEA came into existence and Ghana became independent. By the end of 1960 eighteen more nations were independent, five with confirmed or soon-confirmed uranium: the Central African Republic, Gabon, Madagascar, Niger and Zaire p. 27.

The Board of Governors

The Board “would both reflect global nuclearities and shape their future”. Political credibility needed East and West; technical credibility needed expertise. India worried the IAEA would reproduce “the global imbalances perpetrated by the twin processes of colonialism and industrialization” pp. 27–28. fn 5 At the statute conference Arab, Asian and some Latin American delegations called the Statute “undemocratic”, perpetuating “an elite of ‘have’ nations which would repeat the inequalities of the first industrial revolution”.

India's solution, “easy acceptance”: combine nuclear “advancement” with regional distribution. The eight regions “simultaneously reflected cold war tensions and anticipated decolonizations”: North America, Latin America, Western Europe, Eastern Europe, Africa and the Middle East, South Asia, Southeast Asia and the Pacific, the Far East. In 1956 the 23 seats were p. 28:

  1. Five “most advanced in the technology of atomic energy including the production of source materials”: US, USSR, UK, France, Canada.
  2. Five “most advanced” in other regions, again including source materials: South Africa, Brazil, Japan, India, Australia.
  3. Two rotating producer seats: Belgium alternating with Portugal; Czechoslovakia with Poland.
  4. One designated supplier of technical assistance, likely rotating among the Scandinavians.
  5. Ten elected by the General Conference, “with due regard to equitable representation … of the members in the [eight] areas.”
Hecht's reading

“The focus on ‘advancement’ thus made the obsession with technological rankings that dominated cold war discourse a structural feature of the IAEA. But the regional framework … conjugated that advancement into a postcolonial tense.” p. 28

So what made a nation “most advanced … including the production of source materials”? What were “source materials”, and how nuclear was producing them? For South Africa these were high-stakes questions. By 1957 the IAEA was the only international body where it could hope for major influence. It expected the IAEA to shape the uranium market; uranium “had saved many of South Africa's gold mines from collapse”. A permanent seat seemed vital, and only technical expertise and ties to Western nuclear systems could secure one, since opposition to apartheid made election impossible pp. 28–29.

The nuclearity of “source materials” mattered in two forums: global hierarchies (strongly nuclear source materials put producers higher up) and safeguards (strongly nuclear materials need strict controls). The article takes the first here and the second in the last section p. 29.

Uranium's self-evident nuclearity, and its erosion

In the decade after the war, uranium's nuclearity “seemed self-evident”, resting on four assumptions p. 29:

  • It was the only natural radioactive material that could (with effort) fuel weapons.
  • Atomic bombs could rupture “not only global order but the globe itself” and, in Western rhetoric, were “single-handedly responsible for obviating the old colonial order in favor of a new nuclear order.”
  • Uranium was rare: monopolising it would keep “the Communist ogre at bay”, so the West had to secure all sources. “Nothing mattered more than this (and certainly not the institution of apartheid on the southern tip of Africa).”
  • Its place at the core of Western systems demanded secrecy: surveys, reserves, production and contracts were “state secrets one and all”.

“And if uranium's nuclearity imposed secrecy, that secrecy in turn reinforced its nuclearity”: it was the only ore with legislation ensuring secret production p. 29.

By the mid-1950s the consensus frayed. Pitchblende was rare but lower grades were not: “the stuff was everywhere.” The Soviets had their own sources. A Western monopoly was impossible; the challenge lay in processing, not finding. So “just as the final discussions on the IAEA statute got underway … the nuclearity of uranium ore eroded.” One sign: producers whose only claim to fame was uranium had to rotate seats pp. 29–30.

India had tried “in a blatant attempt to increase its own standing” to relegate South Africa and Australia to mere “producers”, forcing them to share a seat; the US and UK helped them resist. South Africa's delegate successfully got “production of source materials” into the definition of advancement, but foresaw it would not last: South Africa could not “sit back and rely upon [its] importance as a producer or … position in the West” p. 30.

Three positions

  1. Maximal separation of technology and politics: the IAEA should pursue technical, not political, agendas.
  2. Rehearsing South Africa's place in Western nuclear networks while situating it firmly in Africa, “thereby testing the national identity cultivated by their government”.
  3. Emphasising non-uranium nuclear achievements, which meant lobbying Pretoria for an atomic energy programme p. 30.

Donald Sole

South Africa's main IAEA representative, Donald Sole, was a career diplomat, a native English speaker with a history degree from Rhodes University. Posted in London in the war, he befriended Afrikaner diplomats; that and his Afrikaans probably helped him survive the National Party's 1948 victory. His memoirs suggest he “deplored (without actively opposing)” apartheid's racism, not least because isolation made his job harder; loyal to the government, he tried to make South Africa “more palatable” and to temper Pretoria's isolationism pp. 30–31.

Position 1: “primarily a technical Agency”

Sole decided to “depoliticize the terms of negotiations by focusing on technical issues”, a strategy with special resonance in South Africa, where apartheid justified policies as administrative, business or technical. At the first General Conference in 1957 he said:

…this Agency should be primarily a technical Agency. Political considerations, of course, cannot and should not be excluded …, but they should be allowed the minimum possible influence … If they are permitted to get out of hand, this Agency can all too easily develop into a propaganda forum—into yet another platform for the wasteful and time-consuming exposition of conflicting ideologies.

Sole, 7 October 1957 · p. 31

Most delegates heard a reference to East–West tension, “so who could possibly disagree?” For Sole it laid the ground to call objections to apartheid “purely political” and so out of order p. 31.

He also fought to keep the IAEA autonomous from the UN. At the 1956 conference Soviet, Indian and Czech delegates wanted direct UN control, anticipating the postcolonial influx. With only “anemic” US and UK support, South Africa played on fears for sovereignty fn 16 (a persistent theme, raised most often by India and other non-Western nations, per Forland) and warned that reports on members' actions “might open the way to United Nations interference in the domestic affairs”. Sole won a compromise: only UN actions “in accordance with the [IAEA] statute” could be considered, that is, only nuclear resolutions p. 31.

India objected that this was “too restrictive”. The Canadian asked whether India “wished the United Nations to go ‘fishing’ in the affairs of member states”; the Indian, “in some confusion”, withdrew. Sole saw India's “success in giving the impression of speaking on behalf of the new African countries” and feared an “Afro/Asian alignment … under Indian leadership”. He told Pretoria the five words “in accordance with this Statute” were included “to prevent such ‘fishing expeditions’” pp. 31–32. He had won “some sort of statutory separation between technology and politics”, though “the edges of nuclearity remained nebulous” p. 32.

Position 2: western and African

For decades Afrikaners had styled themselves “simultaneously western and African”. Against black African nationalism this “might seem grotesque”, yet the duality strengthened. Western: political heritage, ancestry, corporate ties, industry, “not to mention white rule and fervent anticommunism”, backed by “the still-segregated, even more rabidly anti-communist United States”. African: Afrikaners had cut ties to Europe, pioneered the land, suffered under British colonialism and made South Africa their “God-given homeland”. The 1948 victory offered a chance to forge “the most ‘advanced’ African nation in the history of the world—so advanced, indeed, that it would barely count as African at all.” p. 32

Diplomats used the IAEA “as a laboratory” for this duality: South Africa both supplied the West and was Africa's “most advanced” nuclear nation. The duality had to be “relentlessly performed”. Opening the first general debate in October 1957, Sole described a 1957 radioisotopes conference in Pretoria with experts from colonies, the Central African Federation, Ghana, the UN and WHO, implying non-white scientists were welcome, and mentioned two Japanese scientists given “every facility”. He glossed over the fact that Japan's trade importance had earned Japanese visitors “honorary white” status; no records survive of how Ghanaian and Central African scientists were handled pp. 32–33.

Nuclearity and “Development”

pp. 33–39 · the West and the Rest · demonstration reactors · uranium markets · “apply the brakes” · the Cairo centre · Atomic Energy Board · the questionnaire · CCTA

Claim
Debates on development split “the West and the Rest”. South Africa backed a narrow, commercial, technical agenda, and used the fight over a Cairo training centre to calibrate a barometer of nuclearity in its favour.
Evidence
UK, US and Canadian proposals; Sole's reports; the 1959 questionnaire; CCTA debates.

Everyone agreed, rhetorically, that promoting peaceful uses was the mission, endowed “with tiresome predictability” with high moral purpose. In practice nuclear leaders wanted to build reactors, make nuclear power pay, and create markets for their technologies and uranium. fn 24 Forland shows how commercial considerations shaped the UK's and Canada's approach to safeguards. “Hopes for a global military and economic panopticon embedded in Atoms for Peace” (citing Krige) found wider expression in the IAEA. “The West would sell to the Rest”, somehow ensuring civilian use; the IAEA should back nations able to build infrastructure fast rather than be “yet another international development agency”. Western delegates, “perhaps inspired by Sole's eloquence”, said the IAEA should be technical and leave politics to the UN. The Rest, led by India, said emerging nuclear hierarchies must not perpetuate global inequalities: “On no account should politics be left out.” pp. 33–34

Three Western proposals

  • UK: fund only economically viable reactors, which would “necessarily limit the construction of power projects to the most advanced countries”, as the “have nots” would complain.
  • US AEC: three “demonstration reactors” in different geographic (“read: geopolitical”) settings, funded if they used enriched uranium. Only the US then designed commercial enriched-uranium reactors, so the offer sought “to make U.S. technology the basis for worldwide nuclear development”, bypassing Soviet, French and Canadian natural-uranium designs.
  • Canada: education first, since trained scientists and engineers were “an essential preliminary to the development of a large-scale market in uranium after 1965” p. 34.

South Africa's interests

South Africa hoped the IAEA would track rival producers, set a uranium price it could influence, and be a marketplace. Nothing came of this at first; in 1959 Sole feared South Africa had “considerably over-rated [the IAEA's] importance” for its uranium industry. But his “discreet marketing” paid off: by 1961 France and Israel had asked to buy South African uranium pp. 34–35. fn 30 Sole toured European nuclear facilities with two scientists “in a thinly veiled effort to peddle South African uranium”.

Sole asked whether to bid for a demonstration reactor “in Africa, although not necessarily in the Union in view of the difficulties which would arise in accommodation of nonwhites”. Pretoria ignored him and told him to “apply the brakes” on training for developing nations. He urged caution: rather than “a blank negative”, back a few cheap proposals, such as fellowships, “an important gesture vis-à-vis the under-developed countries” that kept US and Canadian goodwill p. 35.

In 1959, choosing between conferences on radioisotopes and on small and medium power reactors, Pretoria wanted the latter: anything encouraging “increased consumption of atomic fuel” would help narrow “the widening gap between production and consumption”. South Africa kept saying “the development of nuclear power is the main eventual goal”. Sole: the IAEA “shall attract good scientists only if the Agency has scientific prestige and scientific prestige is unfortunately not built up on programs of technical assistance.” The strategy combined “a push to expand uranium demand with an aversion to providing development funds to postcolonial states” pp. 35–36.

Hecht on Sole's logic

The next step: “a nation's legitimacy within the IAEA rested on its nuclearity: the more nuclear a nation, the louder its voice should be.” Sole was disgusted that few underdeveloped countries spoke in the 1959 debate, yet they turned aid for small power reactors into “a major debate”. Was he “truly oblivious to the irony” that the conference he backed made them think such reactors feasible? “The logic of apartheid had sent him into a tailspin”: only technical assistance could give them the expertise that, by his reasoning, would legitimate their voice p. 36.

The Cairo training centre

The Board sought a middle road: regional training centres in places “somewhat—but not overly—‘advanced’”, which meant evaluating nuclearity. Latin America came first, with Brazil and Argentina competing to be “most advanced”. A bigger storm came when the United Arab Republic (UAR) proposed a centre in Cairo in 1958, welcoming “friendly nations” (implicitly excluding Israel). Turkey bid too, backed by Israel and South Africa p. 36. fn 40 The UAR (Egypt and Syria, February 1958; Syria left in 1961; Egypt kept the name until Nasser's death in 1970); Hecht suggests the centre may have been meant to express and build Arab unity, to be tested in Egyptian archives.

Sole saw an Egyptian plot to take South Africa's seat as the region's “most advanced”. He reminded Pretoria that South Africa “has at present no reactor, no national isotope centre and is not in a position to accept trainees from other countries in the regional area” (“for racist reasons”, Hecht adds). The Egyptians seemed to gloat, inviting him to see their progress pp. 36–37.

So South Africa needed its own programme. Of two proposals, a separate Atomic Energy Board (AEB) or research under the Council for Scientific and Industrial Research, Sole backed the AEB as the stronger statement of nuclearity, and “partly (though only partly) for this reason”, Pretoria chose it p. 37.

When the IAEA planned a regional visit, Sole argued the region included all of Africa, lobbied the colonial powers (Britain, France, Belgium, Portugal), and suggested Belgium's reactor in the Congo as an alternative site, to “protect the rights and interests … of the African countries and territories as opposed to the Middle East countries”. He used South Africa's status as a Western fuel supplier “while simultaneously claiming to speak for all Africa”, and it worked: the delegation would visit every country in the region with substantial nuclear activity p. 37.

The questionnaire: a barometer of nuclearity

The IAEA's 1959 questionnaire covered “all possible manifestations of nuclearity” p. 38:

  • teaching programmes; experimental training facilities in nuclear physics;
  • radiochemical and radioisotope laboratories;
  • source materials in the soil and prospecting;
  • the atomic energy programme, now and expected;
  • ore processing and purification plants;
  • research workers; health physicists and radiation protection experts;
  • willingness to finance regional training;
  • cooperation with other states in the region.

“Mightily pleased with how he'd influenced the calibration of this barometer of nuclearity”, Sole urged “comprehensive replies”, which would bear on the “rival candidature” of South Africa and the UAR. With the new programme approved, South Africa would top the technical measures. Regional cooperation was the sticking point, so he suggested stressing cooperation “with the metropolitan States having territorial responsibilities in the area”, another test of the dual identity p. 38.

The CCTA

The Commission for Technical Co-operation in Africa South of the Sahara (CCTA) combined colonial powers (Belgium, France, Portugal, UK), new nations (Ghana, Liberia) and “apartheid-infested southern Africa” (Nyasaland, Rhodesia, South Africa): exactly the western-African duality. Sole pushed the IAEA to formalise relations with it and pushed Pretoria to take part p. 38. fn 48 Afrikaners in government did not necessarily see this, so he fought “on two fronts”.

The UAR governor Ismael Fahmy questioned the CCTA's legitimacy; Egypt opposed any distinction between sub-Saharan Africa and the rest of the continent. Sole told the Western powers Fahmy wanted to divert attention to “the Mediterranean littoral in general and … Cairo in particular”. Rumour said African members never agreed to the link and the CCTA was a creation of “the ‘colonialists’”. Britain rescued South Africa by “trotting out the good old technology-politics distinction”: the CCTA's functions “were essentially technical, hardly political at all, and very useful”, and it was genuinely African because “its headquarters were at Lagos.” pp. 38–39

Who Speaks for African Nuclearity?

Claim

Evidence
Delegation reports 1960–63; the secret telegram; the 1963 declaration.

In 1960 Ghana applied to join, and delegates “fell over each other” to sponsor it. To forestall a UAR and Afro-Asian resolution, the UK proposed Commonwealth sponsorship. Pretoria told Sole by secret telegram that support “should go no further than affirmative vote”. Sole feared being the only Commonwealth member not to co-sponsor would look “a purely political gesture” from a delegation “foremost in pleading that international politics be kept out”. Claiming his office hadn't deciphered the telegram in time, he replied that he had already co-sponsored p. 39.

A month later the General Conference considered more Africa/Middle East Board seats. South Africa, “this year … the only African representative on the Board”, could not let the elected member, Iraq, “act as a spokesman for Africa”. Sole worked with Tunisia, Ethiopia, Ghana, Iraq, Morocco and even the UAR on a statute amendment for “adequate African representation”, adopted unanimously. For him it showed “the extent to which East, West, and neutralists are competing for the favor and support of Africa”, and he urged Pretoria to use his prestige to improve relations with the continent pp. 39–40. “Sole's efforts fell on deaf ears”: months later Prime Minister Hendrik Verwoerd withdrew South Africa from the Commonwealth p. 40.

Decolonization kept threatening the seat. In 1961 the Republic of Congo considered reviving its uranium industry and renegotiating Belgian contracts. Sole saw India behind a bid to replace Belgium with the Congo, since “Belgium owes her seat to production in the Congo”, which would also undermine Portugal (“already under heavy fire”) and cost Europe a seat. Congolese production was not revived and the colonisers kept their seats p. 40.

By 1963 a temporary consensus: the IAEA signed with the CCTA (and Euratom, among others), and the Africa/Middle East region gained two Board seats, securing South Africa. But the Regional Training Centre for the Arab Countries opened in Cairo, boosting Egyptian nuclearity, and Gabon (a producer), Ivory Coast and Nigeria joined. Director General Sigvard Eklund hailed “further steps toward close collaboration with African countries” p. 40.

Twenty nations then signed a “Declaration on the Incompatibility of the Policies of Apartheid of the Government of South Africa with the Membership of the IAEA”, urging a review of its policy. Sole called it “purely political”, said the IAEA was “not a proper forum”, and denied that “all Afro-Asian scientists are unacceptable on South African territory”. With personal backroom support and, above all, US lobbying (“the fact that they were prepared to use their lobbying machine on our behalf”), the motion failed p. 41.

Still, “only a simple majority of the members of the Board is required to oust us”. Sole foresaw “the Afro-Asian upsurge … will bring South Africa very much under the harrow”. The only answer: keep the IAEA narrowly nuclear, not “just one more international organization for the provision of technical assistance which provides at the same time a platform for the propagation of varying ideologies, Western, Communist, Neutralist, anti-White, anti-colonialist and the rest”, while upholding sub-Saharan Africa's interests. “The government's discursive duality was vital for its survival in the IAEA.” p. 41

Safeguards and the Regulation of Nuclearity

pp. 41–46 · morality talk vs markets · accepting safeguards · defining materials · accounting · the ten-ton threshold · a façade · inspectors

Claim
Safeguards, wrapped in “morality talk”, were driven by market concerns. South Africa shaped definitions and thresholds so that “source materials” sat low on the barometer, and helped make safeguards a façade.
Evidence
1956–60 South African files; the technical committee report; Article XX; INFCIRC/66.

The US pushed safeguards hardest; other sellers “at least paid lip service”. As Forland shows, commercial considerations shaped sellers' approaches. Buyers disliked end-use controls. India argued that regulating access through safeguards “perpetuated colonial inequalities”; no one wanted weapons, but a principle was at stake. “Struggles over safeguarding the global nuclear order were saturated with morality talk”, but “more mundane concerns drove the practices” pp. 41–42.

Accepting safeguards, 1956

For South Africa safeguards were “a subset of the uranium market issue”. Canada, which saw South Africa as “their main potential competitor in export markets”, wanted to ensure its position was not weakened by commitments South Africa did not accept. Pretoria's External Affairs would object to controls infringing sovereignty, “so dear to Afrikaner nationalists”; Sole urged acceptance to keep American goodwill, conditional on competitors accepting too and on a Board seat. Secretary G. P. Jooste reassured the government that the draft statute bound members only for transactions through the agency: membership did not commit South Africa, “as a producer of source material”, to controls on bilateral deals p. 42.

W. C. du Plessis feared this looked like a cop-out. Controls would not hurt: they would not cover existing CDA contracts; they would operate “in the recipient country only”; uranium oxide would face lighter controls than fissionable materials. Refusal risked the charge of “having willfully, and in pursuit of its own economic interests, taken the risk of widening the area of international insecurity.” And a Canadian delegate had joked, before State Department and AEC officials, about South Africa's “apparent unwillingness to show its hand”, a sign Canada might pressure Washington pp. 42–43. So South Africa would accept safeguards. But what were they?

Defining the materials

A technical committee defined “fissionable material”. South Africa's scientist reported:

The Indian representative was clearly keen to ensure (a) that natural uranium was not defined as fissionable material and (b) that the concentration of plutonium, U235, U233, etc., required before a “source” material became a “fissionable” material were relatively high …

[We] adopted the view that natural uranium fitted best into the source material category, although it is of course a fissionable material (but then so is U238 under appropriate conditions), that the term “special fissionable material” should be used, that the line dividing source and special fissionable materials should be at the 0.7% enrichment level … The definitions proved astonishingly difficult to draw up.

South African committee member's report · p. 43

The fourth draft passed with India abstaining over an inconsistency. Each category meant different controls; India wanted the ambiguous “fissionable materials” and lost. The Statute used three categories: “source materials”, “special fissionable materials”, and “uranium enriched in the isotope 235 or 233” pp. 43–44. fn 71 Article XX's full definitions: special fissionable material means plutonium-239, uranium-233, enriched uranium and materials containing them; source material means natural and depleted uranium, thorium, and their compounds and concentrates (the quoted text has the misprint “one ore more”).

Hecht's point

All agreed uranium oxide counted as “source material”, each for its own reasons. “These definitions created a barometer of nuclearity, with ‘source materials’ being the least nuclear and therefore subject to the most relaxed safeguards and ‘special fissionable materials’ being the most nuclear and therefore subject to more stringent safeguards.” p. 44

Which stage to control?

The US and Britain first wanted to control “every stage from the sale of source material until the manufacture of the end product—plutonium”, then decided to “work backwards … from plutonium” to find practical control points, the task of the new safeguards division. Its Canadian head reassured South Africa: “fully seized of the concern of the producers”, he favoured accounting “far less elaborate than the American AEC has had in mind”, perhaps just bookkeeping returns and spot inspections every six or twelve months, no extra work for producers willing to open their books p. 44.

The ten-ton threshold

What about small quantities? The US wanted safeguards on 2–10 metric tons of uranium with 0.5–1.0% U-235 (full safeguards above ten tons), reasoning that more than 200 grams of fully enriched uranium was dangerous and two tons of natural uranium yield 200 grams. Sole: “whatever the theoretical technical justification … politically … it is impossible to equate 2 tons of natural uranium with 200 grams of fully enriched material.” Enriching would take so much knowledge, effort and money that it could not escape notice. South Africa should insist on exempting ten or fewer tons below 1% U-235 p. 45.

As Board chairman in 1960, Sole rallied the UK and Commonwealth producers; the UK told the US the producers' support “was a sine qua non if safeguards were to be effective”. Sole threatened that otherwise South Africa would not treat IAEA safeguards as binding on bilateral deals. “With a combination of threats and incentives, he won his case.” fn 78 The ten-ton threshold still applies (INFCIRC/66/Rev.2, 1968) p. 45.

Sole doubted the system would be “more than a façade”, useful for diplomacy but unlikely, “prophetically”, to stop proliferation. From talking to Homi Bhabha, he was sure India would build a bomb p. 45.

Mines and inspectors

First, he made sure uranium mines and ore-processing plants were excluded from “conversion plants”, and so from safeguards; many producers agreed. He also led a “largely successful campaign to liberalize other controls” on source materials. Second, he worked on the “Inspectors' Document”. Apartheid departments worried South Africa might be inspected by Indians or black Africans. Knowing the racial framing “would not fly in Vienna”, Sole translated it into national sovereignty, which most members shared. The document let an inspected state “reject any individual Agency inspector without giving any reasons”, as long as it did not reject all of them pp. 45–46.

Conclusion

pp. 46–48 · 1977 ouster · Fischer's silence · Scheinman's “politicization” · rogue nations · Sole's legacy · 1993 and the erasure of history

In 1977 Sole's fears came true. The group he called “the Afro-Asian upsurge”, now 77 nations, demanded South Africa's replacement by Egypt. David Fischer, the IAEA's official historian, had been Sole's junior colleague on the South African delegation, “a biographical detail mentioned nowhere in the book or its jacket” p. 46. fn 85 Hecht suggests no conspiracy, but notes Hans Blix's preface lists Fischer's IAEA career in detail (with the misprint “Statue”). Fischer writes:

In June 1977, the Board decided by a vote of 19 to 13, with one abstention, to uphold the Chairman's nomination of Egypt as the Member State in Africa “most advanced in nuclear technology including the production of source materials.” Egypt's nuclear program was very modest and it produced no source materials (i.e., uranium) but worldwide revulsion against apartheid made it politically inevitable that the South African Government would sooner or later lose its seat on the Board. This revulsion also led to the rejection of the credentials of the South African delegation when the General Conference met in New Delhi in September 1979. After a democratic government had taken power in Pretoria, South Africa, with Egypt's concurrence, regained its seat on the Board in 1995.

Fischer, quoted pp. 46–47

“Though mild in tone”, the passage implies South Africa was ousted “for purely political reasons”, proven by its reinstatement. Lawrence Scheinman (1987), writing the year after South Africa's state of emergency and Gorbachev's perestroika, was blunter: South Africa's treatment “starkly illustrates the problem of politicization”; the US opposed it “on the basis of the principle of universality and the practical importance of South Africa to nuclear matters”; it was “political opportunism” by “those with a relatively limited interest in the basic purpose of the agency” p. 47.

Hecht's verdict

Such accounts reinforce “a teleological view of the agency as one whose only true, legitimate purpose was to ease cold war tensions and guard against nuclear proliferation”. They “unquestioningly deploy the very objects of negotiation”: the meanings of nuclearity and the boundary between technical and political. They reproduce a vision in which “cold war concerns trump postcolonial ones every time—a vision in which postcolonial nations have less right to weigh in on nuclear issues by the simple virtue of being less nuclear.” p. 47

Calling new nuclear states “rogue nations” hides that they never accepted “a global nuclear order that never acknowledged the legitimacy of their voices”, and, as George Perkovich argues about India, hides their domestic and regional motives. “Condemnations of safeguards and nonproliferation treaties as neocolonial may be intensely self-interested, but they are also genuine expressions of an alternative vision of the technopolitics of global order.” She is not condoning proliferation: “unless we understand this dimension of the making of global nuclearity, we cannot hope to find workable solutions to proliferation” p. 47.

South Africa, with US and UK support, tried to assert a consensus on nuclearity and on technical versus political. Sole helped mark the barometer that legitimated South Africa's Board seat for twenty years; his IAEA–UN separation “did put a leash on postcolonial politics in the IAEA for twenty years”; the West repaid him “with continued support to the bitter end (when thirteen nations voted to keep South Africa in the agency)”; and his whittling of safeguards “helped make his own prophecy—that these would be little more than façades—come true.” pp. 47–48

In 1993 the world learned South Africa had itself been a “rogue nation”, defying the order it helped build. F. W. de Klerk confessed its weapons capacity and announced their dismantling in the same breath: “Bombs, enrichment plants, testing grounds—all gone”. The 1994 elections brought political redemption; in 1995 it rejoined the IAEA. The South African consensus that the bombs were destroyed to keep them from a black government “shimmered only faintly on the edges of global discourse”. IAEA inspectors made South Africa “the poster child for nuclear nonproliferation”. “In a final, jaw-dropping irony”, officials at NECSA (formerly the AEB) say the IAEA itself has forbidden them to release the weapons archives, for fear they reach other “rogue nations”. “It is difficult to imagine how this latest erasure of history will help to dismantle nuclear weapons elsewhere.” p. 48

Nuclear Ontologies

Gabrielle Hecht on what counts as “nuclear”, and who decides

The essay in one breath

Bush's 2003 claim that Saddam sought uranium “from Africa” (Niger) rested on forged documents. For Hecht its real significance is what it reveals: “‘Uranium’ is therefore as underspecified technologically as ‘Africa’ is underspecified politically” pp. 320–321. Since 1945 “nuclear exceptionalism” has treated the atom as ontologically unique, on the left and the right. Yet the nuclear always ran through colonial Africa, where uranium mines were not counted as “nuclear” at all. Nuclearity “is a technopolitical spectrum that shifts in time and space”, with consequences for politics, culture and health. Colonial and nuclear “rupture-talk” were intertwined: Europeans equated non-nuclear with colonised. At the IAEA, India won regional representation, while South Africa made “source materials” a sign of “advancement”. Definitions then pushed uranium ore and yellowcake down the scale until, by 1972, mines were excluded from safeguards altogether. The same ontology denied miners protection (Navajo miners in the US; black miners in South Africa until 1999) and now shapes the fight over depleted uranium. The NPT made peaceful nuclearity an “inalienable right”, the basis of Iran's claim today. With a nuclear “renaissance” coming, the questions are who may develop, who may only buy, and “safe for or from whom?”

I. “Uranium from Africa”

pp. 320–323 · untitled part I · the Niger affair · nuclear exceptionalism · clash of civilizations · colonial uranium · nuclearity defined · rupture-talk

The Niger affair

In October 2002 Bush claimed Saddam had “recently sought significant quantities of uranium from Africa”; the administration cited CIA intelligence of an attempt to buy 500 tons from Niger. As doubts grew (even at the CIA), by the 2003 State of the Union officials credited British intelligence; the source mattered less, said Bush, than the conclusion that Iraq planned nuclear weapons p. 320.

Both claims were “decisively refuted”. In February 2002 the CIA sent former diplomat Joseph Wilson to Niger; he found no sale. Appalled that Bush meant Niger, he went public in the New York Times; an “anonymous source” then outed his wife as a CIA operative. The proof was forged documents “peddled by an Italian businessman” p. 320. fn 1 Iraq had bought uranium from Niger, Portugal and Brazil in the 1970s but stopped in the 1980s; Bush did not mean those.

The story's re-emergence produced “a cacophony of opinion, mostly pitting Bush stalwarts against defenders of Wilson”. Bickering over personal credibility “drowned out” whether Bush misled the nation into war, and missed the episode's real significance p. 320.

Hecht's reading

Would they have said uranium “from Asia” for Kazakhstan? “Highly unlikely. Africa remains the ‘dark continent,’ mysterious and politically corrupt – highly plausible qualifications for a nuclear supplier.” And Niger was “a nation most Americans couldn't distinguish from Nigeria”. Acquiring “uranium” was assumed to be “prima facie evidence of a bomb program's existence”, yet it must be mined, processed into yellowcake, converted into uranium hexafluoride, enriched and made into fuel. “‘Uranium’ is therefore as underspecified technologically as ‘Africa’ is underspecified politically.” pp. 320–321

The episode shows “the ambiguities of the nuclear state, and … the state of being nuclear”: what is a “nuclear state”, and are the criteria scientific, technical, political, systemic? These ambiguities are not just doublespeak; “they lie at the heart of today's global nuclear order – or dis-order” p. 321.

Nuclear exceptionalism

From 1945 “both cold warriors and their activist opponents cultivated the notion of nuclear exceptionalism”: the bomb as fundamentally different from any other creation, the “ultimate trump card”, with status “directly proportional to the number of nukes”. “Nuclear” scientists won prestige and funding; activists called nuclear dangers unprecedented; splitting atoms, “rupture in nature's very building blocks”, reinforced it. Morality-talk boiled down to “a simple duality: nuclear technology represented either salvation or depravity.” p. 321

After the Cold War the “clash of civilizations” replaced the “superpower struggle” fn 2 (Huntington). On the left, Régis Debray (1990): “green [i.e., Islam] has replaced red”, and “the nuclear and rational North deters the nuclear and rational North, not the conventional and mystical South.” On the right, Bush's “axis of evil” fed fears that nuclearity might escape “that ‘rational North’” p. 321.

The nuclear was always global

“Yet the ‘nuclear’ has never been the exclusive province of ‘the North’.” Hiroshima's uranium came from the Belgian Congo; Britain's programme used imperial ties to African suppliers; Australia and South Africa got a “material role in the ‘defense of the West’”; France depended on uranium from its African colonies p. 321.

Policy analysts object that mining alone does not make a nation nuclear. The Office of Technology Assessment's 1995 report on the “nuclear activities” of 172 nations did not list Gabon, Niger or Namibia, which together produced nearly 25% of the world's uranium that year p. 321.

So are mines not nuclear? Can nuclearity be measured with a Geiger counter? Miners are exposed, usually less than enrichment workers. In the 1950s Tandroy men dug small open-cast mines in Madagascar with hand tools, knowing nothing of bombs or radioactivity; their exposure will never be known fn 5. Were they less “nuclear” than Australia's Ranger mine, scrutinised since 1975, and were all these less nuclear than the Malvési conversion plant, the Almelo enrichment plant, or Livermore? p. 322

Hecht's thesis

“Such questions are problems of ontology, not merely of rhetoric.” Nuclearity “can never be defined in simple, clear-cut, scientific terms. Rather, nuclearity is a technopolitical spectrum that shifts in time and space. It is a historical and geographical condition, as well as a scientific and technological one.” Degrees of nuclearity structure control of radioactive materials, anchor anti-nuclear movements, and shape occupational health rules and compensation. “Nuclearity is not an on-off condition. Nuclear ontologies have a history, and a geography.” p. 322

Colonial and nuclear rupture-talk

The geography of nuclearity needs another Cold War rupture-talk, decolonization. Less than three months after Hiroshima, the UN Charter was the first international law to refer to “the principle of equal rights and self-determination of peoples”; in principle “(though certainly not in practice)” a new order of equality. The 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights was a moral leap, like nuclear rupture p. 322.

Leaders blended the two. French and British leaders hoped the bomb would replace colonialism as a source of power and stop their own “colonization by the superpowers”. Lord Cherwell (1951): without its own bomb Britain would “sink to the rank of a second-class nation, only permitted to supply auxiliary troops, like the native levies who were allowed small arms but no artillery.” French deputy Félix Gaillard (1951): nations without atomic development would be, 25 years on, “as backward … as the primitive peoples of Africa [are] to the industrialized nations of today.” “Nuclear = colonizer. Non-nuclear = colonized. Africa remained the eternal site of, and metonym for, backwardness.” pp. 322–323

This mapping extended the “new imperialism”: colonial war assumed different moral rules for “savages”. Aerial bombing followed machine guns: first near Tripoli (1911) and Morocco (1913); the RAF over Baghdad (1923); the French over Damascus (1925). In a Pacific war “with virulent racial overtones”, several hundred thousand Japanese became the first victims of the “white race's superweapon” fn 7. As the Atomic Bomb Casualty Commission built “colonial scientific structures” to study the aftermath fn 8 (Lindee), the US and Britain scoured African colonies to monopolise uranium p. 323.

II. Atoms for Peace, the Board, and “source materials”

pp. 323–325 · power plants as a new nuclearity · Nehru and India's formula · South Africa's “source materials” · uranium's erosion · technopolitical geography

In 1953 Atoms for Peace signalled “an emerging new form of nuclearity: atomic power plants”. As it became the IAEA, it left room for postcolonial leaders to challenge the West's geography of nuclearity. India came first: Nehru made nuclear development “a fundamental building block of Indian national identity”. Indian delegates warned that a Board based only on technical achievement and East–West balance would “reproduce immoral global imbalances”, and proposed combining advancement with regional distribution p. 323.

India succeeded: five seats for the “most advanced in the technology of atomic energy including the production of source materials”, five by region fn 9 (the same ten states as in Reading 5), two rotating producer seats (Eastern and Western blocs), one for “suppliers of technical assistance”, ten elected across eight regions. “Advancement” made Cold War rankings “a structural feature”, but “the regional framework accommodated – even encouraged – post-colonial fantasies of nuclear nationalism.” pp. 323–324

What made a nation “most advanced … including the production of source materials”? These phrases “laid the foundation for the global nuclear order” and their meanings have been renegotiated in treaties, contracts and practices ever since p. 324.

South Africa's delegate put “source materials” into the Statute. By 1956 US and British contracts made uranium vital to its economy fn 10 (the uranium lay in the gold mines, and sales saved many from collapse and channelled foreign investment). Being “the very antithesis of the postcolonial settlement pursued by India”, it could win a seat only by “asserting a depoliticized, technical vision of nuclearity”. In 1954–56 its nuclearity was just uranium plus a tiny research programme, a weak claim since uranium's nuclearity was “in flux” p. 324.

The old narrative: uranium the only natural bomb fuel; bombs able to rupture “the globe itself”; uranium rare, so the West must monopolise it (“Nothing mattered more”); secrecy, which “in turn reinforced the ore's nuclearity”, uranium being “the only ore ever subject to legislation specifically targeted at ensuring the secrecy of its conditions of production.” By the mid-1950s lower grades were found to be “everywhere” and the Soviets had their own; the challenge was processing, not finding p. 324.

Signs of erosion: producers rotated seats; India tried to relegate South Africa and Australia to “producers”. South Africa, backed by its customers the US and Britain, insisted “source materials” count as advancement pp. 324–325.

Hecht's point

“In terms of technological practice, South Africa was no more ‘advanced’ in 1957 than, say, Portugal (which also mined uranium). The difference lay in technopolitical geography.” Portugal sat in Western Europe, at the pinnacle; South Africa sat in Africa/Middle East, where its rivals Israel and Egypt “carried political baggage even heavier than its own”. “In a technopolitical geography where the cold war trumped racial injustice, South Africa's uranium production could serve as the pinnacle of African nuclearity.” p. 325

III. Safeguards, the NPT, and the fall of uranium ore

pp. 325–327 · whom and what to regulate · the fuel cycle diagram · three categories · everyone's mundane motives · the NPT's “inalienable right” · Iran · 1968 and 1972

Safeguarding the order meant agreeing not only how to regulate but whom and what. Who could be trusted? Which materials, knowledges and systems were specifically nuclear, which weapons-only, which dual-use? Strongly nuclear things should get stricter controls, “but what did this mean in practice?” p. 325

“Fissionable materials” needed safeguarding, but at what point did milled, refined, converted and enriched uranium stop being “source material”? A South African scientist: definitions must be “essentially practical, rather than ‘textbook’ in nature, … legally watertight, and must take account of certain political implications.” The committee dropped India's preferred “fissionable material” for three categories: “source materials,” “special fissionable materials,” and “uranium enriched in the isotope 235 or 233” p. 326. fn 12 Plutonium, a “special fissionable material”, highly radioactive, explosive and human-made, “represented (and continues to represent) the pinnacle of nuclearity, the most exceptional of all things nuclear.”

Mundane motives on every side

The US promoted a pledge system with inspections; other sellers paid lip service; buyers were “underwhelmed”. India said regulating access would “perpetuate colonial inequalities and undermine national sovereignty”. “Neither side was completely disingenuous”, but commercial and political issues were hidden p. 326:

  • South Africa and Canada wanted no end-use controls that would disadvantage their uranium.
  • Indian experts disagreed about a bomb but wanted options open by minimising controls.
  • The US, UK and USSR refused inspections of their own installations.
  • Western Europeans accused the US and UK of seeking advantage and wanted only Euratom safeguards.
  • Many “Third World” nations saw “straightforward moves by the North to dominate the global South by writing the rule book in its own favor” fn 13.

The NPT

The 1970 NPT “expressed all of these tensions”: nuclear-weapon states pledged not to transfer weapons; non-nuclear-weapon states renounced them and accepted IAEA safeguards. Strikingly, it used human-rights and development language (Article IV):

1. Nothing in this Treaty shall be interpreted as affecting the inalienable right of all the Parties to the Treaty to develop research, production and use of nuclear energy for peaceful purposes. …

2. All the Parties … undertake to facilitate, and have the right to participate in, the fullest possible exchange of equipment, materials and scientific and technological information for the peaceful uses of nuclear energy. … especially in the territories of non-nuclear-weapon States …, with due consideration for the needs of the developing areas of the world.

NPT, Article IV (signed 1 July 1968) · pp. 326–327 · emphasis Hecht's
Hecht's view

To accommodate postcolonial morality, “the NPT essentially declared that nuclearity – of the ‘peaceful’ persuasion – was a fundamental right. It thus codified nuclear exceptionalism”: no other agreement calls any technology an “inalienable right”. The IAEA implemented it through “technical assistance” fn 15 ($617.5 million in 1958–93; top recipients Egypt, Brazil, Thailand, Indonesia, Peru, Pakistan, Philippines, Bangladesh, South Korea, Yugoslavia). This is “the historical basis for Iran's current invocation of its ‘inalienable right’ to nuclearity”, and why European negotiators could not legally deny it outright p. 327.

Uranium ore falls from grace

The NPT left the ontology of safeguards to the IAEA, which issued five documents between 1961 and 1972, each drawing the line differently. South Africa, now with an extensive R&D programme, led producers in excluding mines and ore-processing plants. The 1968 document defined a “principal nuclear facility” as a reactor or processing, separation or fabrication plant “(excepting a mine or ore-processing plant)”; in 1972 uranium ore was excluded from “source material” too p. 327.

“By 1972, then, the nuclearity of uranium ore and yellowcake had plummeted.” NPT parties reported yellowcake exports, but this triggered no safeguards; no one tracked the shipments; mines and mills escaped inspection p. 327.

IV. Miners, doses, and depleted uranium

pp. 327–329 · the AEC and radon · RECA and Navajo miners · South Africa until 1999 · permissible dose refuted · the DU debate · the nuclear “renaissance”

Mines that were “not nuclear”

Until the late 1960s the US AEC refused to monitor uranium mines and mills, arguing in essence that “mines were not really nuclear workplaces”. A 1957 Public Health Service report found high radon in several Colorado Plateau mines; exposure limits came only in 1967. The 1990 Radiation Exposure Compensation Act (RECA) followed a decade-long campaign for Navajo miners; its payments, not limited to Navajos, were “compassionate payments” to miners and downwinders who had “sacrificed their health to the Cold War”, a “peculiarly American admixture of nuclear exceptionalism and colonial politics” pp. 327–328.

In South Africa uranium did not count as a nuclear material for health regulation until 1999. Mines had no special radiological protections despite 1950s–70s studies of very high radon. The ten-year fight to bring them under the National Nuclear Regulator met “fierce resistance” to classifying any mine as “nuclear”. Using apartheid-era influx and labour records, operators argued black miners were so transient that none could have received a dangerous dose. A radiation protection expert Hecht interviewed estimates thousands of miners multiplied their cancer risk “from two-fold to over ten-fold” p. 328.

The permissible dose

The main border of nuclearity for health was the permissible dose: below a threshold, effects are negligible. It now “seems conclusively refuted”. A National Research Council report of June 2005 found “no threshold of exposure below which low levels of ionizing radiation can be demonstrated to be harmless or beneficial.” p. 328

Depleted uranium

This will shape the fight over depleted uranium (DU) munitions, first used against Iraqi tanks in 1991 and since in the Balkans, Afghanistan and Iraq. Some scientists, activists and veterans stress DU's nuclearity (“dirty bombs”, “weapons of mass destruction”, even “nuclear weapons”) through its radioactivity, toxicity and effects on civilians. Activists coined “atomicity” for the number of radioactive particles released: “800 tons of DU is the atomicity equivalent of 83,000 Nagasaki bombs.” Reports cite leukaemia in Basra and birth defects among Afghan children and veterans' babies. The US and UK governments, the IAEA and the WHO downplay DU's nuclearity and deny proven causality, saying doses fall within permissible ranges pp. 328–329.

Middle-ground critics call “atomicity” meaningless, with “no regard for the type of radiation present, its relative biological impact, method of dispersal, etc.”, yet demand data, saying the Pentagon failed to research DU's health effects and at times suppressed evidence. The outcome “will have major effects on those caught in (nuclear?) battle in the future” p. 329.

Hecht's close

Exceptionalism's political flexibility entrenched it “on the left and the right, in the North and the South”, but “the ontologies of nuclearity are ever-shifting”. Global warming, oil fears (“why we're really in Iraq”) and Bush's ties to the “nucular” industry are turning nuclear power “from ecological Satan to planetary savior”; even James Lovelock agrees. South Africa is building a “so-called ‘inherently safe’ pebble bed reactor” with China, which plans a five-fold increase by 2020; Iran says its enrichment only secures electricity and independence. “If nuclear power experiences the renaissance its promoters so deeply desire, what scheme will shape who gets to develop it, who only gets to buy it, and who doesn't get it at all? What will determine which technologies and materials are ‘safe’? And safe for or from whom?” Anti-DU activists “may not have the right answers, but they're asking the right questions.” From Iran to North Korea, “and yes, even in Niger – the stakes of nuclear exceptionalism remain high” p. 329.

The Power of Nuclear Things

Gabrielle Hecht on three genealogies of “uranium from Africa”

The essay in one breath

“Uranium from Africa” helped sell the 2003 Iraq war because it played on three fears: that acquiring uranium means building a bomb; that Africa is dark and unknowable; and that the line between licit and illicit nuclear trade is clear. Hecht offers “three genealogies”. First, when uranium counts as nuclear. South Africa made ore “advanced” enough for an IAEA seat in 1956, but safeguards, trigger lists and market needs later stripped ore and yellowcake of nuclearity. That stripping made “the uranium market” possible. Second, the real “route de l'uranium” in Niger. President Diori argued uranium's nuclearity should raise its price; France answered with “the banality of the market”. After his fall, Niger sold on its own to Libya, Iraq and Pakistan, outside NPT norms. Third, Namibia's Rössing mine. Under UN Decree No. 1, front companies, “flag swaps” and the “UF6 loophole” hid the uranium's provenance until independence in 1990. Technopolitics, “the distribution of power in material things and symbolic circulations”, “make some things nuclear, some things commodities, some things African, and some things all three” p. 3. Understanding the power of nuclear things demands “a transnational approach fully grounded in local and regional histories” p. 29.

Opening: “uranium from Africa”

pp. 1–3 · untitled in the source · Bush's 16 words · three fears · three genealogies · method

On 28 January 2003 Bush told the State of the Union that “the British government has learned that Saddam Hussein recently sought significant quantities of uranium from Africa.” Inspectors had found a clandestine programme in 1991; Rice had warned “we don't want the smoking gun to be a mushroom cloud”. The aluminium tubes evidence gained “only modest traction” and was disputed; recent inspections found nothing. “The case for war was not going well.” “Uranium from Africa” “sounded much scarier than ‘aluminum tubes’” and could be fleshed out into 500 tons of “yellowcake from Niger”; crediting the British sidelined disputes among US agencies pp. 1–2.

In early March IAEA experts took “only … a few hours” to find the documents were forgeries. Too late: on 19 March 2003 the US attacked Iraq. There was no uranium deal and no restarted weapons programme p. 2. fn 3 Colin Powell's February 2003 UN presentation omitted the uranium claim, reportedly finding it unconvincing.

Hecht's diagnosis

The phrase traded on three sets of fears and assumptions p. 2:

  • fear of nuclear weapons, assuming “uranium” means a bomb;
  • fear of “Africa” as “a dark, corrupt continent”, whose actions are “ultimately unknowable”;
  • fear of nuclear materials outside Western control, assuming “the difference between licit and illicit nuclear trade is clear-cut.”

Commentators wrote much on the first, little on the second, a bit on the third, and missed the threads binding them.

Hence three genealogies: (1) when uranium counts as nuclear (mined, made into yellowcake, converted to hexafluoride, enriched, made into fuel), which “has depended on time, place, purpose, and markets”; (2) the history of “yellowcake from Niger”, a nation “with its own politics, priorities, and conflicts”; (3) Namibian uranium flowing to the US, Japan and Europe under anti-apartheid sanctions, where “licit trade and black markets were materially entwined in ways that made African things invisible” pp. 2–3.

On method: only “a bird's-eye view” shows the patterns of transnational networks, but it is “always partial” and risks the “dangerous illusion” that some places don't matter. “We must land in unfamiliar places and study them on their own terms.” The result is “a fractured history”, because transnational history is “uneven and disjointed”. Hecht uses technopolitics more broadly than in The Radiance of France: “the distribution of power in material things and symbolic circulations”, hybrid powers that “make some things nuclear, some things commodities, some things African, and some things all three.” p. 3

Is Uranium a Nuclear Thing?

pp. 3–9 · exceptionalism made and remade · OTA 1995 · the 1956 Board · South Africa's sales tours · safeguards · NPT · trigger lists · the uranium market · Additional Protocol

Exceptionalism, made and remade

Since Hiroshima, cold warriors and activists portrayed atomic weapons as unique; “the bomb” was the trump card “first for the superpowers, then for waning colonial powers, then for other nations”. Its “technopolitical qualities made it particularly robust”; fission's “rupture in nature's very building blocks propelled claims to a corresponding rupture in historical space and time” p. 3.

“Nuclear exceptionalism could be made, unmade, and remade.” Experts made exceptionalist claims first; anti-nuclear groups made their own about unprecedented dangers; the industry replied by making itself mundane (“radioactivity was part of nature”). After Three Mile Island (1979) and Chernobyl (1986), experts rebranded it: the industry spent more to prevent occupational deaths than any other. Morality-talk made nuclear things “either sacred or profane”, and all sides assumed “an immutable ontology” based on fission and radioactivity p. 4. fn 6 This draws on “Nuclear Ontologies” (Reading 6).

“Yellowcake from Niger made Iraq nuclear enough to justify war in 2003. But in 1995, yellowcake didn't suffice to make Niger itself nuclear.” The 1995 OTA report listed no “nuclear activities” for Niger, Gabon or Namibia, a quarter of world output p. 4.

The IAEA seat and the uranium market

The 1956 Statute's Board formula (five global “most advanced”, five regional, two producer seats, one technical-assistance seat, the rest elected) was the first attempt to define a global nuclear order. The South African delegate pushed “source materials” into it. In 1956 its “nuclear” activity was ore plus a very small research programme, equivalent to Portugal or Australia; Israel and Egypt had better research. The Suez Crisis heightened the risk (for the US and UK) of letting either represent the region, and if “source materials” counted, South Africa qualified easily, “not the least because the other African producers were not (yet) nations.” It won its seat pp. 4–5.

South Africa wanted a seat to shape a uranium market. Today the IAEA is the UN's “nuclear watchdog”, but that function “emerged historically”; the agency was built “to facilitate the circulation of nuclear things” p. 5. In 1959 the delegate escorted two Atomic Energy Board representatives around Western Europe as a “sales survey team” forecasting supply, demand, prices and how safeguards might constrain sales; the tour was repeated regularly p. 6. Members used the IAEA to learn about technologies, make commercial contacts, seek aid (which meant buying and selling), “and yes, as part of all this”, discuss rules.

Safeguards and the NPT

“The problem with the trade in nuclear things was the exceptionalism of things nuclear.” Safeguards sounded like an answer. The US pushed pledges and inspections; buyers rejected controls; India said they perpetuated colonial inequality. Mundane interests underneath: South Africa feared controls would disadvantage it, and suspected Israel broke off talks for “Rand concentrates” in 1962 because it got supplies “from France, without [the] safeguard inspection requirements”; Indian experts kept options open; the superpowers and UK refused inspections; Western Europeans feared “commercial spying” and wanted only Euratom safeguards; the Third World saw Northern domination pp. 6–7.

The 1968 NPT “expressed (but did not resolve)” these tensions: weapon states would not transfer weapons and would make “good faith” efforts to reduce arsenals; others renounced weapons and accepted IAEA measures. It “tried to permanently fix which states had nuclear weapons and which didn't”, invoking human rights and development: an “inalienable right” to peaceful nuclear energy and “due consideration for the needs of the developing areas of the world” p. 7. fn 16 Hugh Gusterson calls the NPT's worldview “nuclear orientalism”.

Stripping ore of nuclearity

The IAEA ran technical assistance and a shifting safeguards system. South Africa pushed to exclude mines and ore plants: the 1968 document excluded “a mine or ore-processing plant” from “principal nuclear facility”; the 1972 document excluded ore from “source material” pp. 7–8. fn 18 INFCIRC/153, Article 112: “The term source material shall not be interpreted as applying to ore or ore residue.”

Inspections “themselves served to define nuclearity”. In 1971 a committee fn 19 (the Zangger committee, 15 supplier states) drafted a list of things nuclear enough to trigger safeguards; the first trigger list (1974) included reactors, fuel fabrication and reprocessing plants, and isotope separation equipment. Competing lists grew fn 20 (INFCIRC/209 and /254, synchronised in 1977). Was ore “source material”? “It depended on the IAEA document.” Was yellowcake “natural uranium” for export? Unclear. Safeguards on uranium sales were “merely … lines in a contract, paper promises”; ore and yellowcake triggered no inspections p. 8.

Hecht's argument

The regime also served “as a technopolitical frame for global trade”, separating things that could be sold from those that could not. “Limiting safeguards on uranium sales to lines in a contract represented an accommodation between the exceptionalism of nuclearity and the banality of commerce.” That accommodation made “the uranium market” possible “as both concept and object”. In the 1940s–50s the US and UK, the only big buyers, had imposed cost-plus pricing and secret terms: “Cold war ideology had thus placed uranium beyond ‘the market.’” Only once safeguards became end-use pledges in contracts could yellowcake be produced “under the mundane conditions that obtained for any other commodity”; producers paid penalties for off-spec chemistry, “but no one followed the ore from its site of production to its final destination” pp. 8–9.

This held until the mid-1990s. After inspections missed secret Iraqi and North Korean programmes, the Additional Protocol (1997) sought to detect undeclared activity and for the first time included uranium mines and mills in inspections. It was voluntary, but promised a “definitive verdict” that aided “global security—and market stability” p. 9. fn 26 By May 2006, 107 nations had signed, 75 in force. It “restored (at least ontologically) some of uranium's lost nuclearity”. So did the Bush administration, which rejected the IAEA's 2002 verdict and displaced Iraqi nuclearity “back into the failed 1990 inspections, and over onto ‘uranium from Africa’” p. 9.

Yellowcake from Niger

The real yellowcake road

Critics turned the claim into “yellowcake from Niger” and the headline “Follow the Yellowcake Road!” Like Oz's road to an Emerald City “sustained by illusion”, it led to forged documents. But as traced by US media it “led only to American destinations”, “parochial and unidirectional” p. 10.

Niger has an actual yellowcake road, the route de l'uranium: over 600 km, the country's northernmost paved road, opened in 1981. Yellowcake travels from the mining town of Arlit through Agadez to Tahoua, then south into Benin to the port of Cotonou and by ship to French conversion and enrichment plants. The road also revived Agadez for trans-Saharan trade and tourism p. 10.

French nuclear exceptionalism goes to Africa

Nuclear technology remade French identity, and nationalism and colonialism “went hand-in-hand”. The CEA's founders saw France “couldn't be fully nuclear without its colonies”; from 1947 prospectors found more than imagined, especially in Madagascar, Gabon and Niger pp. 10–11.

Geologists saw “a great colonial adventure”, which the CEA could not allow, especially after decolonization. A 1963 booklet for “Europeans likely to leave for Africa or Madagascar” said adventure was still possible, “but not adventure with a capital A”; “Bluffing might work in Paris; it will not stand up to the climate of the tropics.” Agents must respect independent states' laws and be “sufficiently non-conformist” to learn “the true face of the African”; Africans were “not inferior to Europeans … just less developed”; “remember that the CEA and France are often judged through you” p. 11.

This conjugated the colonial past into a nuclear future, but the future “only seemed nuclear and exceptional to CEA employees from metropolitan France”. Malagasies, Gabonese and Nigériens working with uranium “barely thought about the nuclearity of their work”; for them uranium continued the mundane practices of colonial mining, “its horrifying accidents; corporate capital and its modes of discipline.” p. 11

Diori bargains

The Africans who most linked their rocks to France's nuclearity were heads of state, especially Hamani Diori, Niger's first president (August 1960). Delineating desert reserves took far longer than in Gabon (production five years after discovery). He bargained hard: Somaïr (1968), with Niger holding 20% (financed by France); then Cominak, with Niger insisting on 32% p. 12. fn 33–34 Niger's stake in Somaïr rose above 35% by 1988; Cominak was formed in June 1974, after Diori's ouster, with the CEA 34%, Japan's OURD 25%, Spain's ENUSA 10%.

He watched France's “war of the systems” over reactor design and grasped how national exceptionalism saturated French nuclear dreams: “the French wanted to translate nuclear exceptionalism into fiscal exemptions” while Diori sought revenue. A 1961 defence treaty gave France priority access to uranium, so Diori insisted on state-to-state talks with de Gaulle and Pompidou; in 1968 de Gaulle created the Commission franco-nigérienne de l'uranium (CFNU) pp. 12–13.

Does the uranium market exist?

The CFNU discussed “price, value, and market”, but no one knew what the uranium market was; “many in the industry wondered whether it existed at all”. At the 1975 World Nuclear Fuel Market conference, a French report asked whether uranium was “like cattle, grain, and soy, a banal commodity”; participants doubted it: “How can one … envisage a futures market when there isn't even a real market for cash transactions?” p. 13

The market was performed by widely distributed, mundane things: contracts; OECD estimates of “free world” reserves; the broker Nuexco, publishing a “spot price” from 1968; a short-lived early-1970s cartel of South Africa, Canada, France and Rio Tinto Zinc. (A yellowcake futures market came only in 2007; two years later Lehman Brothers' bankruptcy left it holding half a million pounds of yellowcake, “enough … to build a nuclear bomb”.) Most sales were secret long-term contracts whose prices reflected investment, diplomacy, quantity and term, and “had almost nothing to do with” the spot price. Yet the spot price was “the only published price”, a “technopolitical referent” for fantasies, forecasts and investors p. 14.

A poor country's president questioned the split of profits, especially after the 1970 drought and famine, OPEC's 1973 oil price rise, and France's 1973 nuclear expansion, which brought French rural communes bonuses “staggering” beside Niger's budget. Inspired by OPEC, Diori called France, Niger and Gabon together to negotiate price and profits p. 14.

Exceptionalism as a price

Diori argued “the Nigérien thesis on the unusual character of uranium”: “the content of uranium transcended commercialism.” A CEA director admitted in early 1974 that “over the next decade, Nigérien uranium was indispensable to the French energy boom.” If Niger could contribute to France's exceptional nuclearity, France could make exceptional contributions to Niger's development (Gabon agreed, with less at stake since oil paid most of its bills) p. 15.

“In response, the French delegation attempted to de-nuclearize uranium by making claims about the banality of the market.” CEA executives who doubted the market in Europe insisted in Niamey that uranium's value came only from treating it as an ordinary commodity, promising revenue if pegged to international “re-valuation”. To Nigériens this sounded “hollow and condescending”. The Africans replied that beyond the calculable lay “the economic independence of France, the guaranteed satisfaction of its energy needs, a substantial savings in foreign currency and the reinforcement of the franc zone, and finally the solidarity of the three countries which could together represent 15% of the world uranium market” pp. 15–16. fn 43 On “calculability”, see Callon and Timothy Mitchell.

Coup, and a market of its own

Talks stalled. In April 1974 Diori was overthrown by Lieutenant-Colonel Seyni Kountché. French troops did not help (as they had for Léon M'Ba in Gabon in 1964); rumours say France was glad, or that Pompidou's death two weeks earlier made a rescue impossible. Opponents called Diori's stand “demagogic declarations aimed at international opinion” and accused him of pandering to French capital and neglecting miners p. 16.

Kountché won the right for Niger to sell directly a share of output equal to its capital stake. Customers reportedly included pp. 16–17:

  • Libya: perhaps 1,200 tons in the early-to-mid 1970s, between signing (1968) and ratifying (1975) the NPT; maybe 1,500 more in 1980–81. fn 47 Qaddafi, 1974: “Atomic weapons will be like traditional ones. … And we in Libya will have our share.”
  • Iraq: about 300 tons in 1981 fn 49 (“NOT the transaction” Bush meant).
  • Pakistan: about 500 tons in 1979, mostly routed secretly through Libya; perhaps more later.

This market had a technopolitical geography the West came to call a black market. Niger, like France, did not join the NPT until 1992; local and regional issues mattered more than superpower politics. Kountché threatened in January 1981 to cut off Qaddafi over Chad, then reportedly said: “if the devil asks me to sell him uranium today, I will sell it to him.” France, meanwhile, could deny involvement in sales to Libya and Pakistan because each shareholder controlled its own share. “Allowing this wide range of political tensions to fester enabled the mines themselves to thrive.” p. 17

When the spot price fell in 1981, Niger, burdened by loans secured on projected sales, could not stockpile. Cogéma (created 1977 from the CEA's fuel cycle arm) stepped in; its chairman said “the best support for Niger is to not give them artificial prices.” Later arrangements left Cogéma marketing Niger's uranium, which is why Joseph Wilson concluded no recent sales had occurred pp. 17–18.

Niger today

The spot price soared in 2005–07. In 2007 President Mamadou Tandja demanded higher prices and some output to sell himself; Areva (Cogéma's successor) agreed, and in January 2009 won the Imouraren licence, meant to make Niger the second-largest producer. Niger also partnered with Chinese and Canadian firms p. 18. fn 55 Niger's first independent sale under the new regime went to an American utility. Areva spans the whole cycle (installations in 40 countries); the French state is its main shareholder.

The Tuareg rebels of the Mouvement des Nigériens pour la Justice (MNJ) demanded uranium revenue and jobs. Their November 2007 blog post “La route de l'uranium” forbade traffic: “AREVA, From now on, all traffic on the uranium axis is forbidden! … Will no longer let Areva pillage our Country”. They raided outposts and briefly kidnapped four Areva employees; Tandja called them “terrorists and bandits” pp. 18–19.

Some Somaïr and Cominak workers now think of their work “as a specifically nuclear activity”; French legal and anti-nuclear NGOs (Sherpa, CRIIRAD) found exposures above international norms and in March 2007 threatened Areva with a lawsuit p. 19.

Nigériens answered Bush too. A civil society group called for a libel suit; writer and activist Moustapha Kadi (February 2007) demanded “at least 1000 billion CFA francs (about 2 billion dollars)” in compensation for a lie “which destroyed the image and credibility of our country”, lamenting that Nigérien leaders “kept their traps shut” pp. 19–20. Like presidents, rebels and miners, Kadi marshalled “the political promises of nuclearity”, here his country's “nuclear innocence” p. 20.

The yellowcake road showed nuclear things “embedded in extreme imbalances of geopolitical power and wealth”, claimed as exceptional or mundane by context, and “the tenuous border between licit trade and black markets” p. 20.

The Technopolitics of Provenance

pp. 20–28 · Rössing · Decree No. 1 · activists · Minserve · flag swaps · rand profits · the CAAA and the UF6 loophole · Urenco · independence

Rössing and Decree No. 1

American journalists “doubtless felt pleased with their witty injunction”, but “the wordplay wasn't new in 2003”. “Follow the Yellowcake Road” headlined a 1980 British documentary about the Rössing mine in Namibia, part of a transnational effort to persuade Western governments and Japan to stop buying uranium from apartheid states. For the Namibian liberation struggle and its European allies, such an embargo was key to ending South Africa's colonial occupation. Here it was “the provenance of uranium that underwent technopolitical transformations and redistributions (more than—though never divorced from—its nuclearity or its markets)” p. 20.

Rössing, a subsidiary of Rio Tinto Zinc (RTZ), began exporting in 1976. Investors included the French-based Total Compagnie Minière, the Atomic Energy Organization of Iran (AEOI), and South Africa's Industrial Development Corporation (IDC), which held most voting shares. RTZ had long-term contracts with German and Japanese utilities and the UKAEA; by the late 1970s Rössing had the world's largest uranium open pit pp. 20–21. fn 61 Hecht had unrestricted access to Rössing's uncatalogued archives in 2004.

On many readings it was illegal from the start. South Africa took South West Africa from Germany after WWI and refused UN trusteeship after WWII, tightening control after 1948. 1966: the UN ended its mandate, created the UN Council for Namibia (UNCN) and demanded withdrawal; 1971: the ICJ ruled these measures binding; 1973: the General Assembly recognised SWAPO as the “sole authentic representative” p. 21. fn 62–63 SWAPO took up arms in 1966; UN recognition also strengthened it over rival groups; a British official called the UNCN “an extremist body”; Nico Schrijver's 1984 report to the UNCN noted only four of its 31 members were Western (Australia, Belgium, Finland, Turkey). In September 1974 the UNCN's Decree No. 1 banned extracting or exporting Namibian resources without its permission, allowed seizure, and warned of damages. Rössing was its main target p. 22.

Many Western governments rejected the decree, but the “bottom line” was that Rössing aimed to supply at least 10% of the Western uranium market: one-third of Britain's needs, probably more for Japan. A fifteen-year struggle followed p. 22.

Activists

From 1975 UNCN delegations lobbied governments; before the mid-1980s only Sweden pledged a boycott. Activists joined the anti-nuclear movement in the UK and the Netherlands (Britain's CANUC, the Campaign Against the Namibian Uranium Contracts). A week-long UNCN hearing in July 1980 fn 65 (which screened the documentary) argued that any purchase supported occupation through Rössing's taxes. An economist noted southern Africa “could account for as much as 50 per cent of the total … available for net export” and that IDC control gave Pretoria leverage over foreign policy pp. 22–23. fn 66 UN Security Council Resolution 418 (1977) barred cooperation with South Africa on nuclear weapons. SWAPO's 1981 trade union seminar exposed European transport workers who had “unknowingly handled barrels of radioactive substances”; a 1982 American Committee on Africa seminar followed; CANUC courted the peace movement p. 23.

Fronts and flag swaps

Customers held firm into the mid-1980s, helped by Rössing “papering over the transnational dimensions”. To meet “the unwillingness of certain customers to deal direct with a SWA company”, RTZ set up a Swiss front, RTZ Mineral Services (Minserve), which signed contracts not naming Rössing and “back-to-back” contracts with the mine. Customers were referred to by number; board members stayed officially ignorant of customers and prices; Minserve's market reports ignored anti-apartheid pressure until late 1985 pp. 23–24.

Japanese utilities began asking for non-Namibian material. In 1983 one customer said: “Politically [they] cannot buy Namibian material but they are willing to discuss taking swapped material in the form of spot deliveries of UF6”, ideally via a third party. Minserve's role was “an open secret” p. 24. Then came “flag swaps”:

  • Converters Comurhex (France) and BNFL (Britain) relabelled Rössing hexafluoride as French or British on customs forms for US enrichment plants.
  • Minserve swapped contracts with other RTZ customers; Swiss utilities “usually obliged happily”.
  • Two converters shuffled titles to oxide and hexafluoride pp. 24–25.

Swapped quantities rose from a few hundred tons (1982) to several thousand (1985–86) fn 76 (swap fees 70 cents to $2 per pound) p. 25.

At first illicitness paid: contracts were in dollars, costs in rand, and as anti-apartheid pressure sank the rand, Rössing's 1985 profit hit a record, over 190 million rand after tax, despite a weak market. But by 1985 even apartheid's allies discussed sanctions. Rössing could dodge supply restrictions via agents, offshore accounts fn 79 (Barclays proposed one in 1987) and fronts; the real danger was import bans, since customers “might welcome an excuse to renege”. Minserve: “uranium is the easiest material for the authorities to trace and block. Without the assistance of the converter or the falsification of origin records … sales … will be severely curtailed”; the task was “damage limitation” pp. 25–26.

The UF6 loophole

In October 1986 Congress overrode Reagan's veto of the Comprehensive Anti-Apartheid Act (CAAA), barring southern African material, a list “keyed to matters of provenance rather than nuclearity”. Much Rössing yellowcake was converted or enriched in the US. Minserve hired Wrightmon USA for $15,000 a month fn 82 (disguised as “consultants”, since lobbyists must disclose clients, and Pretoria had asked Rössing to “do nothing in Washington”) p. 26.

Its president, Diane Harmon, allied with US converters while exploiting a loophole against them: if yellowcake was converted in Britain and relabelled British UF6, “hadn't its nationality changed?” She warned US enrichers could close and jobs be lost. By late 1987 she won a ruling that South African ore “substantially transformed into another form of uranium” elsewhere “is not to be treated as South African” and was not barred: the “UF6 loophole”. Minserve asked RTZ chairman Sir Alistair Frame to “have a word” with BNFL and the Foreign Office to keep relabelling pp. 26–27.

In the Netherlands the UNCN sued Urenco and the Dutch government in The Hague for enriching Namibian uranium at Almelo. Urenco replied that converters mixed Rössing yellowcake with other material, making it “technologically impossible” to tell which feed had been Namibian p. 27.

In 1988 Democrats moved to close the loophole, and the State Department declared that flag or obligation swaps could not evade the CAAA fn 87 (it would be “a fraudulent declaration”). “Nevertheless, the technopolitics of provenance had worked well enough”: delay sufficed. The independence accord came in December 1988; the UNCN dropped its suit; transition began April 1989, elections in November; in March 1990 Namibia became independent, “and Rössing uranium became legitimate.” pp. 27–28

Hecht's verdict

“When does licit trade become black (or gray) marketeering? The answer depends on provenance: not just of the material being traded, but also of the traders and those who monitor them.” Rössing and its partners never saw themselves as black marketeers; for activists the trade was “born illicit”. Transport, conversion and enrichment “increasingly served to conceal its origins”; the market worked to lend legitimacy while activists exposed its technopolitics. Power imbalances, between multinationals and freedom fighters, between embattled international bodies and rich states, shaped “the resulting boundaries between licit trade and illicit flows” p. 28.

“Uranium from Africa”, and coda

pp. 28–30 · exceptionalism vs banality · France's risky business · provenance and erasure · a transnational method · the Senate report

Uranium oscillated “between apocalypse-inducing rock and tradeable commodity”. In 1957 ore was nuclear enough to give apartheid South Africa a central IAEA role; a decade later the Western industry found markets worked better if mines and yellowcake plants lost nuclear status for safeguards. “Nuclearity no longer inhered in raw materials.” pp. 28–29

Hecht's conclusion

France could therefore answer Niger's exceptionalism with market terms, and the imbalance of power gave it the upper hand. That same imbalance pushed Niger into sales that “seriously undermined (the capitalist world's view of)” the NPT. “In Niger, Western nuclear exceptionalism slammed into postcolonial capitalism, regional politics, and the horrible mundanity of poverty.” Nigérien yellowcake may not have gone to Iraq in 2002, but it did in 1981; “chances are overwhelming” it ended up in Pakistan's 1998 bomb and would have fuelled a Libyan one. “France's refusal to include nuclear exceptionalism in the ‘market’ value of African uranium turned out to be risky business.” p. 29

Namibian yellowcake was central to the Cold War capitalist market, keeping plants in business and fuelling reactors and bombs; threatened with illicitness, producers enlisted these allies. Here provenance was reconfigured, and “the technopolitics of provenance not only served to materially intertwine licit trade and black markets; they also enacted a profound symbolic erasure of African things from Western nuclear systems.”

Understanding “the power of nuclear things” requires the many histories of uranium from Africa, and any analysis of technologies claiming global power “demands a transnational approach fully grounded in local and regional histories, however fractured or fragmented” p. 29.

Coda

The 2004 Senate Intelligence report found that uranium “was not key” to the case; the key judgments came from a CIA paper that “only highlighted the acquisition of aluminum tubes”. The tubes worried CIA experts though others found the evidence weak; the uranium evidence “had seemed flimsy to everyone”. So why did the weakest, actually fabricated, evidence play the starring role? “‘Aluminum tubes from an undisclosed location’ just didn't have the same power as ‘uranium from Africa.’” p. 30

The course lens, answered

Whose order, whose justice? Eisenhower offers an order of shared benefit that leaves US superiority intact (Krige: “maintain U.S. nuclear superiority”). Roehrlich shows developing states demanding justice inside that order: Board seats, no discrimination, access. Hecht shows whose voices the order discounted: postcolonial nations were treated as having “less right to weigh in … by the simple virtue of being less nuclear”, and African miners' health was not even counted.

Which actors? Superpowers and diplomats (Eisenhower, Dulles, Molotov, Wadsworth, Zarubin); the UN Secretariat (Hammarskjöld, Bunche, Walser); scientists as negotiators and spies (Rabi, Skobeltzyn, Veksler); uranium producers and apartheid diplomats (Sole, du Plessis, Fischer); postcolonial leaders (Bhabha, Diori, Kountché); corporations (RTZ, Minserve, Areva); activists, rebels and miners (CANUC, SWAPO, MNJ, Navajo and black South African miners).

What makes something “nuclear”? This is the week that names the question. For Eisenhower the civil/military line is simply assumed. Roehrlich and Krige show it blurring (dual use, sharing as watching). Hecht shows nuclearity negotiated in definitions (source vs special fissionable material), thresholds (ten tons), trigger lists and markets. Ore was nuclear in 1956, not in 1972, nuclear again in 1997 and for the 2003 war.

The historiographical move. Roehrlich: against a historiography that forgot the Washington talks, and against reading the IAEA only as a nonproliferation body. Krige: against historians of science who ignored Atoms for Peace. Hecht: against Cold War-only IR accounts and Fischer's and Scheinman's “politicization” story.

Retrieval practice

Self-test

Twelve questions per reading. Answer out loud or on paper before revealing. If you miss one, reread that section.

Q1 · OccasionEisenhowerWhere and when did Eisenhower speak, and who presided?

The 470th Plenary Meeting of the UN General Assembly, New York, Tuesday 8 December 1953, 2:45 p.m. Mrs Vijaya Lakshmi Pandit of India presided. He came straight from the Bermuda Conference with Britain and France (p. 1).

Q2 · New languageEisenhowerWhat “new language” does he say he must speak, and why is it awkward for him?

“The language of atomic warfare”, which he, “who have spent so much of my life in the military profession, would have preferred never to use” (p. 1).

Q3 · NumbersEisenhowerGive three facts from his recital of atomic power.

Any three: 42 US test explosions since 16 July 1945; bombs more than 25 times the first; H-weapons in millions of tons of TNT; the stockpile exceeds all WWII bombs and shells; one air group can outdo all bombs dropped on Britain; all four services can use atomic weapons (pp. 1–2).

Q4 · MonopolyEisenhowerWhat two facts follow from the end of the US monopoly?

Knowledge held by several nations “will eventually be shared by others, possibly all others”; and even vast superiority with retaliation “is no preventive” against surprise aggression (p. 2).

Q5 · ColossiEisenhowerComplete and explain: “two atomic colossi …”

“…are doomed malevolently to eye each other indefinitely across a trembling world.” He refuses this “hopeless finality”: retaliation is fact but not “the true expression of the purpose and the hopes of the United States” (p. 2).

Q6 · RecordEisenhowerWhich offers to negotiate does he list as the West’s record?

Divided Germany, an Austrian peace treaty, Korea (the UN’s request), and the four-Power meeting agreed at Bermuda after the Soviet note dropped its preconditions (p. 3).

Q7 · 715 (VIII)EisenhowerWhat did UNGA resolution 715 (VIII) suggest?

That the Disarmament Commission consider a sub-committee of the Powers principally involved to “seek in private an acceptable solution” and report by 1 September 1954 (p. 3).

Q8 · New conceptionEisenhowerWhat is the “new conception” he brings to private talks?

More than reducing military material: “It is not enough to take this weapon out of the hands of the soldiers. It must be put into the hands of those who will know how to strip its military casing and adapt it to the arts of peace” (p. 3).

Q9 · ProposalEisenhowerState the proposal itself.

The governments principally involved should, “to the extent permitted by elementary prudence”, make joint contributions from stockpiles of normal uranium and fissionable materials to an international atomic energy agency “under the aegis of the United Nations” (p. 4).

Q10 · VirtueEisenhowerWhat “great virtue” does he claim for the plan?

It can be undertaken “without the irritations and mutual suspicions incident to any attempt to set up a completely acceptable system of world-wide inspection and control” (p. 4). Safeguards came later.

Q11 · Agency tasksEisenhowerWhat would the agency do?

Impound, store and protect material in a “bank” “essentially immune to surprise seizure”; more importantly, allocate it to agriculture, medicine and above all electricity for “the power-starved areas of the world” (p. 4).

Q12 · Four aimsEisenhowerName the four aims he would put to Congress.

Encourage world-wide research on peaceful uses; begin to diminish stockpiles' destructive power; show the great Powers put “human aspirations first”; open “a new channel for peaceful discussion” (p. 4).

Q13 · ThesisRoehrlichWhat two shifts does Roehrlich say defined the IAEA negotiations?

The arms-control aspect “declined in importance”, and safeguards, “not mentioned in Eisenhower’s speech”, moved “from the margins to the center” (p. 33).

Q14 · HistoriansRoehrlichHow are historians divided on Atoms for Peace, and whom does she cite?

Propaganda vs honest threat reduction; nonproliferation tool vs spreader, “or all of the above”. Osgood: “talking peace and waging Cold War”; Chernus: “some half dozen goals” (p. 33).

Q15 · CandorRoehrlichWhat was Operation Candor, and why did it need a “positive twist”?

Jackson’s campaign, from the Oppenheimer Panel’s advice, to tell Americans about the nuclear threat. After the Soviet thermonuclear test (12 Aug 1953), the drafts left “bang bang, no hope, no way out”, so a new international organisation became the positive twist (pp. 35–36).

Q16 · X kgRoehrlichWhat did Eisenhower’s memo of 10 September 1953 propose, and why was it clever?

US and USSR each give the UN X kg of fissile material, X set at a figure the US could handle but “difficult for the Soviets to match”: arms control that would not undermine US hegemony (p. 36).

Q17 · NameRoehrlichWhen did the agency get its name, and what was it before?

On the plane back from Bermuda. The earlier draft said “International Atomic Energy Authority”, echoing the Baruch Plan’s International Atomic Development Authority (p. 38).

Q18 · Three rolesRoehrlichWhat three overlapping objectives did the speech give the agency?

Pool (arms control); bank or broker (promotion); a new channel for discussion (moral, psychological). “Nobody had put much thought into how this would look in practice” (p. 38).

Q19 · MolotovRoehrlichWhat did Molotov warn Dulles in 1954, and how did Washington react?

Peaceful atomic energy produces weapons material, so the plan would increase stocks. Dulles was “completely puzzled”; Smith said Molotov was better briefed by Soviet scientists; the administration had turned “a blind eye” (pp. 39–40).

Q20 · VerticalRoehrlichDistinguish vertical and horizontal proliferation as used in the chapter.

Vertical: growth of US and Soviet stockpiles (Molotov’s concern). Horizontal: new weapon states (what the Americans heard) (p. 40).

Q21 · EightRoehrlichWho were the eight negotiators of 1954 and why those states?

US, UK, Canada, France (principally involved), plus Australia, Belgium, Portugal, South Africa for uranium. Five were colonial powers; a “global West” (pp. 42–44).

Q22 · Geneva talksRoehrlichWhat happened at the 1955 Geneva safeguards talks?

Six governments' scientists (Rabi, Skobeltzyn) met outside UN control; the US offered a “half-baked” tracer plan; no solution, but officials found a “community of interest” in stopping new nuclear states (pp. 46–49).

Q23 · Rival blocsRoehrlichWho were the “rival blocs” at the Washington talks?

Not the superpowers: India and the uranium-producing states, above all South Africa, over apartheid and Board seats (p. 53).

Q24 · BudgetRoehrlichDescribe the two-budget system and what it reflects.

From a Canadian proposal: mandatory contributions pay the regular budget (salaries, meetings, publications); voluntary ones the operational budget (materials, projects, technical assistance, later safeguards). It reflects the dual mandate (p. 54).

Q25 · ProliferationRoehrlichWhat does Roehrlich say about the word “proliferation” at the 1956 conference?

It appears once in the records, warning of a “proliferation of paper”. Access, not denial, was the primary objective, and the Statute did not ask members to renounce the weapon option (p. 57).

Q26 · McKnightRoehrlichHow did McKnight summarise the change from Baruch to Atoms for Peace?

“A shift in emphasis from control, with the dissemination of nuclear science and technology as an adjunct, to a proposal for the promotion of peaceful uses, with control as an adjunct” (p. 60).

Q27 · ThesisKrigeState Krige’s thesis in his own image.

Openness and security, sharing and surveillance, “were two sides of the same coin”; scientific internationalism and scientific intelligence were fused (pp. 161, 167).

Q28 · Four dimensionsKrigeWhat four dimensions of Atoms for Peace does Krige study?

Distraction from the weapons build-up (propaganda); diverting others' resources to watched civil programmes; pushing US industry into nuclear power; an international scientific conference (pp. 162–165).

Q29 · Build-upKrigeWhat numbers show the weapons build-up behind the benign atom?

841 US nuclear weapons in 1952, 18,638 by 1960; NSC 162/2 (30 Oct 1953) treated them “as munitions like any other” (p. 162).

Q30 · BravoKrigeWhy did Castle Bravo make propaganda urgent?

1 March 1954, 15 Mt, 2.5× expected; fallout on islanders and the Lucky Dragon Five (23 crew, one died). Eisenhower feared the world would think “we’re skunks, saber-rattlers and warmongers” (p. 163).

Q31 · NeglectKrigeWhom does Krige criticise in the historiography?

Historians of science who ignored Atoms for Peace, studying postwar physics for winning wars and Nobel Prizes, “not for winning hearts and minds” (p. 165).

Q32 · BerknerKrigeWhat did the Berkner report’s classified appendix recommend?

Enrol qualified US scientists to gather intelligence informally through “free and open discussion” at conferences, unions and UNESCO; the Soviet Union the “prime target” (pp. 166–167).

Q33 · PanopticonKrigeWhy does Krige call Geneva 1955 a panopticon?

Exchange is “at once a window and a probe, an ideology of transparency and, by virtue of that, an instrument of control”; the conference was a site to watch others (p. 167).

Q34 · StraussKrigeGive Strauss’s objections to exporting isotopes and his two cases.

Isotopes serve nations' “war-making potential”; scientists would work for any dictatorship. He blocked P-32 to Helsinki and worried about 1 mCi of iron-59 for a Norwegian military lab with a possible Communist (p. 169).

Q35 · EuropeKrigeWhy did Europeans find the refusal “simply absurd”?

For them the atom meant modernity, energy and independence, not war; denying millicuries projected America’s meaning of the atom abroad (“bottle-washings” could fuel months of research) (pp. 169–170).

Q36 · ConditionsKrigeHow did the isotope conditions make openness a form of inspection?

Requests via the State Department; three reports every six months; publication; open visits by “qualified scientists irrespective of nationality”. “Access was an insurance against abuse” (p. 171).

Q37 · ReactorsKrigeWhy did Eisenhower’s plan share reactors, not just isotopes?

The US had lost its monopoly; reactors won new states (“industrial imperialism”, Medhurst); and they were bargaining chips for uranium, NATO nuclearisation and bases (pp. 172–173).

Q38 · Swimming poolKrigeDescribe the US reactor at Geneva and its message.

Oak Ridge swimming-pool reactor in a chalet; 10 kW; 18 kg of 20% U-235 as in the proposed pool; three control rods; visitors (and Eisenhower) operated it; 50,000 visitors; sold to Switzerland for $180,000. “A masterpiece of marketing” (p. 175).

Q39 · OutcomeKrigeHow did Geneva change American and Soviet scientists?

Americans, surprised by Soviet competence and the 10 BeV accelerator, “went home chastened” and pushed for more manpower (Price, Seitz). Soviets “went home reassured” (pp. 178–180).

Q40 · ConclusionKrigeWhat is the “barometer of nuclearity” point in Krige’s conclusion?

The US exported enriched-uranium reactors, which pushed recipients up the barometer and bound them to safeguards: sharing plus surveillance; “autonomy … interwoven with dependence” (p. 181).

Q41 · ThesisRoehrlich 2016State the 2016 article’s thesis.

The IAEA’s creation was shaped by superpower relations, decolonisation and the global South’s influence at the UN; US–Soviet understanding was good, while uranium producers and future aid recipients were rival blocs (pp. 1, 3).

Q42 · PuzzlesRoehrlich 2016What two puzzles does the article set?

Why membership was so broad (56 founders when ≤8 had reactors), and why the rivals cooperated amid Hungary and Suez (p. 2).

Q43 · SwopeRoehrlich 2016What does the article say about the origin of the term “Cold War”?

Herbert Swope, adviser to Baruch at the UNAEC in 1946, spoke of a “Cold War”; Lippmann popularised it, though Orwell used it first (fn 14) (p. 4).

Q44 · HollowayRoehrlich 2016How did South Africa’s ambassador describe US ambivalence?

If Russia joined, a way to bring East and West together; “If Russia stays out it could become an additional weapon in the cold war” (p. 5).

Q45 · PortugalRoehrlich 2016What inconsistency does Roehrlich note about Portugal and China?

The US excluded the PRC because it was not represented at the UN, yet invited Portugal, which was not a UN member until December 1955 (p. 7).

Q46 · DevelopmentRoehrlich 2016When did the IAEA become a “development agency”?

In the UN General Assembly’s First Committee debate of autumn 1954 (5–23 Nov, 19 meetings), led by Egypt, Peru and Pakistan (p. 9).

Q47 · EntryRoehrlich 2016Where, per Roehrlich, did the Soviet Union really enter the negotiations?

At the Geneva technical talks after the 1955 conference (p. 9).

Q48 · StraussRoehrlich 2016What did Strauss answer when asked whether the IAEA might help countries get weapons?

“They will in any event” (2 May 1957): parts of the administration saw proliferation as inevitable (p. 12).

Q49 · CartelRoehrlich 2016Why did the Board grow to 23, and what formula was adopted?

A board of all negotiators' permanent seats would look like “a NATO uranium cartel”. Mainly on India’s proposals: five “most advanced … including the production of source materials”, the most advanced in eight regions, the rest elected; the first IO with a specific list of regions (p. 14).

Q50 · South AfricaRoehrlich 2016How did South Africa secure a quasi-permanent seat?

By getting “production of source materials” into the “most advanced” formula; it could never win election (pp. 14–15).

Q51 · UN linkRoehrlich 2016How was the IAEA’s relation to the UN settled?

An autonomous organisation linked by a separate agreement, reporting to the General Assembly and, on peace and security, the Security Council (Art. III.B.4); US wanted a specialised agency, USSR the Security Council; Vienna underlined autonomy (p. 17).

Q52 · ColeRoehrlich 2016How did Cole become director general?

Strauss insisted on an American; the Soviets objected; Bunche brokered a private 90-minute meeting with Emelyanov, and the Soviets relented “to continue the business-like atmosphere” (pp. 17–18).

Q53 · ConclusionRoehrlich 2016What makes the IAEA’s founding unique, per the conclusion?

It was the first major international organisation whose statute developing countries shaped considerably; the global South showed “many different faces” (India, South Africa, Brazil) (pp. 19–20).

Q54 · ThesisHecht 2006State Hecht’s thesis in “Negotiating Global Nuclearities”.

The separation of technology and politics at the IAEA was “discursive and never enacted in practice”; its regime was the contested outcome of Cold War and decolonization visions of order (p. 25).

Q55 · NuclearityHecht 2006Define nuclearity as Hecht does.

“The degree to which a nation, a program, a policy, a technology, or even a material counted as ‘nuclear’”: “a spectrum, not an on-off condition” (pp. 25–26).

Q56 · AgainstHecht 2006What does she object to in Scheinman, Fischer and Forland?

Scheinman’s split of “intrinsic” and “extraneous” politics; Fischer’s dismissal of the G-77 and of “neocolonial” charges; both Fischer and Forland write “teleological narratives” toward “technical perfection” (p. 26, fn 4).

Q57 · BoardHecht 2006What did the 1956 Board formula do, in Hecht’s words?

Made Cold War rankings of “advancement” a structural feature of the IAEA, while the regional framework “conjugated that advancement into a postcolonial tense” (p. 28).

Q58 · UraniumHecht 2006Why did uranium’s nuclearity erode in the mid-1950s?

Low-grade ore turned out to be “everywhere” and the Soviets had their own; the challenge became processing, not finding (pp. 29–30).

Q59 · SoleHecht 2006What three positions did Donald Sole triangulate?

IAEA as technical not political; South Africa as both western and African; nuclear accomplishments beyond uranium (an atomic energy programme) (p. 30).

Q60 · FishingHecht 2006What did the five words “in accordance with this Statute” achieve?

Only nuclear UN resolutions could be considered by the IAEA, blocking UN “fishing expeditions” into apartheid (pp. 31–32).

Q61 · West/RestHecht 2006How did the West and the Rest differ on development?

The West wanted a narrow, technical, market-building IAEA (sell reactors and uranium); India and the Rest wanted wider distribution to avoid perpetuating inequality, with politics included (pp. 33–34).

Q62 · CairoHecht 2006How did the UAR’s Cairo centre bid affect South Africa?

Sole saw a plot to take the “most advanced” seat; South Africa created the AEB, widened the survey to all Africa, and used the questionnaire as a “barometer of nuclearity” (pp. 36–38).

Q63 · GhanaHecht 2006What did Sole do about the secret telegram on Ghana?

Pretoria said support should go no further than a vote; he claimed it was deciphered too late and co-sponsored, to avoid looking “purely political” (p. 39).

Q64 · Ten tonsHecht 2006What was the ten-ton threshold and why does it matter?

Sole won exemption from safeguards for up to ten tons of uranium below 1% U-235, against a US 2-ton line. With threats and incentives he won; it still applies (INFCIRC/66) (p. 45).

Q65 · ConclusionHecht 2006Why does Hecht reject Fischer’s and Scheinman’s account of the 1977 ouster?

They reproduce a Cold War vision in which postcolonial nations “have less right to weigh in on nuclear issues by the simple virtue of being less nuclear”; anticolonial critiques are self-interested but “genuine” (p. 47).

Q66 · NigerOntologiesWhat does Hecht say the Niger affair reveals?

“Uranium” is “as underspecified technologically as ‘Africa’ is underspecified politically”, and “nuclear state” is ambiguous (pp. 320–321).

Q67 · ExceptionalismOntologiesDefine nuclear exceptionalism and who held it.

The view that the atom is fundamentally unlike any other creation, cultivated by cold warriors and activists alike; morality-talk made it “salvation or depravity” (p. 321).

Q68 · DebrayOntologiesHow did exceptionalism survive the Cold War?

As the “clash of civilizations”: Debray’s “green has replaced red”, the “rational North” vs “mystical South”; Bush’s “axis of evil” (p. 321).

Q69 · OTAOntologiesWhat did the 1995 OTA report omit?

Gabon, Niger and Namibia, nearly 25% of world uranium output, were listed with no “nuclear activities” (p. 321).

Q70 · SpectrumOntologiesGive Hecht’s definition of nuclearity in this essay.

“A technopolitical spectrum that shifts in time and space … a historical and geographical condition, as well as a scientific and technological one” (p. 322).

Q71 · ColonizerOntologiesHow did Cherwell and Gaillard link nuclear and colonial status?

Without the bomb Britain would be like “native levies” allowed no artillery (Cherwell); non-nuclear nations would be as backward as “the primitive peoples of Africa” (Gaillard): “Nuclear = colonizer. Non-nuclear = colonized” (pp. 322–323).

Q72 · PortugalOntologiesWhy was South Africa, not Portugal, “most advanced”?

Same technology (uranium mining), different “technopolitical geography”: Portugal sat in advanced Western Europe; in Africa/Middle East its rivals Israel and Egypt had heavier baggage (p. 325).

Q73 · Fuel cycleOntologiesList the front-end fuel cycle steps in Hecht’s diagram.

Mines (ore, with tailings) → mill (yellowcake) → conversion plant (uranium hexafluoride) → enrichment plant (enriched and depleted uranium) → fuel fabrication for weapons and power plants (p. 325).

Q74 · NPTOntologiesWhat did the NPT codify, according to Hecht?

Peaceful nuclearity as an “inalienable right”, codifying exceptionalism; the basis for Iran’s claim today (pp. 326–327).

Q75 · 1972OntologiesWhat happened to uranium ore’s nuclearity by 1972?

The 1968 document excluded mines and ore plants from “principal nuclear facility”; the 1972 one excluded ore from “source material”. Yellowcake shipments went untracked (p. 327).

Q76 · MinersOntologiesHow did the ontology of nuclearity harm miners?

The US AEC refused to monitor mines until the late 1960s despite 1957 radon findings (RECA 1990 for Navajo miners); South Africa did not regulate uranium as nuclear for health until 1999; thousands may have multiplied cancer risk 2- to 10-fold (pp. 327–328).

Q77 · DUOntologiesWhat is the depleted uranium debate about?

Whether DU munitions are “nuclear”: activists' “atomicity” (800 tons = 83,000 Nagasakis) vs governments, IAEA and WHO invoking permissible doses; middle-ground critics want data. The 2005 NRC report (no safe threshold) matters (pp. 328–329).

Q78 · Three fearsHecht 2010What three fears made “uranium from Africa” powerful?

Uranium equals a bomb; Africa as dark and unknowable; clear line between licit and illicit nuclear trade (p. 2).

Q79 · TechnopoliticsHecht 2010How does Hecht use technopolitics here?

More expansively: “the distribution of power in material things and symbolic circulations”, which makes things nuclear, commodities, African, or all three (p. 3).

Q80 · Made/unmadeHecht 2010Show how exceptionalism was “made, unmade, and remade”.

Experts claimed it; activists claimed their own; the industry made itself mundane; after Three Mile Island and Chernobyl it rebranded (spending most on safety) (p. 4).

Q81 · IAEA purposeHecht 2010What was the IAEA originally for, per Hecht, and how did South Africa use it?

“To facilitate the circulation of nuclear things”; South Africa’s delegate ran uranium “sales survey” tours of Europe from 1959 (pp. 5–6).

Q82 · MarketHecht 2010How did limiting safeguards create “the uranium market”?

Safeguards on sales became “lines in a contract”, so yellowcake could be produced like any commodity: an accommodation between exceptionalism and “the banality of commerce” (pp. 8–9).

Q83 · Trigger listHecht 2010What was the trigger list and what did it leave out?

The Zangger committee’s list (1971–74) of items nuclear enough to trigger safeguards: reactors, fuel fabrication and reprocessing plants, isotope separation equipment. Ore and yellowcake did not trigger inspections (p. 8).

Q84 · RouteHecht 2010Trace the route de l’uranium.

Arlit → Agadez → Tahoua, then south through Benin to Cotonou and by ship to France; over 600 km, opened 1981 (p. 10).

Q85 · DioriHecht 2010How did Diori and France argue about uranium’s value?

Diori: uranium “transcended commercialism”; if Niger fuelled France’s exceptional nuclearity, France owed exceptional development. France “attempted to de-nuclearize uranium” by invoking “the banality of the market” (pp. 14–15).

Q86 · KountchéHecht 2010What changed under Kountché, and to whom did Niger sell?

Niger could sell its share directly: Libya (~1,200 t), Iraq (~300 t, 1981), Pakistan (~500 t, 1979). “If the devil asks me to sell him uranium today, I will sell it to him” (pp. 16–17).

Q87 · DecreeHecht 2010What was Decree No. 1 and why did it target Rössing?

The UN Council for Namibia’s 1974 ban on exploiting Namibian resources without its permission; Rössing aimed to supply 10%+ of the Western market (pp. 21–22).

Q88 · Flag swapsHecht 2010What were flag swaps?

Ways to hide Namibian origin: converters relabelling UF6 as British or French; swapping contracts with other RTZ customers; shuffling titles. Swaps grew from hundreds to thousands of tons (pp. 24–25).

Q89 · UF6 loopholeHecht 2010What was the UF6 loophole?

A 1987 US ruling, won by Wrightmon’s Diane Harmon, that South African ore “substantially transformed” elsewhere (e.g. into UK hexafluoride) was not South African under the CAAA (pp. 26–27).
