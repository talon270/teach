# Tutorial 07, Part Two: The Lickanantay of the Salar de Atacama

In April 2023, Chile announced a National Lithium Strategy to bring the Salar de Atacama under state partnership, placing the Lickanantay, or Atacameño, people at the centre of the lithium triangle. Their case tests the claim of our reflections that Latin America's future depends on alternatives to Western models of modernisation, and all five of our themes meet in it.

The Lickanantay have farmed and herded in the oases around San Pedro de Atacama for centuries, managing scarce water through communal irrigation that the Europe-facing account of Chilean culture left out. Today the communities are reviving Kunza, their dormant language, and treat the salt flat as a living territory rather than an empty deposit, making recognition a question of who may draw water from the basin.

Chile's 2022 constitutional draft would have made the country plurinational, with reserved Indigenous seats and rights over land and water, but voters rejected it by about 62 per cent. The plurinational idea therefore exists, while the nineteenth-century model of one homogeneous nation still prevails.

SQM, the main lithium producer, was privatised under Pinochet and came under the control of his son-in-law, Julio Ponce Lerou, ensuring that authoritarian rule decided who owned the salt flat while the communities had no say. Decisions on extraction still come from Santiago through state contracts, which supports our claim that the state remains centralised.

Lithium extraction pumps brine from beneath the salt flat to evaporate it, and communities report falling water tables and drying lagoons. The pattern repeats the colonial mine, with wealth leaving as raw material while the costs stay local. In 2016 the Consejo de Pueblos Atacameños secured an agreement under which Albemarle pays the communities a share of its sales and accepts environmental monitoring, but benefit-sharing still falls short of control.

The 2023 strategy joins Codelco, the state copper company, with SQM and promises consultation with Indigenous communities, presenting the choice we described between exporting raw lithium and building processing at home. Endogenous innovation would join this industrial ambition to Lickanantay water knowledge, giving the communities a real voice over extraction rates.

The Lickanantay case supports our conclusion. Cultural recognition has won the communities a voice, but without institutional reform and economic autonomy the salt flat will remain an export zone. Chile, and the region, must turn recognition into shared control over land, water and the wealth beneath them.
