## Question 1

Experts surveyed in Annex 1 rank three references, the Malvinas/Falklands, the Conquest, and the Panama Canal, among the most important for Latin American identity, with the Tahuantinsuyo as the main Indigenous one, and the annex's point that they are all "about power" is the key to reading them together. Each leads back to the European takeover of the "New World", which created both the hierarchies Quijano calls the coloniality of power and the region's subordination to outside powers, while memory has also become a way of recovering what that takeover erased. The annex treats these events as "sites of memory" and argues that societies remember to interpret the present rather than preserve the past, much like Querejazu and Tickner's account of Indigenous thought, where ancestrality is a form of sense-making because "the past holds answers to present and future problems". Remembering the Conquest is therefore less about nostalgia than explaining present inequality. The Conquest is the founding wound, since, as Mignolo points out, the "New World" was new only "for Europeans, of course", and taking land and exploiting labour "required the simultaneous ideological construction of racism". Johnson notes that pan-Africanists see the result as a structure of white supremacy that exploited Black and Indigenous people for three centuries and outlived abolition, and Lucero shows how the later slogan "we are all mestizo" became "an all-inclusive ideology of exclusion". Querejazu and Tickner tie the coloniality of power to dispossession in a region that is still the most unequal in the world in terms of land, supporting the annex's view that questions of who holds power and who defines national narratives find part of their answers in the colonial founding, whose hierarchies independence did not necessarily eliminate. The prominence of the Tahuantinsuyo shows memory pushing back against this. Mignolo argues that when America was added to the Christian world map, Tawantinsuyu and Anáhuac "lost their autonomous history", so naming the Tahuantinsuyo today, much like Indigenous movements claiming "Abya-Yala" as the continent's name, works to reverse that erasure. Lucero also recalls the Andean rebellions of 1780–81 led by Túpak Amaru, Micaela Bastides, Túpaj Katari, and Bartolina Sisa, and Katari's possibly apocryphal dying promise to return "made into millions" later rallied the Kataristas in Bolivia. However, this memory is not innocent, because Peruvian elites have taken over Inca legacies in a way that empties Indianness of its subversive potential, summed up as "Incas yes, Indians no", and an expert survey may well capture this elite version rather than living Indigenous voices. Meanwhile, the Malvinas show that European control did not end with independence. Tulchin notes that the Argentines had beaten back a short-lived British invasion before independence, and the islands, still disputed with Britain, now stand for what the annex calls "incomplete sovereignty". Russell adds that the failure of the Organization of American States and the Inter-American Treaty of Reciprocal Assistance to halt the 1982 war revived suspicion of hemispheric institutions, so the Malvinas carry two memories, European colonial presence and the realisation that these institutions would not defend Latin American sovereignty against a European power. Panama shows the same logic passing to a new external power, since the isthmus recalls Bolívar's Congress of 1826, whose dream Tulchin calls "a powerful concept" across the region, but also Theodore Roosevelt dealing with the unelected leaders of the Panamanian independence movement to clear the way for the canal. Mignolo adds that the 1850 conflicts in Panama were about controlling the crossing between two oceans, and that after 1898 "Latin" America took on the inferior role in relation to the United States, which is why the canal stands for foreign influence and not just engineering. All four share one root, with the Conquest founding the hierarchies, the Tahuantinsuyo recalling what they erased, and the Malvinas and Panama showing external control outliving independence. As the annex concludes, memory "is not an archive. It is a way of seeing the world."

## Question 2a

Mignolo's claim that the Creoles "were already outside of history" makes sense once history is understood as the story of Western Christians, "assumed to be the total and true one for every inhabitant of the planet" and used to justify violence. Three European conceptions of the world, all promoted during colonisation, produced this colonisation of being, the idea "that certain people do not belong to history". The first was Christian cosmology, in which the T-in-O map gave Asia to Shem, Africa to Ham, whose son was cursed to be "a slave", and Europe to Japheth, so America became a fourth continent that "came into being late". This geography came with a ranking of humanity whose logic was "you are not like me, therefore you are inferior", so missionaries wrote the histories "they thought Incas and Aztecs did not have", while Africans were "simply left out of the picture of conversion and taken as pure labor force". The second was the imperial difference, born when Britain spread the "Black Legend" of Spanish corruption in the seventeenth century, pushing Iberia itself behind the North. The Creoles, of Spanish and Portuguese descent but "no longer Spanish or Portuguese", surfaced into this world. Displaced by peninsular elites, they "could not claim the past that belonged to the Spaniards, to the Indians, or to the Africans", and lived with a "double consciousness" of "not being who they were supposed to be (Europeans)". The Enlightenment then moved these differences from space into time, turning "barbarians" into "primitives", and Mignolo shows how Buffon and Hegel saw the New World as "young", with the colonies "trailing behind" Europe. The Creoles' marginalisation was "far from the extremes" faced by Indians and Africans, who were not even seen as human, but it still left them stuck "between the limits of humanity (Indians and Africans) and humanity proper (Europeans)", outside a history that was never theirs to write.

## Question 2b

Munck notes that some scholars see the prospects of democracy in Latin America as shaped by the Iberian culture Spain and Portugal passed on to local elites, and colonial politics supports this view, since it was built around the expectations, imaginations, and mindsets of Iberian elites. Their main expectation was wealth, so colonisation, as Mignolo shows, linked "faith, land possession, and the massive exploitation of labor" in "mines as well as in plantations", and power sat with transplanted "Spanish and Portuguese elites" keeping that wealth "for themselves". This came with the imagination of a Christian empire, as papal bulls and the Treaty of Tordesillas divided new lands between Spain and Portugal and gave Christians the right to "take possession", enacted when "officers of the Castilian crown and the church" read the Requerimiento to Indigenous peoples. The Iberian powers claimed a continent and organised it like Spain and Portugal, through viceroyalties such as Mexico and Peru, an "imperial coalition between the Spanish crown and the Catholic Church", and a theological ideology under which Spain "organized, controlled, and sustained its colonies". Underneath was a mindset of ranking, as taking land and labour "required the simultaneous ideological construction of racism", so Creoles were "socially and economically displaced" and Indigenous and African people were "marginalized and exploited" in what pan-Africanists, as Johnson notes, call a structure of white supremacy. The pattern outlived the empire, as independence replaced external colonialism "with internal colonialism" under Creole elites. However, while the cultural argument looks good on paper, Munck warns that it, like its rivals, has not been systematically tested, and a preliminary test he cites finds no democratic effect of Catholic culture. Indigenous rebellions like those of 1780–81, put down by Spanish colonial authorities, challenged the colonial order from below, so the Iberian legacy shaped colonial politics deeply but was one influence among several, not a fixed fate.
