## Question 1

The study ranks three references, the Malvinas/Falklands, the Conquest, and the Panama Canal, among the most important for Latin American identity, with the Tahuantinsuyo as the main Indigenous one, and the study's point that they are all "about power" is the key to reading them together. Each leads back to the European takeover of the "New World", which created both the hierarchies Quijano calls the coloniality of power and the region's subordination to outside powers, while memory has also become a way of recovering what that takeover erased. The article treats these events as "sites of memory" and argues that societies remember to interpret the present rather than preserve the past. Remembering the Conquest is therefore less about nostalgia than explaining present inequality. The Conquest is the founding wound, and the article's view is that questions of who holds power and who defines national narratives find part of their answers in that colonial founding, whose hierarchies independence did not necessarily eliminate.

The prominence of the Tahuantinsuyo reflects an effort to recover histories marginalised by the Conquest, in line with the article's point that there is growing recognition of histories that predate European arrival. However, the article itself asks "which cultures are made visible and which remain marginalized". Greater visibility of the Tahuantinsuyo in regional narratives does not show that Indigenous peoples hold more power, and the survey draws on experts, which leaves open whose understanding of Indigenous memory it represents.

The Malvinas extend this concern with colonial legacies to the question of sovereignty. The article presents the dispute with Britain as a symbol of the region's incomplete autonomy and of a region still debating the limits of its autonomy in relation to external powers. Panama reflects the continuation of this struggle under foreign influence. The article presents the canal as embodying debates over strategic control, foreign influence, and the capacity to decide on resources regarded as fundamental to the region, with US-China tensions making Panama a space where international interests intersect with aspirations for regional sovereignty.

The Conquest, Indigenous memory, the Malvinas, and Panama therefore connect collective memory to continuing struggles over power, identity, and sovereignty. Their relevance comes from the survival of colonial hierarchies and external influence, making the past a basis for interpreting present inequalities and asserting greater autonomy.

## Question 2a

Mignolo's claim that the Creoles were "outside of history" refers to a European understanding of history that treated Western Christianity as the standard for all societies. By presenting their own past as the history of humanity, European powers could dismiss other histories and justify conquest through claims of religious and civilisational superiority.

This understanding drew on Christian cosmology, with the T-in-O map dividing the world between Noah's sons, assigning Asia to Shem, Africa to Ham, and Europe to Japheth. America was then added as a fourth continent within this account, making European arrival the starting point for its recognised history and marginalising the histories of societies that already existed there.

The same logic shaped how the continent's populations were understood, as European beliefs became the basis for ranking their humanity. Missionaries assumed that the Incas and Aztecs lacked histories and wrote European accounts on their behalf, while enslaved Africans were reduced to a source of labour. Mignolo calls this denial of historical identity and humanity the colonisation of being.

It was within this hierarchy that Creoles of Spanish and Portuguese descent, born in the Americas, emerged as a distinct group by the late seventeenth century. Their ancestry did not give them equal standing with European-born elites, while Indigenous and African histories were not their own to claim. This produced what Mignolo calls a double consciousness, as they identified as Europeans but could not secure full recognition as such.

Their position was also shaped by rivalries within Europe, which increasingly placed Iberia below the northern European powers. Mignolo links British portrayals of Spanish corruption during the seventeenth century to this imperial difference, showing how European powers ranked each other as well as their colonies. Creoles therefore faced exclusion within the Americas while their European origins were themselves being devalued.

The Enlightenment later reinforced these divisions through the idea of historical progress, with Buffon and Hegel presenting the New World as young and immature. Europe became the standard against which other societies were measured, turning cultural differences into supposed stages of development. Although Creole marginalisation was less extreme than the dehumanisation imposed on Indigenous peoples and Africans, their ancestry could not secure recognition as equals within a history whose direction and standards were defined in Europe.

## Question 2b

Munck notes that some scholars see the prospects of democracy in Latin America as shaped by the Iberian culture Spain and Portugal passed on to local elites, and colonial politics supports this view, since it was built around the expectations, imaginations, and mindsets of Iberian elites. Their main expectation was wealth, so colonisation, as Mignolo shows, linked "faith, land possession, and the massive exploitation of labor" in "mines as well as in plantations", and power sat with transplanted "Spanish and Portuguese elites" keeping that wealth "for themselves". This came with the imagination of a Christian empire, as papal bulls and the Treaty of Tordesillas divided new lands between Spain and Portugal and gave Christians the right to "take possession", enacted when "officers of the Castilian crown and the church" read the Requerimiento to Indigenous peoples. The Iberian powers claimed a continent and organised it like Spain and Portugal, through viceroyalties such as Mexico and Peru, an "imperial coalition between the Spanish crown and the Catholic Church", and a theological ideology under which Spain "organized, controlled, and sustained its colonies". Underneath was a mindset of ranking, as taking land and labour "required the simultaneous ideological construction of racism", so Creoles were "socially and economically displaced" and Indigenous and African people were "marginalized and exploited" in what pan-Africanists, as Johnson notes, call a structure of white supremacy. The pattern outlived the empire, as independence replaced external colonialism "with internal colonialism" under Creole elites. However, while the cultural argument looks good on paper, Munck warns that it, like its rivals, has not been systematically tested, and a preliminary test he cites finds no democratic effect of Catholic culture. Indigenous rebellions like those of 1780–81, put down by Spanish colonial authorities, challenged the colonial order from below, so the Iberian legacy shaped colonial politics deeply but was one influence among several, not a fixed fate.
