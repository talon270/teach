# INT365 Tutorial 06: National Pride and Private Gains

---

On 15 October 2026, Haas confirmed that Rafael Câmara, a Brazilian driver backed by Escudería Telmex, would join Formula 1 in 2027, marking another success for the motorsport programme of Carlos Slim Domit, chairman of América Móvil, Telmex and Grupo Carso. The programme's most famous product is Sergio "Checo" Pérez. According to one marketer, Pérez's "biggest asset is national pride", making him "a money-printing machine" for Telcel, Infinitum and Claro.

This overlap of national pride and private gains is far from new. For Mignolo, the official Baroque of the colonies served Iberian elites who kept "the wealth generated by the colonial economy" for themselves. Independence changed the owners rather than the arrangement, turning the Creoles into "the postcolonial elite".

The nineteenth century tied this arrangement to the nation-state. In Central America, Tulchin shows, coffee and banana oligarchies built liberal states that "sought to maximize the oligarchy's comparative advantage", granting foreign investors privileges that reduced "the autonomy of the national state", and these states answered to no one "except to the faction that controlled it". In Chile, a nationalism forged against Spain in 1865 became "the base from which Chilean expansion was launched" in the war of 1879 to 1883, a push "strongly supported by foreign capital" into the nitrate fields. National glory and private profit marched together.

Elite prestige was borrowed from abroad. Slim Domit links Formula 1 to "speed, modernity, technology and international competition", much as Tulchin's nineteenth-century elites were "captivated by the central notions of civilization" and looked to Europe for education and art. In Brazil, the elite split between admirers of Europe and admirers of the United States. Slim Domit's companies now back a Mexican at Cadillac and a Brazilian at Haas, "the American team".

The twentieth century briefly shifted the balance. Lucero describes "interventionist, nationalist and developmental states" that pushed agrarian reform "in the face of elite opposition", while Querejazu and Tickner recall dependency authors' concern with how capitalism "made allies out of Latin American states and elites". However, the oil and debt shocks of the 1980s led to structural adjustment that "effectively dismantled the old corporatist state". Hence national celebration is no longer a project of the state but a sponsorship opportunity, from Pérez's run down Paseo de la Reforma to the Grand Prix that Slim Domit helped return in 2015.

National symbols have served decorative ends before. Lucero shows that Indigenous symbolism in Diego Rivera's murals "served largely decorative functions for plans of modernization", celebrating the nation while its Indigenous people stayed at the margins. Pérez's image, "everywhere in Mexico City", works the same way. The returns, by contrast, remain hidden. No published figure shows what he brings to Telcel, and the 19.84 billion pesos of Grand Prix impact comes from studies "commissioned by the organizers", making the pride public and the profit private.

Another overlap concerns the region itself. An analyst calls Câmara "a diversification play" for companies "expanding beyond just Mexico". Mignolo shows that "Latinidad" began as an identity "asserted by the French and adopted by Creole elites", and Tulchin notes that France used the Latin "race" as "an excuse for their intervention in Mexico". A label that once excused intervention now defines a customer base.

Lucero reports many movements calling neoliberal elites "anti-national" because they are "more worried about foreign investors than poor citizens". The Slim case complicates that charge. Here a business elite is not anti-national but has learned to own the nation's emotions, sponsoring the hero, the race, and the young drivers who follow.

Private gain in Latin America has depended not only on land, mines, and companies, but on the story of the nation.

As national pride moves from the mural to the racetrack, any account of Latin American nationalism must trace who profits from it as closely as who celebrates it.
