## Question 1

The study identifies the Malvinas (Falklands), the Conquest, and the Panama Canal as among the most important historical references for understanding Latin American identity, with the Tahuantinsuyo, or Inca Empire, as the primary reference for Indigenous memory. The study's own verdict is that all three are "about power", a big theme with a simple core, and it is the key to reading them together. According to the article, these references organise regional identity around three concerns, namely sovereignty, the colonial experience, and relations with external powers. The Conquest speaks to the colonial experience, while the Malvinas and Panama speak to sovereignty and external powers, making each reference a site where the region's unresolved questions about power are kept alive. The article draws on Pierre Nora to treat these events as "sites of memory", and on Elizabeth Jelin to argue that societies remember in order to interpret the present rather than preserve the past. Hence remembering the Conquest is less about nostalgia than about explaining present inequality. The Conquest remains the founding wound, having created the hierarchies Aníbal Quijano calls the "coloniality of power". According to the article, the questions of who holds power and who defines national narratives find part of their answers in that colonial founding, since independence did not necessarily eliminate its hierarchies. Five centuries on, the bill is still coming due, making the Conquest a living reference rather than a distant one. The prominence of the Tahuantinsuyo reflects an effort to recover histories marginalised by the Conquest, in line with the article's point about a growing recognition of histories that predate European arrival. However, the article itself asks "which cultures are made visible and which remain marginalized". Greater visibility of the Tahuantinsuyo in regional narratives does not show that Indigenous peoples hold more power. Additionally, the survey draws on experts, leaving open whose understanding of Indigenous memory it represents. The Malvinas carry this concern with power into the question of sovereignty. The article presents the dispute between Argentina and the United Kingdom as a symbol of incomplete sovereignty, of a region still debating the limits of its autonomy in relation to external powers, and notes that such conflicts survive because they represent something greater than the territory under dispute. Panama, meanwhile, shows the same struggle in a strategic setting. The article presents the canal as embodying debates over strategic control, foreign influence, and the capacity to make decisions about resources regarded as fundamental to the region. With tensions between the United States and China reshaping global geopolitics, Panama remains a space where international interests intersect with aspirations for regional sovereignty. This focus on the past is notable given that the region faces challenges such as artificial intelligence, the energy transition, and the climate crisis. Far from signalling stagnation, the article reads it as evidence that memory serves a political function, since these historical symbols still speak to debates the region has yet to resolve. Hence the Conquest, Indigenous memory, the Malvinas, and Panama connect collective memory to continuing struggles over power, identity, and sovereignty. Their relevance comes from the survival of colonial hierarchies and external influence, turning the past from a closed chapter into a basis for interpreting present inequalities and asserting greater autonomy. As the article concludes, memory "is not an archive. It is a way of seeing the world."

## Question 2a

Mignolo's claim that the Creoles were "already outside of history" rests on a European view that treated the story of Western Christians as the history of humanity. Four conceptions of the world, promoted during colonisation, built on this view and placed Latin American populations, including the Creoles, outside that history. The first was Christian cosmology, in which the T-in-O map assigned Asia to Shem, Africa to Ham, and Europe to Japheth. America was then added as a fourth continent, making European arrival the start of its recognised history and marginalising the societies already there. The second was a ranking of humanity, with missionaries writing the histories they "thought Incas and Aztecs did not have", while Africans were "taken as pure labor force". Mignolo calls this the colonisation of being, the idea "that certain people do not belong to history". The Creoles surfaced into this ranked world by the late seventeenth century. Of Spanish and Portuguese descent but born in the Americas, they "could not claim the past that belonged to the Spaniards, to the Indians, or to the Africans", leaving them "living in a present without history". Hence they were stuck in the middle, neither here nor there, with a double consciousness of not being who they were supposed to be, namely Europeans. The third was the imperial difference, as the British spread the black legend of Spanish corruption during the seventeenth century, placing Iberia below northern Europe. As a result, Creoles were excluded in the Americas while their European origins were being devalued. The fourth was Enlightenment progress, which moved difference from space into time. Barbarians "located in space" became "primitives", while Buffon and Hegel saw the New World as "younger and immature", with the colonies "trailing behind" Europe. Though their marginalisation was "far from the extremes" faced by Indigenous peoples and Africans, the Creoles remained "between the limits of humanity (Indians and Africans) and humanity proper (Europeans)", outside a history whose standards were defined in Europe.

## Question 2b

Munck notes that some scholars trace Latin America's democratic prospects to the Iberian culture Spain and Portugal transferred to local elites. Colonial political organisation supports this view, since it was built around the expectations, imaginations, and mindsets of European elites. The main expectation was wealth, and colonisation, as Mignolo shows, linked "faith, land possession, and the massive exploitation of labor" in "mines as well as in plantations". Tulchin notes that the Spanish concentrated on the mining regions of Mexico and the altiplano, so authority clustered around mines, ports, and administrative centres, while elsewhere "that control was thin". The imagination of a Christian empire gave this order its legal basis, as papal bulls and the Treaty of Tordesillas divided the new lands and gave Christians the right to "take possession", enacted through the Requerimiento. It also produced an "imperial coalition between the Spanish crown and the Catholic Church", ruling through viceroyalties such as Mexico and Peru. Tulchin contrasts this with British North America, where no established church "complemented the imperial mandate". Underneath sat a mindset of ranking, since taking land and labour "required the simultaneous ideological construction of racism". Peninsular elites stood above the "socially and economically displaced" Creoles, while Indigenous and African people were, as Johnson notes, "marginalized and exploited". The same mindset of control limited self-government. Cabildos grew "in spite of Spanish efforts to control them" but lacked "effective networking among viceroyalties or intendencies", and the Crown's eighteenth-century reforms failed against "the centrifugal force of local power brokers". After independence, Creole elites replaced external colonialism "with internal colonialism". However, Indigenous rebellions such as those of 1780–81 challenged this order from below, and Munck warns that such arguments lack "systematic empirical tests", so the cultural thesis looks good on paper but the proof is not there. Hence elite mindsets shaped colonial political organisation deeply, but not as a fixed fate.
