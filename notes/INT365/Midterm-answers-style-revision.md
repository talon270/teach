## Question 1

Annex 1 identifies the Conquest, the Malvinas/Falklands and the Panama Canal as key references for Latin American identity and the Tahuantinsuyo as the main reference for Indigenous history. Taken together, the annex argues, they are “about power”. This evidence suggests that each leads back to the European takeover of the “New World”, whose hierarchies and external control persisted post-independence. In this annex, Pierre Nora’s concept of “sites of memory” is invoked to explain the retention of certain events in the public discourse. The annex adds that societies remember to interpret the present, not to preserve the past, much like Querejazu and Tickner’s ancestrality, where “the past holds answers to present and future problems”. The Conquest is therefore less a matter of nostalgia than a site of dispute over land, inequality and the right to narrate the region’s history. Aníbal Quijano refers to the survival of colonial hierarchies after independence as the “coloniality of power”. To Mignolo, the “New World” was new only for Europeans. The appropriation of land and exploitation of labour demanded a justification, so European narratives presented Indigenous and African people as inferior. Johnson traces the pan-Africanist view of a structure of white supremacy that exploited Black and Indigenous people for three centuries and outlived abolition. Lucero finds an echo of this hierarchy in the slogan “we are all mestizo”, which made Indigenous difference invisible. Querejazu and Tickner associate colonial rule and its aftermath with dispossession in a region characterised by extreme land inequality. The Tahuantinsuyo brings the pre-Conquest history of these regions into this debate. Mignolo holds that maps naming the continent America erased the Tawantinsuyu and Anáhuac, suggesting that their histories began with conquest. Indigenous movements reclaiming the name Abya-Yala for the continent are a rejection of this perspective. Resistance memories have provided organisation for subsequent movements. Lucero recalls the Andean rebellions of 1780–81 led by Túpak Amaru, Micaela Bastides, Túpaj Katari and Bartolina Sisa. Katari’s perhaps apocryphal promise to return “made into millions” later rallied Bolivia’s Kataristas to the memory of an earlier rebellion and its anti-colonial message. Yet the Inca past can be celebrated without acknowledgment of living Indigenous communities. Lucero recounts the way the Peruvian elites laid claim to the Inca heritage while Indigenous people remained excluded, echoing “Incas yes, Indians no”. Annex 1 is guided by expert opinion; the choice of the Tahuantinsuyo does not establish how Indigenous communities themselves understand this memory. The Malvinas/Falklands carry a different memory of European power. The islands remain a point of contention with Britain, becoming a symbol of “incomplete sovereignty” in Annex 1. Russell adds that the failure of the Organization of American States and the Inter-American Treaty of Reciprocal Assistance to halt the 1982 war rekindled suspicions of hemispheric institutions. The dispute therefore reflects both the territorial claims issue and the limitations of hemispheric support. Panama introduces the United States into the narrative. Tulchin notes that Bolívar’s 1826 Congress in Panama promoted collaboration among the new republics, a dream that remains “a powerful concept” in the region. Mignolo documents the conflicts over Panama in 1850 concerning the route between the Atlantic and Pacific and identifies 1898 as the moment “Latin” America took on “the inferior role” in relation to the United States. Theodore Roosevelt’s dealings with unelected Panamanian leaders to clear passage for the canal, which Tulchin describes, gave foreign power a material presence. For Annex 1, the canal poses the question of who determines the use of Panama’s territory. The Conquest and the Tahuantinsuyo raise a similar question of who determines the region’s past. The Malvinas dispute, US control over a transit route and land inequality keep these arguments in contemporary politics. All four lead back to the European takeover, whose hierarchies and external control outlived independence. As the annex concludes, memory “is not an archive. It is a way of seeing the world.”

## Question 2a

Mignolo’s statement that the Creoles were “outside of history” reflects the European belief in Western Christian history as universal, letting Europeans dismiss other histories and justify conquest as a religious mission. The first conception was Christian cosmology, whose T-in-O map allocated Asia to Shem, Africa to Ham and Europe to Japheth, Noah’s sons, with Ham’s son cursed to be “a slave”, while America became a fourth continent that “came into being late”. This geography carried a ranking of humanity, “you are not like me, therefore you are inferior”, that determined whose past was deemed worthy of European narration. Missionaries produced the histories the Incas and Aztecs were assumed to lack, while enslaved Africans were regarded as labour, not as individuals with histories of their own. Mignolo calls this denial of historical identity and humanity the colonisation of being. By the late seventeenth century, Creoles of Spanish and Portuguese descent born in the Americas formed a distinct group. Their European origins placed them above Indigenous and African populations, but European elites did not accept them as equals. Unable to claim the European past, or the histories of Indigenous and African people, as their own, they lived in “a present without history”, with a double consciousness of “not being who they were supposed to be (Europeans)”. The second conception, the “imperial difference”, ranked European powers themselves: seventeenth-century English portrayals of Spanish corruption placed Iberia, and so the Creoles’ origins, below the north. The third, the Enlightenment’s narrative of progress, moved this hierarchy from space into time, turning “barbarians” into “primitives”, as Mignolo shows Buffon and Hegel depicting a young, immature New World “trailing behind” Europe. The Creoles’ exclusion was less severe than the dehumanisation of Indigenous peoples and Africans, but their European descent left them “between the limits of humanity (Indians and Africans) and humanity proper (Europeans)”, without an equal place in the history Europe chose to narrate.

## Question 2b

Munck presents the Iberian colonial legacy as one possible explanation for Latin America’s subsequent political regimes. Spanish and Portuguese elites expected their colonies to generate wealth and imagined them within a Christian empire. These expectations shaped who held power and how land and labour were organised. Mignolo links colonial wealth to faith, the seizure of land and the exploitation of labour in mines and plantations. Transplanted Spanish and Portuguese elites held power, concentrating land and its produce in their hands. Papal bulls and the Treaty of Tordesillas demarcated territories for Spain and Portugal, which organised them like their own kingdoms, and gave Christian occupation legitimacy. Officers of the Castilian crown and the Church read the Requerimiento to Indigenous peoples to enact possession of their land. Viceroyalties in Mexico and Peru placed administration under Spanish royal authority, and the Church upheld this order through the theology under which Spain “organized, controlled, and sustained its colonies”. A mindset of ranking also determined who could exercise power. Mignolo argues that the seizure of land and the exploitation of labour were predicated on portraying colonised people as inferior. Creoles were displaced by elites from Europe while Indigenous and African people faced dispossession and exploitation. Johnson reports the pan-Africanist view that Spanish and Portuguese colonialism, slavery and racial oppression created a structure of white supremacy. Independence transferred political power to Creole elites, but according to Mignolo this also enabled internal colonialism to replace direct rule from overseas. The Andean rebellions of 1780–81 illustrate that Indigenous communities had challenged colonial authority, although Spanish forces quelled them. Munck cautions that the claim associating Iberian culture with subsequent regimes has not been systematically tested against alternative explanations. He also cites preliminary research finding no impact of Catholic culture on democratic results. Colonial beliefs explain how rulers organised power and sustained this order; Munck’s caution curtails any conclusions about twentieth-century democracy.
