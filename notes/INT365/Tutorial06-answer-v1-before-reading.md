# INT365 Tutorial 06: National Pride and Private Gains

---

In contemporary Latin America, private profit is often presented in the language of the nation, and the pattern is an old one. From colonial mines to today's lithium deals, natural resources have been claimed in the name of the whole nation, while their rents have gone to a narrow group of insiders.

The colonial economy set this pattern. Mignolo ties Europe's splendour to "gold and silver mines", plantations and "the exploitation of Indian and African labor", and the official Baroque served Iberian elites who kept "the wealth generated by the colonial economy" for themselves. Independence changed who held the wealth, not how it was organised. The Creoles, Mignolo argues, simply became "the postcolonial elite".

In the nineteenth century, nationalism and private interest grew together. Tulchin shows Central American oligarchies built on coffee or bananas creating liberal states to maximise "the oligarchy's comparative advantage". Those states gave foreign investors privileges that reduced "the autonomy of the national state", and the result was the "banana republic", accountable only "to the faction that controlled it". Chile's nationalism, forged against Spain in the 1860s, then drove a push into the nitrate fields "strongly supported by foreign capital", ending in the War of the Pacific. National glory and nitrate profits went hand in hand. In Argentina, the export elite's satisfaction with the international division of labour came before national strategy. Oil found in Patagonia in 1905 was left undeveloped until British coal stopped arriving in 1914 and "the lights in Buenos Aires went out".

The twentieth-century developmental state tried to turn this relationship around. Cárdenas's 1938 oil expropriation made Pemex a symbol of Mexican sovereignty. Lucero describes "interventionist, nationalist and developmental states" that pushed agrarian reform "in the face of elite opposition". Yet that same nationalism set out to "Mexicanize" the Indian, so national pride was still defined from above. Dependency theorists, as Querejazu and Tickner explain, studied how capitalism "truncated state sovereignty and made allies out of Latin American states and elites". The oil and debt shocks of the 1980s brought this model down and opened the way to privatisation.

Bolivia shows how the two sides clashed again. Under Sánchez de Lozada, the privatisation of water in Cochabamba raised prices by up to 400 percent. A plan to export gas through "the historic national enemy (Chile)" to "the contemporary imperial center (the United States)" brought hundreds of thousands into the streets. These movements called "neoliberal elites" "anti-national", because those elites cared more about "foreign investors than poor citizens". Morales rode that anger to power, but Guaraní protests against state extraction on their land show that nationalised resources can still exclude.

The same overlap shapes current events. Tulchin notes that ExxonMobil's 2015 oil discovery under a Guyanese concession revived Venezuela's claim to the Essequibo, so a private find set off territorial nationalism. Petrobras, once a national champion, became the centre of the Lava Jato kickback scheme. Chile's national lithium strategy runs through a partnership between state-owned Codelco and the private firm SQM. Mignolo's description still fits: the "idea" of Latin America remains "a large mass of land with a wealth of natural resources and plenty of cheap labor". Russell warned that unmet economic promises could bring back leaders who wave "the flags of nationalism and anti-imperialism". Over time, the group capturing the rents has changed. It moved from Iberian elites to export oligarchies and their foreign partners, then to state firms and connected contractors. National pride gives each arrangement its legitimacy. Meanwhile the communities living on the land pay the costs, in a region where, as Lucero puts it, extractive industries "drill first and ask questions later".
