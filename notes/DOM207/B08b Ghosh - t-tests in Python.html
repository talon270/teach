t Test in Python

One claimed diameter, two factories, one new machine: four SciPy calls, each decided by a p-value.

Jaideep Ghosh, t Test in Python (Lect-TTestPython, 31 slides). School of Management and Entrepreneurship, Shiv Nadar University. DOM207 Introduction to Data Science for Business Using R and Python, Monsoon 2026.

The deck in one breath. The t test compares means through hypothesis testing: start with a null (the status quo) and look for evidence to reject it. The p-value, the significance level α (the Type 1 error rate) and statistical significance are defined first, together with four assumptions: independence, identical distribution, normality and equal variances. A Honda supplier's claim of 10 cm ball bearings then drives four SciPy tests. ttest_1samp tests the claim against 21 bearings, and the same test is coded by hand. ttest_ind compares a second factory's bearings (reject: the factories differ). ttest_rel runs a before/after pilot of a new machine (reject). ttest_ind(equal_var=False), Welch's test, compares the factories again with a temperature-sensitive machine (fail to reject, but collect more data). On the way, the TTest-2 male/female scores get a one-sided Welch p of 0.0135. Every bearing dataset is random, with no seed, so the printed numbers will not reproduce.

1–5slides

The t test, p-value, SL, statistical significance

1t Test in Python2The t Test3The p-val4The SL5Statistical Significance
Ghosh · sl. 2

"The t test allows you to compare the means of two groups and determine whether they're statistically different." It is based on HT: you start with a null (H₀) and try to find evidence to reject it. "The null represents the status quo, while the alternate/alternative (H₁) represents its opposite." Example: use HT to determine if a new product launched by a company receives a good customer response and is reflected in the success metric.

p-val sl. 3

The p-val is the probability of obtaining results as extreme as the observed ones, assuming the null is true. A small p-val means that probability is very low, given that the null is correct.

SL sl. 4

The SL, denoted by alpha, is the probability of rejecting the null, assuming it is true. It is also known as a Type 1 error: the probability of rejecting a true null.

Significance sl. 5

Statistical significance is your confidence that the observed result is not due to chance. It does not necessarily mean practical significance: the result could still be negligible in practical terms. Sampling error can contribute to statistical significance if the sample does not accurately represent the larger population.

outside slidesStrictly, α is the Type 1 error rate that you choose in advance. A Type 1 error is the event of rejecting a true null; B07 makes the same distinction.

t-test assumptions

6t-test Assumptions

Four assumptions

sl. 6
Independence
Observations are not influenced by any implicit factor in the data and are not correlated over time.
Identically distributed
Understand the underlying reasons for anomalous behaviour to determine whether it is legitimate or erroneous data.
Normally distributed
The t-test is only valid for normally distributed data. Skewed data may lead to incorrect rejection of the null hypothesis.
Equal variances
The two populations being compared should have equal variances. "You can test it graphically using a Q-Q plot."
Trap · sl. 6

A Q-Q plot checks normality (points on the line = normal), not equal variances. For equal variances use a boxplot (TTest-1 sl. 29) or Levene / Fligner-Killeen / var.test (TTest-2 sl. 15–24). In SciPy these are stats.levene and stats.fligner outside slides. The equal-variance assumption applies only to the pooled two-sample test; Welch's test (sl. 28) drops it, which is why sl. 13 runs Welch "to be on the safe side".

7–10slides

One-sample t-test and the Honda bearing claim

7One-sample t-test8Example9Specs10Null (H₀) & Alternative (H₁)

The one-sample t-test helps you determine if an unknown population mean (μ) does not equal a claimed/desired value (sl. 7).

Test statistic · sl. 7t = x̄ − μs / √n  with x̄ sample mean, μ population mean, s sample SD, n sample size
Case · sl. 8, 10

A Honda parts supplier manufacturing company claims to produce ball bearings for the Honda City of 10 cm diameter (the null). You decide to check (sl. 10: "audit") whether that is true. You collect 21 ball bearings from multiple shops in the city and measure their diameters: this is your sample. You must determine whether the company's claim is false (the alternate) based on the measurements.

Specs

sl. 9
Hypotheses
H₀: x̄ = μ; H₁: x̄ ≠ μ [→ H₀: μ = 10; H₁: μ ≠ 10]
Numbers
x̄ = 11, s = 1, n = 21, μ = 10
Logic
To declare the claim false, you must statistically prove that the sample measurements differ from the 10 cm claimed.
Why t
The sample size is 21, which is less than 30, so use a t-test.
Function
scipy.stats.ttest_1samp()

outside slidesHypotheses are always about the population: the sample mean x̄ = 11 is known, so "H₀: x̄ = μ" is not something to test. With the stated numbers, t = (11 − 10)/(1/√21) = 4.58 on 20 df, p ≈ 0.0002: reject the claim. This matches the "4.69" in the sl. 15 text much better than the printed 8.998.

In SciPy, scipy.stats.ttest_ind() calculates the independent 2-sample t-test with the null that two independent samples have identical average values (sl. 12). The data are TTest-2's vectors.

from scipy import stats

          31,30,21,28,22,22,27,27,26,30]

res = stats.ttest_ind(mscore, fscore, equal_var=False)
print(f'p-val for single-sided t-test: {res.pvalue / 2 :.4f}')

ttest_1samp on the bearings

14Python Code15Results
Pythonsl. 14
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

mu = 10   # pop mean
n1 = 21   # sample size
dof = n1 – 1   # dof      [sic: en dash "–" → minus "-"]
# random sample with mean = 11 and SD = 1
x = np.random.randn(n1) + 11
# Using Stats library, compute t-stat and p-val
t_stat, p_val = stats.ttest_1samp(a=x, popmean = mu)
print("t-statistic = " + str(t_stat))
print("p-value = " + str(p_val))
t-statistic = 8.997784455932862 p-value = 1.8112582604688212e-08
Ghosh · sl. 15

"The value of t-stat is 4.69 which seems to be very far from the mean of zero in a t-dist." To quantify such an extreme value you refer to a p-val. The p-val is less than the default SL of 0.05: the probability of such an extreme outcome is close to zero and the null can be rejected. NOTE: you MUST set the SL at the beginning of your analysis. Choosing an SL to fit your result is considered CHEATING.

Trap · sl. 14–15

Three problems. (1) dof = n1 – 1 uses an en dash, a SyntaxError if typed as shown (also sl. 21, 25, 30). (2) The output says t = 8.998 but the text says 4.69. The code draws a fresh random sample on every run and sets no seed, so each run prints a different t. (3) In 4,000 re-runs the median t is 4.6 and 90% fall between 2.8 and 7.1, so the printed 8.998 is an unusually extreme draw, while 4.69 is typical. Add np.random.seed(…) (or rng = np.random.default_rng(…)) before drawing to make results repeatable. The decision (reject) holds in 99% of runs. outside slides

Answer to "will you or will you not reject the null?" Reject: p ≈ 1.8 × 10⁻⁸ < 0.05, so the bearings' mean diameter is not 10 cm and the supplier's claim is false.

16–18slides

A different approach: the one-sample t by hand

16Different Approach17Python Code18Results

Do the one-sample t-test without invoking ttest_1samp(). The t-stat is the difference between sample and population means divided by the STDERR: (x̄ − μ) / STDERR(x̄) (sl. 16).

Algorithm

sl. 16
1
Compute the sample mean x̄.
2
Compute the sample SD with dof = 1 [→ ddof = 1, i.e. divide by n − 1] (sample SD).
3
Compute STDERR by dividing SD by √n.
4
Use the one-sample t-stat formula.
5
Compute the p-val to establish the significance of the t-stat.
Pythonsl. 17
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

n1 = 21
x_bar = x.mean()            # sample mean
std = np.std(x, ddof=1)     # SD
ste = std/np.sqrt(n1)       # STDERR
# Calculate t-stat
t_stat = (x_bar - mu) / ste
# p-val of t-stat
p_val = 2*(1 - stats.t.cdf(abs(t_stat), df = dof))
# str function converts a numerical value to a string
print("t-statistic = " + str(t_stat))
print("p-value = " + str(p_val))
t-statistic = 2.554659214030404 p-value = 0.01888648096144374

The slide asks: are you getting the same result as before? outside slidesOn the same x, yes, to every digit: the hand formula is exactly what ttest_1samp computes. The slide's 2.55 differs from 8.998 only because x was redrawn between runs. Two details: ddof=1 matters, because NumPy's np.std defaults to ddof = 0, the population SD, unlike R's sd(). And 2*(1 - stats.t.cdf(|t|, dof)) is the two-sided p; 2*stats.t.sf(|t|, dof) is the same and more accurate for tiny p.

Two-sample t-test: are the two factories' bearings the same?

19Two-sample t-test20Example21Python Code22Results

The two-sample t-test helps to determine if unknown population means are the same (sl. 19).

Test statistic · sl. 19t = x₁ − x₂√( s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂ )  with x₁, x₂ the sample means, s₁, s₂ the SDs, n₁, n₂ the sizes
Case · sl. 20

The company sets up another factory to produce identical ball bearings. Do the two factories' bearings differ in size? Factory 1 shares 21 samples with a mean diameter of 10.5 cm; Factory 2 shares 25 samples with a mean of 9.5 cm. Both have an SD of 1 cm.

Pythonsl. 21
# Two-sample t-test
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

n1, n2 = 21, 25              # Sample sizes
dof = min(n1,n2) – 1        # dof
# Gaussian data: mean = 10.5 and var = 1
x = np.random.randn(n1) + 10.5
# Gaussian data: mean = 9.5 and var = 1
y = np.random.randn(n2) + 9.5
t_stat, p_val = stats.ttest_ind(x, y)
print("t-statistic = " + str(t_stat))
print("p-value = " + str(p_val))
t-statistic = 3.262833474726092 p-value = 0.002136389222601085
Ghosh · sl. 22

The p-val of 0.002 is less than the SL of 0.05, so you reject the null. Bottom line: the bearings from the two factories are nonidentical. Reject H₀

outside slidesThree mismatches. The sl. 19 formula is the unpooled (Welch) standard error, but ttest_ind(x, y) defaults to equal_var=True, the pooled test with df = n₁ + n₂ − 2 = 44. dof = min(n1, n2) − 1 = 20 is a conservative textbook shortcut, and the code computes it but never uses it; SciPy uses its own df. With the stated numbers the expected t is 1/√(1/21 + 1/25) ≈ 3.38, so the printed 3.26 is a typical draw, and about 92% of re-runs reject.

Paired-sample t-test: the new-machine pilot

23Paired-sample t-test24Example25Python Code26Results27What's the Catch?

The paired-sample t-test helps to determine if the means of paired items are the same (sl. 23).

Case · sl. 24

The company decides to improve its manufacturing technology by introducing a new machine. It starts this pilot from one of the factories and conducts a test to identify if the new machine leads to a change in the bearings' diameter. It compares two samples of 25 bearings, one before the new machine was introduced and the other after. The mean diameters are 10.5 cm and 9.9 cm respectively.

Pythonsl. 25
# Paired t-test
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

n = 25                       # Sample sizes
dof = n – 1                  # dof
# Gaussian data: mean = 10.5 and var = 1
x = np.random.randn(n) + 10.5
# Gaussian data: mean = 9.9 and var = 1
y = np.random.randn(n) + 9.9
t_stat, p_val = stats.ttest_rel(x,y)
print("t-statistic = " + str(t_stat))
print("p-value = " + str(p_val))
t-statistic = 2.8122050639123084 p-value = 0.009651455034900126
Ghosh · sl. 26

"Bottom line: The low p-val indicates that the null is rejected – there's no change in the diameter of the ball bearings after introducing the new machine."

Trap · sl. 24–26

The bottom line contradicts itself. Rejecting H₀ (no change) means there is a change: p = 0.0097 < 0.05 → the new machine changed the diameter (here, about 0.6 cm smaller). Reject H₀: there is a change

The design is not really paired. "Two samples of 25 bearings, one before … and the other after" are different bearings, and the code draws x and y independently, so ttest_rel pairs unrelated items by position. A bearing cannot be measured before it was made. Unrelated before/after samples call for ttest_ind (two independent groups, TTest-1 sl. 28). With truly independent data, re-runs of this code reject only about 54% of the time. outside slides

What's the catch?

sl. 27
Rule
The assumptions of your paired t-test are valid on the differences of the pair and not on the original observations.
So
Ensure the differences between the pairs are symmetrically distributed and have no outliers among them.
Check
outside slidesd = x - y, then stats.shapiro(d) and a boxplot of d. ttest_rel(x, y) is exactly ttest_1samp(x - y, 0).
28–31slides

Welch's t-test: the temperature-sensitive machine

28Welch's t-test29Example30Python Code31Results

Welch's t-test is also known as the unequal variances t-test. It is a 2-sample test of the null that two populations have equal means (sl. 28).

Case · sl. 29

After a successful pilot at one factory, the company tries out another pilot at its second factory with a new machine. This machine is known to produce highly accurate ball bearings but is affected by temperature and power fluctuations in the environment. Are the two factories' bearings different? Find out by comparing 21 samples from factory 1 (mean 9.9 cm, SD 1 cm) and 25 from factory 2 (mean 10 cm, SD 3 cm).

Pythonsl. 30
# Welch's t-test
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

n1, n2 = 21, 25              # Sample sizes
dof = min(n1,n2) – 1        # dof
# Gaussian data: mean = 9.9 and var = 1
x = np.random.randn(n1) + 9.9
# Gaussian data: mean = 10 and var = 3   [sic: SD 3, so var = 9]
y = 3*np.random.randn(n2) + 10
t_stat, p_val = stats.ttest_ind(x, y, equal_var = False)
print("t-statistic = " + str(t_stat))
print("p-value = " + str(p_val))
t-statistic = 1.0863729246416722 p-value = 0.2864969833328288
Ghosh · sl. 31

The observed p-val is greater than the SL of 0.05, so you fail to reject the null. "So, bearings from the two factories are identical." Caveat: you cannot really be sure of the results because of the high SD of the samples from factory 2, so you will need to collect more data. Fail to reject

outside slides"Identical" overstates it: failing to reject means no evidence of a difference (B07, B08a sl. 38). The caveat is the right instinct. The true gap is 0.1 cm against a standard error of about 0.64, so this design would detect a 0.1 cm difference only about 5% of the time, which is what the re-runs show. Multiplying randn by 3 scales the SD to 3, so the variance is 9, not 3 as the comment says.

Quick revision

The deck on one screen

The argument in ten lines

  1. The t test compares means by HT: start with H₀ (status quo) and look for evidence to reject it (sl. 2).
  2. p-val = P(results this extreme | H₀ true); small p → unlikely under H₀ (sl. 3).
  3. SL α = probability of rejecting a true null = Type 1 error (sl. 4).
  4. Statistical ≠ practical significance; sampling error can fake significance (sl. 5).
  5. Assumptions: independence, identically distributed, normal, equal variances (sl. 6).
  6. One-sample: is a 10 cm claim true? n = 21 < 30 → ttest_1samp; reject (sl. 7–15).
  7. By hand: mean, SD with ddof = 1, SE = SD/√n, t, p from stats.t.cdf (sl. 16–18).
  8. Two factories, 10.5 vs 9.5 cm: ttest_ind, p = 0.002 → different (sl. 19–22).
  9. New machine, before vs after: ttest_rel, p = 0.0097 → reject; assumptions apply to the differences (sl. 23–27).
  10. Unequal SDs (1 vs 3): Welch equal_var=False, p = 0.29 → fail to reject; collect more data (sl. 28–31).

t Test in Python

1What does the null represent, and what does the t test try to do with it?

The status quo; the alternative is its opposite. You start with the null and try to find evidence to reject it. (sl. 2)

2Define the p-val and say what a small one means.

The probability of results as extreme as the observed ones, assuming the null is true. Small → the observed results are very unlikely if the null is correct. (sl. 3)

3What is the SL, and what other name does it have?

Alpha: the probability of rejecting the null when it is true; also called a Type 1 error. (sl. 4)

4Give the deck's two warnings about statistical significance.

It does not necessarily mean practical significance (the effect may be negligible); and sampling error can produce it if the sample does not represent the population. (sl. 5)

5List the four t-test assumptions.

Independence; identically distributed; normally distributed (skew can cause false rejection); equal variances. (sl. 6)

6The slide says you can test equal variances with a Q-Q plot. What is wrong?

A Q-Q plot checks normality. Equal variances: boxplot, Levene, Fligner-Killeen or the F test (TTest-1/2). (outside slides)

7State the Honda bearing problem and its specs.

A supplier claims 10 cm bearings for the Honda City; you sample 21 from shops. x̄ = 11, s = 1, n = 21, μ = 10. Is the claim false? (sl. 8–10)

8Why a t-test rather than a z-test for the bearings, and which SciPy function?

n = 21 is less than 30 (and σ is unknown). scipy.stats.ttest_1samp(). (sl. 9)

9Write the sl. 12 call and explain why its p is divided by 2.

res = stats.ttest_ind(mscore, fscore, equal_var=False); res.pvalue / 2 gives the single-sided p (0.0135) for H₁: male < female. SciPy returns a two-sided p by default. (sl. 12–13)

10Why does sl. 13 use the unequal-variance test?

"To be on the safe side": do the equal-variance test only when you're sure the population variances are really equal. (sl. 13)

11sl. 15 gets p = 1.8e−08. What is the decision, and what rule about the SL does the slide add?

Reject the null: the claim of 10 cm is false. You MUST set the SL at the start; choosing it to fit the result is CHEATING. (sl. 15)

12Why do the slide's printed t-values (8.998, 2.555) disagree with each other and with "4.69"?

The data come from np.random.randn with no seed, so every run draws a new sample. Set a seed for reproducible results. (outside slides)

13Give the 5-step algorithm for the one-sample t by hand.

Sample mean; sample SD (ddof = 1); STDERR = SD/√n; t = (x̄ − μ)/STDERR; p-val from the t distribution. (sl. 16)

14Write the line that computes the two-sided p-value by hand.

p_val = 2*(1 - stats.t.cdf(abs(t_stat), df = dof)) with dof = n − 1. (sl. 17)

15What does ddof=1 do in np.std, and what happens without it?

Divides by n − 1, giving the sample SD. NumPy's default ddof = 0 divides by n (population SD), which makes t slightly too large. (sl. 17; default outside slides)

16State the two-factory problem and its result.

21 bearings at mean 10.5 vs 25 at 9.5, SD 1 each. ttest_ind(x, y): t = 3.26, p = 0.002 < 0.05 → reject; the factories' bearings are nonidentical. (sl. 20–22)

17Which variance assumption does stats.ttest_ind(x, y) make by default?

Equal variances (pooled), because equal_var=True is the default. Welch needs equal_var=False. (outside slides; sl. 21 vs 30)

18What does the paired-sample t-test determine, and which function runs it?

Whether the means of paired items are the same; stats.ttest_rel(x, y). (sl. 23, 25)

19sl. 26 says "the null is rejected – there's no change in the diameter". Correct it.

p = 0.0097 < 0.05 → reject H₀ of no change → the new machine did change the diameter (smaller, 10.5 → 9.9 cm).

20Is the sl. 24 pilot really a paired design?

No. Two different samples of 25 bearings (before and after) are independent groups; pairing needs the same items measured twice. ttest_ind fits. (outside slides)

21What's the catch with the paired t-test?

Its assumptions apply to the differences, not the original observations: the differences must be symmetric with no outliers. (sl. 27)

22What is Welch's t-test also called, and what null does it test?

The unequal variances t-test; a 2-sample test of H₀: the two populations have equal means. (sl. 28)

23State the Welch example, its result and the caveat.

A temperature-sensitive machine: 21 bearings (9.9, SD 1) vs 25 (10, SD 3). t = 1.086, p = 0.2865 > 0.05 → fail to reject. Caveat: factory 2's high SD means you can't be sure; collect more data. (sl. 29–31)

24Map each SciPy function to its R call from TTest-2.
