From big data to a list

The DS fundamentals lecture (big data, two approaches, statistics vocabulary), then lists in R and Python.

Jaideep Ghosh, Data Science using R and Python: Fundamentals (Lect-DSA-Fundamentals, 58 slides) and IDSBRP: Advanced Data Structures in R and Python (Lect-AdvDataStruct-R-P, 19 slides). School of Management and Entrepreneurship, Shiv Nadar University. DOM207, Monsoon 2026.

Fundamentals in one breath. Slides 2–21 retell the Intro-1 lecture (summarised on the B04 page) with small changes. The new material starts at sl. 22. Big data is data that is expensive to manage and hard to extract value from, characterised by 5 V's: velocity, volume, variety, veracity, value. With data you index/search/query, aggregate (OLAP over OLTP data) and discover knowledge. DS is multidisciplinary, raises ethical problems (a store guessing a pregnancy; election apps), and needs storytelling. There are two approaches. Top-down: problem → literature → hypotheses → model → test, using t-tests, χ², ANOVA, OLS, logit. Bottom-up: data → patterns → theory, using clustering, NLP, trees, neural nets, LLMs. The rest is statistics vocabulary: population vs sample, why sample, statistical inference, primary vs secondary data, sources, informed consent, data objects and attributes (data frames), and a glossary from mean to histogram.

Advanced Data Structures in one breath. After ordinary variables, vectors, data frames, functions and classes comes the list, a container for arbitrary objects of the same or different types. In R: lst5 <- list(myDF, 1:10, lst3), name its elements with names(), access them with [[ ]] by number or name, measure with length(), and grow it with lst5[[4]] <- 2 and lst5[["Novelty"]] <- 3:6. In Python, a list is one of 4 built-in collections (list, tuple, set, dictionary): ordered, changeable, duplicates allowed, indexed from [0], built with [ ] or list(( … )), sized with len().

sl. 2 What is DS and Analytics? DSA is the study of data to extract meaningful insights. It is a multidisciplinary approach that combines principles and practices from the fields of mathematics, statistics, AI and computer engineering to analyze large amounts of data. This analysis helps data scientists to ask and answer questions like what happened, why it happened, what will happen, and what can be done with the results.

sl. 3 Why is DSA Important? DSA is important because it combines tools, methods and technology to generate meaning from data. Modern organizations are flooded with data. There is a proliferation of devices that can automatically collect and store information. Online systems and payment portals capture more data in the fields of e-commerce, medicine, finance and every other aspect of human life. We have text, audio, video and image data available in vast quantities.

sl. 9 Who is a Data Scientist? A data scientist "is a high-ranking professional with the training and curiosity to make discoveries in the world of big data" (Harvard Business Review). They're big data wranglers, gathering and analyzing large sets of structured and unstructured data, numerical and non-numerical data. They analyze, process and model data, and interpret the results to create actionable plans for companies. Their jobs are in Business Intelligence and Data Analytics in IT/IS and many other industries and scientific disciplines.

sl. 10 Hats to Wear as a Data Scientist. You can be a chemist / business entrepreneur / biologist / financial analyst / astronomer / physicist / economist or econometrician / linguist / archaeologist / sociologist and a data scientist; and a data scientist and a data scientist.

sl. 11–12 What is Data Science? A multidisciplinary field that uses scientific methods, processes, algorithms and systems to extract knowledge and insights from data; it includes components of data mining, deep learning and big data. It is not just computational techniques in statistics, NNs, AI, Hadoop, NoSQL with its Python API, Spark, Python, R, Java and so on; it's much more: the way the human mind is used to see the connections between various things, draw conclusions from facts, learn from experience to make informed decisions in the future, and provide effective solutions for business, commerce and economics.

sl. 13 Data All Around. Lots of data is being collected and warehoused: web data, e-commerce; social media sites like Twitter, FB, MySpace; financial transactions, bank/credit transactions; online trading and purchasing.

sl. 14 The World of Data. Google processes more than 50 PB a day; Facebook has 97 TB of daily logs; eBay has 2.5 PB of user data + 92 TB/day; 1000 genomes project: 200 TB; cost of 1 TB of disk: INR 4,500; time to read a 1 TB disk: 3 hrs (100 MB/s). Relational data (tables/transaction/legacy data); semi-structured data (XML); text data (Web); streaming data; graph data (engineering); social network, Semantic Web, …; metaverse data (futuristic technology). Semantic Web: a proposed extension of the WWW providing programs with machine-interpretable metadata (data descriptors) of published information and data.

sl. 16 General Numerical Data. Engineering: electrical signals (digital or analog); magnetic field strength in materials. Biology: genome data; protein folding data. sl. 17 Modeling Numerical Data. Time series models: y = f(t); ỹ(ω) = FFT(f(t))(ω). Example: y = A cos(αt) + B sin(βt): seasonality or periodicity; y could be stock price or signal voltage. sl. 18 Non-Numerical Data. How do you model non-numerical data? Suppose I come to you and ask "How happy are you as a student of SNU?" This is a common feature of HR analytics: how am I supposed to measure this happiness variable? Suppose you ask the potential customer of a cosmetic product "What's your perception of the quality of this face cream?" A common feature of marketing analytics: how are you supposed to measure this perception variable?

sl. 19 Structured and Unstructured Data. Structured data conform to a specific data model, have a well-defined structure, follow a consistent order and organization, can be easily stored in a spreadsheet or a table in a relational DB, and can be easily accessed and used (example: data stored in EXCEL spreadsheets or relational DBs). Unstructured data are not arranged according to a predefined data model or schema and cannot be stored in a spreadsheet or relational-DB table (example: call center logs; audio interviews; video recordings).

sl. 20 Semi-structured Data. Have some structure; do not conform to the tabular structure of data models associated with relational DB or EXCEL spreadsheet; contains tags and markers to separate semantic elements in the data; enforce hierarchies of records and fields within the data. Example: emails, XML, binary executables, TCP/IP packets for internet, ZIP files, data integrated from different sources, and web pages.

36–40slides

Two approaches to DS: top-down and bottom-up

36Two Approaches to DS37Top-down Approach to DS38Common Top-down Tools39Bottom-up Approach to DS40Common Bottom-up Tools

There are two primary approaches to DS, top-down and bottom-up. Both are used in the field, but the types of problems they solve are different; this course uses both (sl. 36).

Top-down

sl. 37–38
Steps
The data scientist (1) identifies a problem; (2) looks up existing literature or collects expert opinions; (3) formulates hypotheses to establish some truth about the problem; (4) selects a model to empirically justify them; (5) implements the model and empirically tests it on data collected on the problem.
Tools
t-tests; χ²-fit; ANOVA; OLS MLR; logistic regression (logit; probit; Poisson; etc.); time series models and regressions. Not in course: confirmatory factor analysis, structural equation models, advanced time series models, panel regression.

Bottom-up

sl. 39–40
Steps
The data scientist (1) collects data about some phenomenon to be studied but is not driven by foreknowledge about it (there may not exist any); (2) uses appropriate DS techniques to try to find patterns in the data; (3) finally, based on her findings, theorizes about the problem and tests further generality of her analysis.
Tools
Cluster analysis; natural language processing; Bayesian classification; decision trees and random forest models; neural networks and deep learning; large language models. Not in course: principal component analysis, multidimensional scaling, exploratory factor analysis.

outside slidesThis is the frame for every later deck: TTest-1 calls the t-test a "top-down DSA procedure" and MLR-Theory-1 calls MLR "one of the most important of all top-down approaches" (B08a, B09). The course-file syllabus does list PCA (with clustering), unlike sl. 40.

Statistics sl. 41–42

Statistics is defined as the collection, compilation, analysis and interpretation of data. Like mathematics, it is a tool for DS. Why: understand variability and how it impacts products, processes and systems; estimate the present and predict the future; study methods to solve problems; convert data into information; understand reliability and randomness; "statistics is very important in our lives in many, many ways".

Measurements/observations: we assign #s to some variables. Data: a set of observations. Sample: some data we've collected. Population: all possible data out there (practically impossible to collect). 46–50slides

Population, sample, sampling, statistical inference

46Population & Sample47Population48Sample49Why Sampling?50What is Statistical Inference?

Figure, sl. 46: a large blue "Population" ellipse with a small green "Sample" box inside it. Population is the entire universe of data on something about which information is sought. NOTE: population does not necessarily refer to people; it may refer to collectivities like organizations, states, districts, countries, social groups (sl. 47). Sample pertains to the particular group from which you collect data: Sample Size < Population Size (sl. 48).

Why sampling? sl. 49

In most studies it's very hard to collect information from the whole population because of high cost, long time, extra resources and non-availability of all respondents. So we depend on samples to make estimates or inferences about the population.

Statistical inference sl. 50

We want to estimate things about the population, but we don't have access to it; we only have samples, and we estimate population parameters from the corresponding sample quantities. Statistical inference is the method by which we draw conclusions about populations from the data we've collected (our samples). In DSA we commonly use samples; it's a very important method for decision making.

outside slidesThis is the bridge to B07 (Inferential Statistics), B08 (t-tests) and B10 (regression), all of which estimate population quantities from samples.

Primary vs secondary sl. 51

There're primarily two types of data for applications. Primary data: collected directly from primary sources (people, machines, organizations, etc.). Secondary data: collected from secondary sources; such data were previously collected from primary sources. NOTE: DS and analytics use both kinds of data. Primary data may be difficult and time-consuming to collect; to collect secondary data you must know where it exists.

Question sl. 52

Your company wants to find out about various attributes that make employees happy. Where will you find this kind of data? How will you collect it? outside slidesMostly primary data: an employee survey (with consent and anonymity), plus secondary data from HR records such as attrition, tenure and leave.

Data sources (sl. 53): data can come from practically anywhere: people (opinion and market surveys); social media (FB, Twitter, Myspace, Pinterest, YouTube, Snapchat, LinkedIn etc.); emails (public services like Yahoo and Gmail, or private); company logs; call-centre logs; stock exchanges (trading data from BSE and NSE).

Data object sl. 55

Data object: a composite or collection of attributes of some variables. Example: a sales data object represents customers and sales.

Attribute sl. 55

Attribute: a certain characteristic or feature of a data object. Example: a customer has a customer id, gender, address, phone #, etc. NOTE: widely used in relational DBs, data warehouses and data marts; in R and Python these data objects are called data frames.

Lists in Python

16Lists in Python17Lists in Python18List Characteristics – 119List Characteristics – 2

Lists store multiple items in a single variable. Python has 4 built-in data types for storing data: list, tuple, set, dictionary. List items are ordered, changeable and allow duplicate values; they are indexed, the first item at [0], the second at [1], etc. Lists are created with square brackets (sl. 17).

PYsl. 17–18
myLst = ["apple", "banana", "cherry"]
print(myLst)
# ['apple', 'banana', 'cherry']

# duplicates allowed
myLst = ["apple", "banana", "cherry", "apple", "cherry"]
print(myLst)
# ['apple', 'banana', 'cherry', 'apple', 'cherry']

# how many items: len()
myLst = ["apple", "banana", "cherry"]
print(len(myLst))       # 3

# any data type
lst1 = ["apple", "banana", "cherry"]
lst2 = [1, 5, 7, 9, 3]
lst3 = [True, False, False]
PYsl. 19
# different data types in one list
myLst = ["abc", 34, True, 40, "male"]

# lists are objects of data type 'list'
myLst = ["apple", "banana", "cherry"]
print(type(myLst))      # <class 'list'>

# the list() constructor
# use double round-brackets
myLst = list(("apple", "banana", "cherry"))
print(myLst)
# ['apple', 'banana', 'cherry']

outside slidesThe "double round-brackets" are a tuple inside the call: list() takes one iterable and copies it into a new list. R's length() ↔ Python's len(); R lists are indexed from 1 with [[ ]], Python lists from 0 with [ ]. Py07-08 covers list slicing and tuples.

Fundamentals

1Define big data and name the 5 V's with one-line meanings.

Data that is expensive to manage and hard to extract value from. Velocity (processing latency vs demand for interactivity), volume (size), variety (sources, formats, quality, structures), veracity (good quality, clean), value (useful for business). (F sl. 22)

2What is the "common misconception" about big data?

That it is just a huge volume of data. (F sl. 22)

3What can you do with data? Name the three groups.

Indexing, searching and querying (pattern matching XML/RDF, keyword search); aggregation and statistics (data warehousing and OLAP); knowledge discovery (data mining, statistical modeling). (F sl. 24)

4What are RDF, OLAP and OLTP?

RDF: Resource Description Framework, describing web resources for computers, written in XML. OLAP: online analytical processing, organizing large business DBs for complex processing. OLTP: online transaction processing DBs storing all transactions and records. (F sl. 24)

5What does McKinsey (per the slide) say the U.S. will need?

190,000–230,000 predictive analysts and 2.7 million global managers/analysts by 2024; India even more. (F sl. 25)

6Which disciplines feed DS, with one contribution each?

Computer science (pattern recognition, visualization, warehousing, HPC, DBs, AI); mathematics (modeling); statistics (statistical and stochastic modeling, probability); business and economics (financial/econometric, marketing, HR process modeling, operations, IS). (F sl. 27)

7Give the deck's two ethics examples.

A store with CCTV inferring that a woman is expecting and targeting baby products; millions installing Narendra Modi's FB apps that gave access to friends' info. (F sl. 30)

8What is storytelling with data, and why does it matter here?

The fundamentals of data visualization and communicating effectively with data; a learnable skill, very important in the MPs. Never simply show data; tell a story with it (Knaflic). (F sl. 35)

9List the five steps of the top-down approach.

Identify a problem; look up literature/expert opinions; formulate hypotheses; select a model to justify them; implement and empirically test it on data. (F sl. 37)

10Describe the bottom-up approach.

Collect data on a phenomenon without foreknowledge; find patterns with DS techniques; theorize and test generality. (F sl. 39)

11Classify: ANOVA, random forest, OLS MLR, cluster analysis, LLMs, χ²-fit.

Top-down: ANOVA, OLS MLR, χ²-fit. Bottom-up: random forest, cluster analysis, LLMs. (F sl. 38, 40)

12Which listed methods are NOT in the course?

Confirmatory factor analysis, structural equation models, advanced time series, panel regression (top-down); PCA, multidimensional scaling, exploratory factor analysis (bottom-up). (F sl. 38, 40)

13Define statistics and give three reasons to study it.

Collection, compilation, analysis and interpretation of data. Understand variability; estimate the present and predict the future; convert data into information (also: solve problems, reliability and randomness). (F sl. 41–42)

14Study vs peripheral variables: give two of each from the salary example.

Study: salary (primary DV), age, tenure, performance (IVs). Peripheral: gender, religion, mom's/dad's education, political affiliation, # of FB posts. (F sl. 43–44)

15Define population and sample, and give four reasons to sample.

Population: the entire universe of data about which information is sought (not necessarily people). Sample: the group you collect data from; sample size < population size. Reasons: high cost, long time, extra resources, non-availability of respondents. (F sl. 47–49)

16What is statistical inference?

The method of drawing conclusions about populations from sample data, estimating population parameters from sample quantities. (F sl. 50)

17Primary vs secondary data, with the deck's caveats.

Primary: collected directly from people, machines, organizations; difficult and time-consuming. Secondary: previously collected by others; you must know where it exists. (F sl. 51)

18What is an informed consent form, and name two other ethical concerns.

A process of telling participants the study's key elements and what participation involves. Others: video/audio collection issues; telling subjects how data will be used; acknowledging secondary sources; AI data. (F sl. 54)

19Data object vs attribute; what are data objects called in R and Python?

Object: a collection of attributes (sales data object); attribute: a characteristic (customer id, gender, address). Data frames. (F sl. 55)

20Parameter vs statistic; proportion.

Parameter: a # representing a population characteristic; statistic: a sample characteristic estimating it. Proportion: # of successes ÷ total # in sample. (F sl. 58)
