1 · Introduction to Data and Statistics · 52 slides

Data cluster around a centre: the mean, median and mode. They spread out, measured by variance, SD, range, quartiles and the IQR. They have a shape: skewness (which tail is longer) and kurtosis (how the tails compare with a bell curve). Because population parameters are unknown, we sample and use statistics such as x̄ to infer them. The standard error σ/√n measures how sample means scatter, and the CLT says they are roughly normal once n ≥ 30.

The deck in one breath

Describe a distribution by its centre (mean, median, mode), its spread (variance, SD, range, quartiles, IQR) and its shape (skewness, kurtosis), each with an Excel function. Real populations are too big or unknowable, so you sample: a sample statistic (x̄, s) estimates a population parameter (μ, σ). The statistic changes from sample to sample, and its sampling distribution describes that variation. The SD of the sampling distribution of the mean is the standard error, σ/√n. By the CLT, sample means are approximately normal for n ≥ 30. Probability sampling gives everyone an equal chance; non-probability (convenience) sampling is cheaper but open to bias. Several factors push the sample size up.

Mean, median and modeTitle · Central Tendency · Mean · Sigma Notation · Example: Calculate the Mean · Excel Exercise · Median · How to Find the Median? · Example: Calculate the Median · Excel Exercise · Mode · Example: Calculate the Mode · Excel Exercise

Central tendency of a distribution is “the tendency of the data to cluster around some central value”. The most popular measures are the mean or average, the median and the mode. sl. 2

Mean · sl. 3–4
x̄ = μ = 1n Σi=1n xi = x1 + x2 + ⋯ + xnn
Excel: =AVERAGE() · Σ in Excel: =SUM()

The arithmetic mean, “the most popular measure”: the sum of the data points divided by how many there are. Sigma notation Σ stands for the sum of a set of numbers; “we use this notation, because it is easier to write the sum in this way”.

Median · sl. 7–8
location = n + 12
Excel: =MEDIAN()

The middlemost number in a sorted list. Sort smallest to largest. If (n+1)/2 is a whole number, that position is the median. If not, take the values at the whole-number positions just below and just above it and average them.

Mean example · sl. 5

Five test scores 90, 86, 91, 82, 73: Mean = ⅕(90 + 86 + 91 + 82 + 73) = 84.4.

Median example · sl. 9

15 quiz scores 9, 6, 7, 10, 9, 4, 9, 2, 9, 10, 7, 7, 5, 6, 7 (n = 15). Sorted: 2, 4, 5, 6, 6, 7, 7, 7, 7, 9, 9, 9, 9, 10, 10. Median at (15+1)/2 = 8, so the 8th value, 7.

Variance, SD, range, quartiles and the IQRVariance · Standard Deviation · Range · Quartiles · Quartile Example · Interquartile Range (IQR)

Variance · sl. 16
s2 = 1n − 1 Σi=1n (xi − μ)2
Excel: =VAR()

“A measure of the variability or dispersion of data around the mean μ.”

Standard deviation · sl. 17
s = √[ 1n − 1 Σ (xi − μ)2 ]
Excel: =STDEV()

“Just the positive square root of variance.”

Range · sl. 18
R = Largest − Smallest
Excel: =MAX() − MIN()

MAX returns the largest value in a set, MIN the smallest.

IQR · sl. 21
IQR = Q3 − Q1

“A measure of where the mid-50 of the data is.” It represents the mid-spread of the data.

Quartiles sl. 19–20 divide a list into 4 parts or quarters: sort it (say, increasing), divide into 4 parts, and the quartiles are the cutting points. Example: “#s are 2, 5 4, 4, 8, 7, 6”; sorted 2 4 4 5 6 7 8, with Q1 under the second 4, Q2 = the median, 5, and Q3 at 7. Excel: =QUARTILE(…, 1 or 2 or 3).

sl. 22–32

Skewness and kurtosisSkewness · Positive Skewness · Negative Skewness · Examples of Skewed Data · Kurtosis · Mesokurtic · Leptokurtic · Platykurtic · Kurtosis Comparison · Notes on Kurtosis · Let's Summarize

Skewness is “a measure of the asymmetry of your data from the symmetrical bell curve” (Excel =SKEW()); the slide draws “a perfectly symmetrical bell curve” for reference. Positive skew: the right tail is longer. Negative skew: the left tail is longer. sl. 22–24

Examples of skewed data sl. 25: household income, employee salary, country populations, real estate prices, reaction times of humans, contribution to flood victims, efficiency levels of employees in an organization.

Kurtosis “measures how the tails of your data distribution differ from the tails of the perfectly symmetrical bell curve” (Excel =KURT()). sl. 26

Mesokurtic · K = 0Same or almost the same as the bell curve · sl. 27
Leptokurtic · K > 0Tails thinner, peak higher · sl. 28
Platykurtic · K < 0Tails flatter, more spread out · sl. 29

Notes on kurtosis sl. 31: a standard normal distribution (pure bell shape, the Gaussian distribution) “has a kurtosis of 3” and is called mesokurtic. Kurtosis > 3 is a thin bell with a high peak (leptokurtic); < 3 is a flatter peak with thicker tails (platykurtic). Sl. 30 overlays the three curves.

Let's summarize sl. 32

  • Skewness and kurtosis describe departures from symmetry and relative flatness; “they use deviation scores”.
  • Skewness measures deviation from symmetry. In a symmetrical distribution the mean, mode and median are in the same location. Cases stretching toward one tail make it skewed.
  • Kurtosis measures a distribution's peakedness. Mesokurtic approaches normal: “Kurtosis for a normal distribution is 0”. Leptokurtic is +ve (scores pile up in the centre); platykurtic (flat) is −ve.
0 or 3? Both, on different scales

Sl. 27, 32 say a normal curve has kurtosis 0; sl. 31 says 3. Both are right. The raw kurtosis of a normal distribution is 3. Excess kurtosis subtracts 3, so normal = 0. Excel's KURT and pandas' .kurt() report excess kurtosis, so with software use the 0 cut-off: > 0 leptokurtic, < 0 platykurtic. Outside slides

Means · sl. 40
x̄ = 1n Σ xi  ·  x̄g = (Π xi)1/n  ·  x̄h = nΣ 1/xi

The arithmetic, geometric and harmonic means. Π means product: Πxi = x1 × x2 × … × xn. “Geometric mean and harmonic mean are only infrequently used.”

Sample variance and SD · sl. 41
s2 = 1n − 1 Σ (xi − x̄)2  ·  s = +√s2

They measure the spread of the sample values around the sample mean x̄.

“A very important result” · sl. 41
s2 = x2 − x̄2 = 1n Σ xi2 − ( 1n Σ xi )2

This shortcut (the mean of the squares minus the square of the mean) is exact only for the version that divides by n. For the n − 1 sample variance above, multiply it by n/(n − 1). Outside slides

Inference from samples sl. 43 Population parameters are “either unknown or unknowable”. So take a sample, calculate a statistic for the parameter, and draw an inference from it. The sampling distribution shows how the value of the statistic changes from one sample to another: it is the distribution of the statistic over all possible samples of size n from the same population.

Example · “Very Important – Understand this” · sl. 44

Population: 14, 5, 7, 9, 12, 6, 17, 21, 10, 8, 19, 33, 27, 13, 18, 30, 6, 14. Population mean 14.94, population SD 8.43. Suppose you can't see the population and take 4 samples of size 3:

SampleValuesMeanSD
1{12, 19, 30}20.339.07
2{6, 21, 13}13.337.50
3{27, 33, 14}24.679.71
4{7, 8, 17}10.675.51

The point: each sample mean misses 14.94 by a different amount, and those four means are draws from the sampling distribution of x̄. Checks: 8.43 is the n − 1 SD; dividing by n gives 8.20. Sample 2's SD is 7.51 to two places. The expected scatter of means of size 3 is σ/√3 ≈ 4.7–4.9. Outside slides

Sample mean as estimate sl. 45 To estimate the unknown μ, use x̄, and consider the sampling distribution of x̄: the probability distribution of all possible x̄ values from samples of size n.

Standard error · “Extremely Important Concept” · sl. 46
SE(x̄) = σ√n

The standard error of the mean is the SD of the sampling distribution of the mean. For a sample of size n it equals σ divided by √n, and it measures the dispersion or spread of sample means around the population mean.

Central Limit Theorem sl. 47 “No matter what quantity you're measuring”, the distribution of the statistic across all possible samples approximates a normal distribution, as long as each sample has 30 or more data points. Repeatedly draw samples and take the mean, and those means are normally distributed (illustrated with “# of samples” against “Employee salary”, a bell centred near 22).

SD and the normal distribution sl. 48 A normal curve (probability density against SDs from the mean, −4σ to 4σ) shades the bands: 68.3% within ±1σ, then ±2σ and ±3σ.

The usual figures are 68.3% (±1σ), 95.4% (±2σ) and 99.7% (±3σ); the slide's smaller labels are hard to read in the image. Outside slides

sl. 49–52

Probability vs non-probability samplingRandom/Probability Sampling · Nonprobability Sampling · Characteristics of Nonprobability Sampling · Larger Sample Considerations

Random / probability · sl. 49

  • Each sample has an equal chance of being selected.
  • Permits randomization of sample selection.
  • So every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected.

Non-probability · sl. 50

  • Samples chosen by subjective judgment, not randomly.
  • “Not a very stringent method”; depends on your expertise and familiarity with the study and participants.
  • Popular example: convenience sampling, choosing samples that are easy for you to access.

Characteristics of non-probability sampling sl. 51

  • The probability of selecting population elements is unknown.
  • A bigger opportunity for bias to enter the sample and distort findings.
  • You cannot estimate a range within which the population parameter is expected to fall.
  • When research doesn't require generalization to a population parameter, we cannot ensure the sample fully reflects the population.
  • The researcher may have limited objectives.
  • Less time-consuming and less expensive.

Larger sample considerations sl. 52: the sample must be larger when…

  1. the population's dispersion or variance is greater;
  2. the desired precision of the estimate is greater;
  3. the error range is narrower or smaller;
  4. the confidence level in the estimate is higher;
  5. there are more subgroups of interest (each must meet minimum sample size requirements).

Cost considerations also influence the size and type of sample and the data collection methods.

What's going on (the answer the slides leave to class)

tomato[3] (no comma) returns a one-column data frame, not a vector, and mean() of a data frame gives NA with a warning (“argument is not numeric or logical”). na.rm can't fix that (task 2), and converting the column (task 3) doesn't either, because price <- tomato[3] is still a data frame. tomato[, 3] (with the comma) returns the column as a vector. The batch in ten lines

  1. Central tendency is the mean (Σx/n), the median (the value at position (n+1)/2 once sorted) and the mode (most frequent; there can be none, or two for bimodal).
  2. Dispersion is the variance (Σ(x−x̄)²/(n−1)), the SD (√variance), the range (max − min), quartiles (Q2 = median) and IQR = Q3 − Q1, the mid-spread.
  3. Shape: positive skew has a long right tail and negative skew a long left one. Kurtosis compares tails with the bell: meso ≈ normal, lepto is peaked with thin tails, platy is flat. Normal = 3 raw, or 0 excess.
  4. A population parameter (μ, σ) is usually unknown, so sample, compute a statistic (x̄, s) and infer.
  5. The sampling distribution is how a statistic varies across all samples of size n. SE = σ/√n is the SD of the sample means.
  6. CLT: sample means are approximately normal when n ≥ 30, whatever the quantity.
  7. Probability sampling gives equal chances; non-probability (convenience) sampling is cheap but biased and has no error range. Bigger variance, precision, confidence or more subgroups all mean a bigger sample.
  8. R: sample, mean/var/sd/quantile with na.rm = TRUE, weighted.mean, summary, cor, cov. R indexes from 1.
  9. Python: pd.read_csv → DataFrame, .mean/.median/.mode/.var/.std/.quantile/.skew/.kurt, stats.trim_mean, .describe(), np.corrcoef, .corr(). Python indexes from 0.
  10. Reading in R: project first. Save Excel as CSV, then read.table(fl, header = TRUE, sep = ","), or read_excel. tomato[, 3] is a vector but tomato[3] is a data frame. save/load .rdata.

  • “No mode” is a valid answer when every value ties.

Positive skew. Examples: household income, employee salary, country populations, real estate prices, reaction times, flood-victim contributions, employee efficiency. sl. 23, 25

8Define mesokurtic, leptokurtic and platykurtic, with their kurtosis signs.

Meso ≈ the bell curve, K = 0. Lepto: thinner tails, higher peak, K > 0. Platy: flatter, more spread-out tails, K < 0. (Sl. 31 uses the raw scale, where normal = 3.) sl. 27–31

9Define population element, population, census and sample frame.

The unit measured; the complete collection we want conclusions about; a count of all elements; the listing of population elements the sample is drawn from. sl. 34

10What four benefits does sampling provide, and when is a census appropriate?

Lower cost, greater speed, greater accuracy, availability of elements. A census when feasible and necessary. sl. 35–36

11Distinguish a population parameter from a sample statistic.

Parameter: a measure of the population (μ, σ), usually unknown. Statistic: a measure calculated from sample data (x̄, s). sl. 42

12What is the standard error of the mean, and its formula?

The SD of the sampling distribution of the mean: σ/√n. It measures the spread of sample means around the population mean. sl. 46

13State the CLT as the slides do.

Whatever you measure, the distribution of the statistic across all possible samples approximates a normal distribution as long as each sample has 30 or more data points; repeated sample means are normally distributed. sl. 47

14Give three drawbacks of non-probability sampling, and two reasons to use a larger sample.

Unknown selection probability; more room for bias; can't estimate a range for the parameter. Larger sample: greater population variance, higher precision, narrower error range, higher confidence, more subgroups. sl. 51–52
