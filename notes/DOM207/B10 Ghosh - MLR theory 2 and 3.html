Residuals, R² and the trouble with many IVs

From one line through salary data to a model with k predictors, and the problems that come with them.

Jaideep Ghosh, Multiple Linear Regression (MLR) Analysis (Lect-MLR-Theory-2, 25 slides; Lect-MLR-Theory-3, 19 slides). School of Management and Entrepreneurship, Shiv Nadar University. DOM207, Monsoon 2026.

MLR-Theory-2 in one breath. Starting from the salary-on-education line, the deck defines the predicted value ŷ and the residual y − ŷ. Over-prediction means ŷ > y; under-prediction means ŷ < y. Regression is "linear" because it is linear in the β's, not the IV. Goodness of fit hinges on whether the slope is zero (H₀: β = 0, tested two- or one-sided), and r² = MSS/TSS = 1 − RSS/TSS, ideally 80% or more. MLR extends this to k IVs, yᵢ = β₀ + β₁x₁ᵢ + … + βₖxₖᵢ + εᵢ, and tests H₀: all βᵢ = 0. It is used to predict, to control for confounders and to test causal theories (sales on housing starts, marriages and income; SNU college performance on school performance and study hours). IVs are chosen by forward, backward or stepwise selection. The deck closes on pair plots and multicollinearity (beware correlations above 0.75).

MLR-Theory-3 in one breath. Multicollinearity makes β's unstable, inflates standard errors, distorts p-values and makes coefficients oscillate (IQ vs school performance). Heteroskedasticity, non-constant variance of the DV across the IVs (food spending vs income), keeps OLS unbiased but no longer BLUE, and invalidates SEs, t/F tests and CIs. The p-value recap covers rejection regions, p = P(T > t) and its variants, and "NEVER accept the null". Statistical significance (from p) is not practical significance (effect size); look for PS only after SS. R² rises with every IV, while adjusted R² = 1 − (n − 1)(1 − R²)/(n − k − 1) penalises bogus IVs and guards against overfitting.

Predicted values, residuals, and why "linear"

4Predicted and Residual Values5Overpredicting or Underpredicting6Why Called LINEAR Regression?7What is Meant by Residuals?

Figure, sl. 4: a scatter of observed points (×) with a red regression line. For one point the slide marks the observed 'y' below the line, the predicted 'y' on the line directly above it, and the vertical gap between them as the residual: y − ŷ.

Overprediction sl. 5

Your predicted value is higher than the actual value: ŷᵢ > yᵢ (the residual is negative).

Underprediction sl. 5

Your predicted value is lower than the actual value: ŷᵢ < yᵢ (the residual is positive).

Ghosh · M2 sl. 6

The regression relationship is expressed as a linear equation, y = β₀ + β₁x + ε, where β₁ is the slope and β₀ the y-intercept, called the constant term. "The equation is linear in the parameters of the model (the β's), not the IV."

Residual · M2 sl. 7Residual = Observed value (yᵢ) − Predicted value (ŷᵢ)

A residual is the difference between an actual observed value and the value predicted by your regression line for that same data point. Residuals represent any leftover error that the model failed to explain (sl. 7).

outside slides"Linear in the parameters" means y = β₀ + β₁x² or y = β₀ + β₁ log x still count as linear regression: x may be transformed, but each β only multiplies something. y = β₀ + x^β₁ is not linear.

Multiple linear regression: model, test, uses, applications

Ghosh · M2 sl. 13

"This is more fun and more challenging!" MLR is a generalization or extension of bivariate/ordinary linear regression. It explains or predicts a continuous DV by modelling its linear relationship with multiple IVs; it investigates how multiple factors influence the DV simultaneously. You can forecast values and understand the unique contribution of each IV while controlling for others.

19–22slides

How to select IVs

19How to Select IVs?20Forward Selection21Backward Selection22Stepwise Selection

The MLR model equation can be built with either of the following methods (sl. 19): Include all variables all at once; Include specific combinations; Sequentially add or remove variables, via one of three procedures (Forward, Backward, Stepwise):

Forward sl. 20

Starts with the constant and adds variables that result in the largest R² increase.

Backward sl. 21

Begins with a model containing all IVs and removes the variable that changes R² the least.

Stepwise sl. 22

Combines forward and backward. The IV that contributes most to explaining the DV is added first; later variables are included on their incremental contribution and whether they meet the entry criterion (SL); variables are removed at each step if they meet the removal criterion.

Multicollinearity

M2 sl. 25 (repeated as M3 sl. 2)
Definition
Multicollinearity exists when more than two [→ two or more] IVs are highly correlated: as one predictor increases or decreases, the other one always follows it.
Effect
Could be damaging: "your β-coefficients become biased"; the estimated coefficients may also fluctuate widely.
Threshold
Be careful of correlations exceeding 0.75. NOTE: we do allow some correlations, but not a high one.
Remedies
(1) Choose one of the variables and delete the other. (2) Create a new variable that is a composite of the others.

outside slidesStrictly, multicollinearity does not bias OLS coefficients; it makes them imprecise (large standard errors), which is M3 sl. 3's point 2. The usual numeric check is the variance inflation factor (VIF), not on the slides.

Multicollinearity: implications and an example

1Multiple Linear Regression (MLR) Analysis2Multicollinearity3Multicollinearity Implications4Multicollinearity Example

Sl. 2 restates M2 sl. 25 ("arises" for "exists"; "their effects on your MLR model could be damaging"; "They may also fluctuate widely"; "You do allow some correlations but not a very high one").

Implication (sl. 3)What happens
1. β-coefficients become unstableThe model cannot clearly separate the individual effect of each correlated IV on the DV.
2. Inflated STDERRsCorrelation blows up the standard errors of the coefficients, making estimates much less precise.
3. Incorrect p-valsImportant study variables can appear statistically nonsignificant even when the model is good.
4. OscillationsSmall changes in the data, or adding/removing a single IV, can make coefficient values oscillate or change signs.
Example · M3 sl. 4

The performance (perf) of UG students in a particular year at SNU is determined by HS perf (hsperf), hours of study per day (hrstud) and student IQ (iq): perf = β₀ + β₁hsperf + β₂hrstud + β₃iq + ε. PROBLEM: iq may have a very high correlation with hsperf.

5–7slides

Heteroskedasticity

5Heteroskedasticity6Heteroskedasticity Implications7Heteroscedasticity Example

Figure, sl. 5: two scatter plots with a regression line, both fanning out: the spread of points around the line grows as x grows. "Heteroskedasticity is a hard word to pronounce, but it's not a difficult concept to comprehend." Heteroskedasticity is the circumstance in which the variability of your DV is nonconstant across all values of the IVs that predict it. If this happens, it causes problems in MLR.

Implication (sl. 6)What happens
1. Inefficient estimatesOLS coefficients remain unbiased, but lose their minimum-variance property and are no longer the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE).
2. Biased STDERRsStandard-error calculations for the β-coefficients become invalid.
3. Invalid HTsBecause the STDERRs are incorrect, t-tests and F-tests become unreliable, giving wrong p-vals and false significance.
4. Misleading CIsCIs can become too wide or too narrow, making range estimates invalid.
Example · M3 sl. 7

In a sample of people, consider the relationship between food expenditures and income. For people with lower incomes, food expenditures are restricted by their budget. If so, the variance of the residuals will be unequal across the IV (income). The figure shows points fanning out above a rising line, with orange curves tracing the widening band.

outside slidesLow earners can only spend a little, so their spending is tightly bunched; high earners may spend a little or a lot, so their spread is wide. The opposite, constant variance, is homoskedasticity, the same "homogeneity of variance" idea as the t-test (B08a). Spellings with k and c are both used; the deck uses both.

The p-value, and how to use it

8p-val9How to Use p-val – 110How to Use p-val – 214(figure: p-value as tail area)

You already know the p-val indicates whether to reject or not reject H₀. The smaller the p-val, the stronger the evidence against the null, the evidence that you should reject H₀. In HT, the p-val is the probability of obtaining test results at least as extreme as those actually observed (computed from your sample), under the assumption that H₀ is correct (sl. 8).

How to use – 1

sl. 9
Step 1
Determine H₀.
Step 2
Select a test statistic t.
Step 3
Think about the nature of H₁. This determines the rejection region (RR): t > c; t < −c; or |t| > c.
Step 4
Use the observed value of t as the critical value c and compute the corresponding SL of the test: tobs = tcrit = c. This is your p-val.

How to use – 2

sl. 10
RR t > c
p-val = P(T > t)
RR t < −c
p-val = P(T < t)
RR |t| > c
p-val = P(|T| > |t|)
Decide
With SL α, reject H₀ at the 100α% level if p-val < α; if p-val ≥ α, fail to reject H₀ at the 100α% level, but NEVER accept the null.
Bottom line
A small p-val leads to rejection of your null. IMPORTANT: if the test is significant, only then look for the magnitude and the sign of the coefficients.

Figure, sl. 14 (printed sideways): a bell curve of the "set of possible results" against probability density, with "more likely observation" in the middle and "very un-likely observations" in both tails. An "observed data point" sits in the upper tail, and the shaded green area beyond it is labelled p-value. Caption: "A p-value (shaded green area) is the probability of an observed (or more extreme) result assuming that the null hypothesis is true."

11–13slides

Statistical vs practical significance

11Statistical vs Practical Significance12SS13PS

SS sl. 11–12

Statistical significance (SS) tells you whether a result is likely to be real and not just due to chance, usually determined by a p-val. It means the result is unlikely to have occurred purely by chance, "meaning it's perhaps an impactful result (you've already learned this in an earlier class)". Use statistical tests to calculate a p-val; a low p-val (typically < 0.05) suggests statistical significance (rejection of null). "In this case, the probability that the observed effect is a real thing and not of a chancy affair" [sentence unfinished on the slide].

PS sl. 11, 13

Practical significance (PS) assesses whether the result is meaningful or impactful in a practical situation. PS is also called effect size (ES). It implies the effect is large enough in size to be important, useful, meaningful and impactful in practice. You measure it by examining effect sizes, which quantify the magnitude of the observed difference or association; that magnitude has real-world or practical implications.

A finding can be SS but not PS if the effect is too small to matter, or vice versa; both are crucial for interpreting data (sl. 11). NOTE: you look for PS only if you find SS first (sl. 13).

Trap · M3 sl. 12

A p-value is not "the probability that the observed effect is a real thing". It is the probability of data this extreme if H₀ were true (sl. 8, sl. 14, and B07: "not the probability of the hypothesis, given the outcome"). Quote sl. 8's definition in an exam.

15–18slides

R² and adjusted R²

15R² and Adjusted R²16Finer Points: R² and Adjusted R²17Computing R²adj18Uses of R²adj

R² sl. 15–16

Measures the proportion of the variation in your DV explained by all your IVs in the model. It assumes every IV helps explain variation in the DV. In reality some IVs (predictors) don't help explain the DV (target): they're called bogus variables, serving no purpose in your model. R² shows how your data points fit the regression line.

Adjusted R² (R²adj) sl. 15–16

Measures the proportion of variation explained by only those IVs that really help in explaining the DV. It penalizes you for adding bogus IVs that do not help predict the DV. It also indicates how well the data fit the line, but adjusts for the # of IVs in your model.

Remember

sl. 16
Bogus IVs
If you include more bogus variables in [sic: "inb"] your model, R²adj will actually decrease.
Useful IVs
If you add more useful variables, adjusted R² will increase.
Always
R²adj ≤ R²
Adjusted R² · M3 sl. 17R²adj = 1 − (n − 1)(1 − R²)n − k − 1,  n = # of sample observations (sample size), k = # of IVs in your regression model

In words (sl. 17): you can compute adjusted R² by dividing the residual mean square error by the total mean square error (which is the sample variance of your DV); the result is then subtracted from 1.

Uses of R²adj

sl. 18
When
Use R²adj instead of the regular R² when you want to avoid overfitting issues in your MLR.
Why
It provides a more reliable measure of model fit by adjusting for the # of IVs; it penalizes irrelevant IVs and gives a more accurate representation of the model's true performance.
Especially
When comparing models with different # of IVs. [sic: "It's especially when" → "especially useful when"]
19slide

Overfitting

19Overfitting

Overfitting in MLR happens when your model follows your data too closely, capturing random noise instead of the true relationship. Reasons:

  • You included too many IVs for the amount of data in your sample.
  • Your dataset is too small to support your model.
  • Your data contain random errors and/or outliers that the model thinks are real and tries to incorporate.

outside slidesExtreme case: with n observations and k = n − 1 IVs, the model passes through every point, R² = 1, and the adjusted R² formula divides by zero. The lab shows R²adj falling as bogus IVs are added.

The batch in twelve lines

  1. Residual = observed yᵢ − predicted ŷᵢ: the leftover error the model failed to explain (M2 sl. 7).
  2. Over-prediction ŷ > y; under-prediction ŷ < y (M2 sl. 5).
  3. "Linear" = linear in the β's, not the IV (M2 sl. 6).
  4. Key GOF test: is the slope zero? H₀: β = 0; H₁ two- or one-sided (M2 sl. 10–11).
  5. TSS = MSS + RSS; r² = MSS/TSS = 1 − RSS/TSS; desired ≥ 80% (M2 sl. 12).
  6. MLR: yᵢ = β₀ + β₁x₁ᵢ + … + βₖxₖᵢ + εᵢ; n = workable sample after cleaning (M2 sl. 14).
  7. MLR's H₀: all βᵢ = 0 (no model, best guess ȳ); H₁: some βᵢ ≠ 0 (M2 sl. 15).
  8. Uses: predict; control confounders; test causal theories (M2 sl. 16).
  9. Selection: forward (largest R² gain), backward (drop smallest R² change), stepwise (both, with SL criteria) (M2 sl. 20–22).
  10. Multicollinearity: correlated IVs (beware r > 0.75): unstable β's, inflated SEs, wrong p's, sign flips; drop one or combine (M2 sl. 25, M3 sl. 2–4).
  11. Heteroskedasticity: non-constant DV variance; OLS unbiased but not BLUE; SEs, tests and CIs invalid (M3 sl. 5–7).
  12. SS before PS (effect size); R²adj ≤ R² penalises bogus IVs and guards against overfitting (M3 sl. 11–19).

Traps

  • Residual sign: over-prediction gives a negative residual.
  • "Linear" refers to the β's, not to x.
  • MLR's null is "all β's are zero" (joint), not "one β is zero".
  • M2 sl. 25 / M3 sl. 2 say "more than two IVs": two highly correlated IVs are enough.
  • Multicollinearity inflates SEs; heteroskedasticity leaves β's unbiased but SEs wrong. Don't swap them.
  • p-value ≠ probability the effect is real (M3 sl. 12 wording).
  • Fail to reject ≠ accept the null (M3 sl. 10).
  • R² never falls when you add an IV; R²adj can.
  • The predicted form has no ε (M2 sl. 18).

MLR-Theory-2

1Define a residual and give its formula.

The difference between an observed value and the value the regression line predicts for that point: yᵢ − ŷᵢ. It is the leftover error the model failed to explain. (M2 sl. 7)

2What are over- and under-prediction?

Over: ŷᵢ > yᵢ. Under: ŷᵢ < yᵢ. (M2 sl. 5)

3Why is it called linear regression?

The relationship is a linear equation y = β₀ + β₁x + ε, linear in the parameters (the β's), not the IV. (M2 sl. 6)

4In the bivariate summary, what is ε?

Anything other than x that may influence y: the error or disturbance term, an unobserved factor not accounted for by the model. (M2 sl. 8)

5Give the slopes of lines a, b, c and d on M2 sl. 9.

a −1, b 0, c +1, d ∞ (vertical).

6What is goodness of fit, and what is the most important test in bivariate regression?

How well the model predicts the DV / fits the data. Whether the slope β equals zero (H₀). (M2 sl. 10)

7Name the three situations on M2 sl. 10 where a straight line does not help.

Y unrelated to X with no pattern (best guess Ȳ); constant Y for every X (best guess Y₀); related but by a nonlinear function.

8Write H₀ and the three possible H₁'s for a slope.

H₀: β = 0. H₁: β ≠ 0 (2-sided), β > 0 (1-sided), β < 0 (1-sided). (M2 sl. 11)

9Define TSS, MSS, RSS and r², and state the desired r².

TSS = Σ(yᵢ − ȳ)², MSS = Σ(ŷᵢ − ȳ)², RSS = Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²; TSS = MSS + RSS; r² = MSS/TSS = 1 − RSS/TSS; desired 80% or more. (M2 sl. 12)

10Write the MLR equation and define k and n.

yᵢ = β₀ + β₁x₁ᵢ + … + βₖxₖᵢ + εᵢ. k = # of IVs including controls; n = # of observations after data cleaning (workable sample size). (M2 sl. 14)

11What is H₀ in MLR and what does it mean if true?

βᵢ = 0 for all i: no model at all; your best prediction is the mean of the DV. H₁: βᵢ ≠ 0 for some i. (M2 sl. 15)

12List the three uses of MLR.

Develop an estimating equation to predict DV values; control for confounding variables; test and explain causal theories. (M2 sl. 16)

13Why does the predicted form of the colperf model have no error term?

Because prediction/estimation has already been done. (M2 sl. 18)

14Contrast forward, backward and stepwise selection.

Forward: start with the constant, add the variable giving the largest R² increase. Backward: start with all IVs, remove the one that changes R² least. Stepwise: combines both, adding by contribution and SL entry criterion and removing by a removal criterion. (M2 sl. 20–22)

15How do you draw a pair plot in R and in Python?

R: built-in pairs(). Python: Seaborn sns.pairplot(), with histograms on the diagonal. (M2 sl. 23)

MLR-Theory-3

16Define multicollinearity, its threshold and two remedies.

IVs highly correlated (one follows the other). Beware correlations over 0.75. Remedies: delete one of the variables; create a composite variable. (M2 sl. 25; M3 sl. 2)

17List the four implications of multicollinearity.

Unstable β's; inflated STDERRs; incorrect p-vals (important IVs look nonsignificant); oscillations/sign changes. (M3 sl. 3)

18What is the multicollinearity problem in the SNU perf model?

iq may be very highly correlated with hsperf. (M3 sl. 4)

19Define heteroskedasticity and give the deck's example.

The variability of the DV is nonconstant across the values of the IVs. Food expenditure vs income: low incomes restrict spending, so residual variance is unequal across income. (M3 sl. 5, 7)

20Are OLS coefficients biased under heteroskedasticity? What does go wrong?

No, they stay unbiased but are no longer BLUE (inefficient). STDERRs become invalid, so t/F tests, p-vals and CIs are unreliable. (M3 sl. 6)

21How do you get the p-value for each rejection region?

RR t > c: P(T > t). RR t < −c: P(T < t). RR |t| > c: P(|T| > |t|). Reject H₀ at 100α% if p < α; otherwise fail to reject, never accept. (M3 sl. 9–10)

22When should you look at the magnitude and sign of a coefficient?

Only if the test is significant. (M3 sl. 10)

23Distinguish SS and PS. What is PS also called, and which comes first?

SS: unlikely due to chance, judged by p. PS: large enough to matter in practice, judged by effect size (ES). Look for PS only after SS. (M3 sl. 11–13)

24What are bogus variables, and how do R² and R²adj react to them?

IVs that don't help explain the DV. R² still rises (it assumes every IV helps); R²adj penalises them and can fall. R²adj ≤ R². (M3 sl. 15–16)

25n = 30, k = 3, R² = 0.70. Compute R²adj.

1 − (29 × 0.30)/26 = 1 − 0.3346 = 0.665. (M3 sl. 17 formula)

26What is overfitting and what causes it?

The model follows the data too closely, capturing noise. Causes: too many IVs for the data; too small a dataset; random errors/outliers the model treats as real. (M3 sl. 19)
