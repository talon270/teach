Who gets recognised?

Tokyo, 1946 to 1948. Eleven judges, all from victor nations, try Japan's leaders. Ten judge them guilty. One judge, Radhabinod Pal, a potter's son from Bengal, finds every accused not guilty. Nandy asks why. Becker Lorca asks the wider question: when did non-Western lawyers first get a seat at the table, and what did they do with it?

Your win todayGive Pal's three legal points, Nandy's reading of Pal, Lorca's "mestizo" idea and the standard of civilization, each with one fact.

1 · Warm-up from lesson 11

What is India's "double-move" when its record on race is questioned?

It invokes its antiracist history and denies that the instrument applies (caste is not "descent" under ICERD; Girijashanker).

2 · Nandy: the strange case of Justice Pal

Ashis Nandy, "The Other Within: The Strange Case of Radhabinod Pal's Judgment of Culpability", in The Savage Freud (1995), pp. 53–80. Pal (1886–1967) was a retired Calcutta High Court judge and former Vice Chancellor of Calcutta University (pp. 54–55). Born to a poor family of low-caste potters, the Kumbhakaras (pp. 78–79).

  • The verdict. Pal held that "each and every one of the accused must be found not guilty of each and every one of the charges" (pp. 56–57). His warning: justice must not be invoked "only for the prolongation of the pursuit of vindictive retaliation" (pp. 56–57). The Tribunal sat from 3 May 1946 to 4 November 1948, with eleven judges; 25 defendants, 7 sentenced to death (pp. 70–73). Only the majority judgment was read in open court (pp. 60–61).
  • Point 1: no new crimes. Prisoners "can be tried only for breach of recognized laws of war"; victors cannot create new crimes and punish prisoners under them (pp. 60–61). "Conspiracy to commit aggression" did not exist in international law; the Tribunal "created" it ex post facto (pp. 66–67).
  • Point 2: aggression was not yet a crime. Until 1944 France, Britain and the US had agreed that aggression was not a crime. The Pact of Paris (28 August 1928) left each state "the prerogative of judging for itself" what self-defence covered (pp. 66–67). So the Japanese could be tried only for conventional war crimes (pp. 66–67).
  • Point 3: the victors' own acts were left out. Pal wanted the fire-bombing of Japanese cities (over 100,000 civilians dead in the first Tokyo attack) and the atom bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki within the Tribunal's reach (pp. 68–69). He set them beside the Kaiser's letter ordering "fire and sword" in 1914. Nandy notes that even Pal's critics concede that Hiroshima was the clearest state-organised atrocity on civilians in the eastern war (pp. 70–71).
  • Why the Tribunal was weak. A charter drawn up by the American chief prosecutor, Joseph Keenan (pp. 66–67). Justice Jackson admitted it was a military tribunal "to avoid precedent-creating effect" on US law (pp. 74–75). Minear: all eleven judges were citizens of victor nations, and only one, Pal, had a background in international law (pp. 74–75). The Soviet judge spoke neither official language; the first American judge resigned (pp. 72–73).
  • Two readings of Pal, and Nandy's third. In Japan, nationalists used the dissent as vindication; Tojo wrote a haiku in his honour, and the Pal-Shimonaka Memorial Hall stands at Hakone (pp. 58–59). Outside India and Japan it was dismissed as "woolly Gandhian sentiments" (Appleman, pp. 58–59). Nandy says the judgment had private politics too (anti-imperial Bengal, the Indian National Army, Tsushima 1905; pp. 76–79), but "the break between the morality of politics and the politics of morality can never be total" (pp. 78–79).
  • The "other within". Pal read the dissent as an invitation to the judges "to discover the accused in them". Nandy borrows Money-Kyrle: the "enemy" is "an inner representation become flesh" (pp. 64–65). The Tokyo trial let the victors point outward and "avoid the painful look inward" (pp. 64–65).

Pal said the Tribunal "created" conspiracy to commit aggression how, in Latin?

Ex post facto: after the act (pp. 66–67).
Quiz trap. Pal did not say the Japanese acts were good. He said the law and the Tribunal could not convict. His point on aggression, the Pact of Paris and self-defence, is a law point (pp. 66–67). Nandy, not Pal, supplies the psychoanalytic "other within".

3 · Becker Lorca: mestizo international law

Arnulf Becker Lorca, Mestizo International Law (2014), Introduction and Part I, pp. 1–38. Start with a meeting.

  • Frankfurt, 1878. At the sixth meeting of the Association for the Reform and Codification of the Law of Nations (founded 1873; later the International Law Association) a Chinese delegate, Guo Songtao, and a Japanese delegate, Ueno Kagenori, speak for the first time. Ueno: its aims "are cosmopolitan" (p. 2). Guo hoped IL would improve China's relations (p. 3).
  • Field's answer. The American David Dudley Field said the capitulations (consular courts) must stay "so long as there was not something like a parity of civilisation" (p. 3). Ueno did not reject the test. He said crucifixion "was now happily abolished in Japan" and that Japan was "quickly mastering the enlightened notions of the West" (p. 4). He contested Japan's place, not the standard of civilization.
  • Why it matters. Lorca: an "exchange of opposing views articulated in a common language of international law" (p. 4). Valladolid (1550) had also argued about the New World, but "the convener, the audience and the opponents were all Europeans" (p. 5). Only in the nineteenth century did IL become global and non-Western elites become international lawyers (p. 5).
  • The span. It runs from the Treaty of Nanjing (1842) to the Montevideo Convention (1933). Montevideo replaced the standard of civilization with "a formal definition of statehood and doctrine of recognition" (p. 8).
  • Three claims (p. 15): (1) IL went global because non-Western jurists appropriated and transformed it, not by simple admission or imposition; (2) the move from classical to modern IL (around 1907) was not simple progress; (3) the standard of civilization was dissolved in the interwar period through semi-peripheral engagement, which later allowed self-determination.
  • Semi-periphery. A world-systems idea (pp. 18–20). Core states set the terms; the periphery supplies primary goods; the semi-periphery has "some margin of autonomy" but not core power (China, Japan, Latin American states). They were not colonised, but great powers still intervened: the 1900 Boxer intervention and the 1902 Venezuelan blockade (p. 20). They turned to IL to narrow that power (p. 20). Meiji Japan is the "paradigmatic example" (p. 21).
  • "Mestizo". Hybrid origins "without ignoring the privileged position the Western legal tradition has occupied" (p. 22). The non-Western jurist is like the mestizo, caught between rejection and belonging: V. K. Wellington Koo, "Westerners think of Koo as a Chinese, and Chinese think of him as a Westerner" (p. 23).
  • Centre against periphery. The French jurist Lapradelle argued for a right of Western intervention in China, since Western civilisation is "movement and progress" and Oriental civilisation "too contemplative" (p. 25). Koo's book on aliens in China argued from the Chinese point of view (pp. 25–26). Same law, different pictures of the world.
  • The core's defences. Brownlie called Alvarez's regional Latin American law "insubstantial" (p. 27). Verzijl, Verdross, Schmitt and Grewe all insisted on a Western origin (pp. 31–32). Alexandrowicz argued that East Indian and European sovereigns dealt on equal terms in the 17th and 18th centuries; Grewe read the same treaties as ones "of convenience" (pp. 33–34). Oppenheim in 1905 called IL "a product of modern Christian civilisation" and kept out Persia, Siam, China, Korea and Abyssinia (pp. 34–35).
  • Two sensibilities (pp. 36–37). Classical (1842 to 1907): sovereign autonomy, the standard of civilization and positivism; semi-peripherals stressed universality. Modern (after the war): the interests of the international community, with a "professional style of resistance".

Which 1933 convention ends Lorca's story by replacing the standard of civilization with a formal definition of statehood?

The Montevideo Convention, 1933 (p. 8).
Link to lesson 10. Lorca begins in 1842 and names no Vitoria or Grotius (p. 10). It is a "new records" history in d'Aspremont's sense: Guo, Ueno, Koo and Alvarez are new records, not a new spin of the old ones.

4 · Class handouts: two real cases (class handout, week not stated)

The ICC under sanctions. ASIL President Oona Hathaway, statement of 13 October 2026. On 9 October the US placed "the entire Court" on its Treasury sanctions list (Specially Designated Nationals), promising to "dismantle the ICC, brick by brick". She notes that the US is not a party to the Rome Statute and that the Court can reach US persons only for crimes on the territory of states parties, and only if the US does not act itself. Use it against Nandy: Pal asked whether a tribunal run by the powerful is law or retaliation; now a great power attacks a court that states built.
Navi Pillay. The same statement says she won the Nobel Peace Prize on the same day. The Nelson Mandela University citation (2010) gives the career: born in Durban in 1941; the first woman to open her own law practice in Natal; her passport was denied for years; the first woman of colour on the Bench (1995); judge at the Rwanda tribunal, where in Akayesu rape was held to be an act of genocide; an ICC judge from 2003; UN High Commissioner for Human Rights from 2008. Use her next to Koo in Lorca: a jurist from outside the core, working inside the institutions.
