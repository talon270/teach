Who makes the law?

Lesson 1 gave the textbook answer: rules come from the sources in Art 38(1). Hilary Charlesworth asks the next question: who decided that these are the sources? Her answer makes "sources" a matter of power, not just technique.

Your win todayContrast Shaw and Charlesworth (2012) on sources in three sentences, using her key phrases.

1 · Warm-up from lesson 1

List the four sources in Art 38(1), and the two elements of custom.

Treaties, custom, general principles, judicial decisions and teachings (subsidiary). Custom = state practice + opinio juris.

2 · Charlesworth's argument

  • Talk of "sources" comforts lawyers that they practise a technical craft. But doctrine is "selective and partial".
  • Art 38(1) is a "pithy mantra", "dated and increasingly misleading". It postpones the politics of who designates sources.
  • Sources are "inflected by the identities of those who have the power to designate them". This is the shared point of Third World and feminist critiques.
  • IL mixes consent and values. She uses Koskenniemi's apology and utopia: law swings between excusing state power (apology) and moralising above it (utopia). Example: Iraq 2003.
  • Every source leaks; soft law blurs them. Legitimacy is shifting from formal sources to values, and from state consent to wider participation.

What two-word phrase does Charlesworth use for Art 38(1)?

"Pithy mantra": "dated and increasingly misleading".
