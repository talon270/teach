New frontiers

A German court once ordered travel agencies to pay damages to tourists who were "disturbed and disgusted" by disabled guests in their hotel. A US horse ban sent the horses to Mexico to be killed under weaker rules. These two cases open the week's question: how does a new branch of international law form, and who counts as its subject? Degener answers for disability; Peters for animals.

Your win todayName Degener's three equality concepts and six treaty arguments, and Peters's six grounds for global regulation, each with one fact.

1 · Warm-up from lesson 13

What did the 1933 Montevideo Convention put in place of the standard of civilization?

A formal definition of statehood and the doctrine of recognition (Lorca, p. 8).

2 · Degener: international disability law

Theresia Degener, "International Disability Law: A New Legal Subject on the Rise", Berkeley Journal of International Law 18 (2000), pp. 180–195. It reports the Hong Kong experts' meeting of 13–17 December 1999. (The outline's "Vol. 12" is a misprint; the volume is 18.)

  • The core claim. Disabled persons "have been depicted not as subjects of legal rights but as objects of welfare, health and charity programs" (p. 180). Exclusion "do[es] not logically follow from impairments, but rather from political choices based on false assumptions" (pp. 180–181): building steps but not ramps. The shift is medical model to social model, and welfare law to civil rights law (p. 181).
  • Three equality concepts (p. 182): (1) formal or juridical equality (ignore difference; Minow's dilemma: ignoring difference fails "the reality of difference", noticing it risks "false assumptions"); (2) equality of results (equal allocation of resources; needs "a strong welfare state"; could make segregated schools legitimate, p. 183); (3) equal opportunity or structural equality, built on "reasonable accommodation" (developed in the US in the 1970s; the UK says "adjustments"). It is the most used in modern laws and the most compatible with the market (p. 183).
  • The wave. "The 1990s in particular was a banner decade for disability law; more than twenty nations enacted disability discrimination laws" (p. 184). The ADA (1990) was widely copied. National disability movements were the driving force (p. 184).
  • UN history. About 600 million disabled people, two in three in developing countries (p. 187). The UDHR, ICCPR and ICESCR name no disability in their equality clauses (p. 187). Then: 1971 and 1975 declarations (still medical model); the 1982 World Programme of Action, whose third goal was equalization of opportunities (p. 188); a binding treaty proposal that lost in the General Assembly in 1987; and the non-binding Standard Rules (1993) as "a compensatory alternative" (pp. 188–189).
  • Existing treaties read for disability. ICESCR General Comment No. 5 (1994) is "the only legal U.N. document" that broadly defines disability-based discrimination, including "denial of reasonable accommodations" (p. 190). The 1999 Inter-American Convention is the first regional treaty to define it, but it lets a state declare a person legally incompetent without that counting as discrimination (pp. 191–192).
  • A new treaty? The obstacle: "treaty fatigue" (p. 193). Six arguments for one (p. 194): binding law (current standards are a "toothless tiger"); attention and resources; new content such as the "right to be different"; domestic use; a catalyst for the movement; human rights, not welfare. Three principles: open drafting, disabled persons as principal participants, no dilution of existing standards (p. 194).
  • Judges. Judges "tend to adhere to the medical model". The example: the Flensburg court, 1993 (p. 195).

Which term is the key idea of the equal-opportunity concept, developed in the US in the 1970s?

Reasonable accommodation (p. 183).
Quiz trap. Equality of results = strong welfare state, "who is responsible?". Equal opportunity = the market and reasonable accommodation. The treaty that followed (the CRPD, 2006) is not in Degener; she ends at the recommendation to start the process.

3 · Peters: global animal law

Anne Peters, "Global Animal Law: What It Is and Why We Need It", Transnational Environmental Law 5:1 (2016), pp. 9–23. A foreword to a symposium.

  • Claim. Animal law must be global or transnational (transboundary and multilevel) to work (p. 9). Animal welfare began as a scientific idea: the "five freedoms" and three dimensions (health and functioning, affective state, natural living; p. 10). It aims to reduce suffering while keeping human use. It is not animal rights.
  • The gap. Whether welfare is already a general principle of law (Art. 38 ICJ Statute) is "as yet unsettled" (p. 12). The 180 member states of the OIE show concern is global (p. 12). Yet no "coherent and 'thick' body of law" exists (p. 15): a few clauses (CITES transport, the 1991 Antarctic Protocol), and mostly non-binding secondary law (OIE standards, the Bern Committee, the IWC). Europe has more, only regionally.
  • Six grounds for global regulation (pp. 16–19): (1) consumers, who need information and labels; (2) evasion and the "race to the bottom": relocation ("leakage") to low-standard states; example: after the last US horse slaughter plants closed in 2007, exports to Mexico rose by 660% (p. 18); the Basel Declaration (2010) on biomedical research (p. 18); (3) a level playing field for businesses in high-standard states (p. 18); (4) animal welfare is embedded in global problems (p. 19); (5) a benchmark for activists (p. 19); (6) an interpretative guideline for trade law through VCLT Art. 31(3)(c) (p. 19).
  • Global animal law. "Global law" is a regulatory mix of levels, makers (state and private) and force (hard and soft). The corpus is thin but "has reached a critical mass" to count as a field (p. 20).
  • Three proposals (pp. 21–22): strengthen the OIE (Peters doubts it: its mandate is animal disease and human health, and welfare is a "side show", p. 21); an NGO-led Universal Declaration on Animal Welfare, modelled on the 1948 UDHR (non-binding); a binding umbrella treaty with protocols (Favre).
  • Method and the power lens. Research must be comparative and interdisciplinary, and "sensitive to problems of Eurocentrism, of legal imperialism, and of a North-South divide"; it should "not naively export European values" but seek an overlapping consensus (p. 22).
  • The ending. Global justice: Horta says birth in Sweden or Somalia is equally irrelevant to animals' basic rights if sentience is the relevant feature (p. 23). This needs "the leap from animal welfare to animal rights", which faces "doctrinal, political, and moral objections". Global animal law is part of the "animal turn" (p. 23).

What name does Peters give to the downward spiral of standards when industry relocates to weaker regulators?

A "race to the bottom" (p. 18).
The link. Both readings move a group from objects of welfare to subjects of rights (Degener: disabled persons; Peters's last step: animals). Both ask whether the field needs a binding treaty or can live on soft law. Use this pairing in an essay on "how new fields of IL form". The pairing is mine, not the authors'.
