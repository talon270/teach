What international law is

Inside a country, a parliament makes laws, police enforce them and courts must hear cases. Between countries there is no legislature, no executive and no compulsory court. So where do the rules come from? International lawyers answer: from sources. This lesson is the baseline every later reading argues with.

Your win todayName the four sources in Art 38(1) of the ICJ Statute, and explain the two parts of custom.

1 · The players

  • States are the main subjects of international law (IL): they make it and are bound by it.
  • The UN Charter (1945) is the treaty that founded the United Nations. Remember two articles now: Art 2(4) bans the threat or use of force; Art 2(7) protects matters of domestic jurisdiction.
  • The International Court of Justice (ICJ) is the UN's court. It hears a case only if the states agree. Its rulebook is the ICJ Statute.

2 · The list of sources: Art 38(1)

Art 38(1)SourcePlain words
(a)Treaties (conventions)Written agreements between states. Bind the parties that consent.
(b)Custom"Evidence of a general practice accepted as law"
(c)General principlesPrinciples "recognised by civilised nations" (note the colonial wording)
(d)Judicial decisions and teachingsSubsidiary means to find the law, "subject to Art 59"

Art 59: an ICJ decision binds only the parties, and only in that case. So there is no strict precedent.

Which article of the ICJ Statute says a decision binds only the parties to that case?

Art 59. That is why judicial decisions in Art 38(1)(d) are only "subsidiary means".

3 · Custom: two elements

  • State practice: what states actually do, widely and consistently.
  • Opinio juris: states do it because they believe the law requires it, not from habit or courtesy.

Shaw adds that custom is shaped by power: powerful states' practice counts for more. His running point: every source both makes and evidences law.

What is the Latin name for the belief that a practice is legally required?

Opinio juris. Practice without it is mere habit (usage).

4 · Beyond the list

  • Jus cogens: peremptory norms no treaty can override (for example, the ban on genocide).
  • Erga omnes: obligations owed to the whole international community.
  • Soft law: non-binding texts (General Assembly resolutions, declarations) that still shape behaviour.
  • Also: equity, the International Law Commission (ILC), unilateral acts.
